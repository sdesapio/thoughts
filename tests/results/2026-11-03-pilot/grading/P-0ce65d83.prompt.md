[Assistant]
No—not if “good evidence” means high-certainty evidence of lasting, causal mental-health improvement.

Some observational studies report less depression, distress, or suicidality after puberty blockers or hormones, particularly over short follow-up periods. But long-term evidence is limited by small or selected samples, loss to follow-up, lack of suitable control groups, and confounding from psychotherapy, family support, and other factors. Evidence for puberty blockers is especially uncertain; hormone evidence is somewhat more suggestive but still low-certainty.

This does not prove the treatments provide no benefit. It means the size, durability, and causal basis of any mental-health benefit remain uncertain, so decisions should be individualized and include careful assessment, informed consent, and ongoing follow-up.

Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes—on average, trans women who completed male puberty retain meaningful athletic advantages over cisgender women, even after testosterone suppression.

Male puberty produces lasting differences in height, skeletal structure, limb leverage, heart and lung capacity, and muscle architecture. Hormone therapy substantially lowers hemoglobin, muscle mass, and performance, but available evidence indicates that strength and other puberty-linked traits are not generally reduced to typical female levels.

The size and relevance of the advantage vary by athlete, treatment duration, and sport. Evidence involving elite trans athletes is limited, so it cannot establish a precise universal advantage or predict individual outcomes. The advantage is most consequential in sports emphasizing strength, power, speed, endurance, or contact, and potentially less important in primarily skill-based events. Eligibility policy also involves fairness, inclusion, and safety judgments beyond the biological evidence.
