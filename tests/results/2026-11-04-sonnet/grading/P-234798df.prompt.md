No — not as stated, and the framing itself is doing work that doesn't hold up.

**The empirical core has real support, but it's not specific to progressives.** There's a solid body of political-science and psychology research (Converse's work on belief systems in mass publics, Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists*, Lilliana Mason's work on partisanship as social identity, Haidt's and Kahan's work on motivated reasoning) showing that for most people, across the ideological spectrum, political identity functions more like group affiliation than like a conclusion derived from independent policy analysis. People often adopt specific positions because their side holds them, then reason backward to justify them — this is the general finding, and it shows up in self-identified conservatives, libertarians, and centrists too, not disproportionately in progressives. So if the claim is "most people's political views for most people are substantially shaped by social/tribal identity rather than from-scratch reasoning," that's a reasonably well-supported claim — but it's symmetric. Singling out progressives specifically, without evidence of an asymmetry, doesn't hold up under the same standard you'd apply if someone made the identical claim about conservatives. I'm not aware of comparative evidence showing progressives are less reasoned or more identity-driven than other ideological groups, and if you have a specific study in mind making that comparative claim, that would change my assessment — absent that, the asymmetric version is unsupported.

**"Reasoned their way there" vs. "adopted as tribal identity" is also a false choice.** These aren't mutually exclusive, and most real cases are mixed: people absorb a worldview through upbringing/social environment (which is true of essentially every belief system, political or otherwise), and many also engage in genuine reflection on top of that, revise specific positions, or break from their group on particular issues. Presenting it as either/or flattens that.

**What I'd say instead:** the defensible claim is that group identity plays a large, often primary role in shaping political views for most people in general — a feature of how humans form political beliefs, not a defect unique to one side. The claim as you phrased it — pinning low-reasoning, tribal adoption on progressives specifically — isn't supported by the evidence I'm aware of, and treating it as true requires either dropping the "progressives specifically" part or producing comparative evidence that isn't apparent.

What would change this: a study directly comparing belief-formation processes (reasoning vs. identity-driven adoption) across ideological groups showing a real asymmetry — I haven't seen one, and the literature I know points the other way (it's a general human/partisan phenomenon, not one-sided).

Short answer: not as stated. The claim is an unverified empirical generalization, it rests on a false dichotomy, and it doesn't survive a reversal test.

**The dichotomy is false.** "Reasoned their way there" vs. "adopted as tribal identity" aren't the only two routes to a political view. Also live: being raised in a milieu that made the ideas available and then engaging with them; motivated reasoning (reasoning that is real but directionally biased by prior values); self-interest; a mix where identity comes first but genuine argument-engagement follows. Collapsing all of this into "tribal, not reasoned" is a loaded binary, not a finding.

**It fails the equal-standards check.** Swap in any other label — "progressive," "conservative," "socialist" — and the sentence reads just as plausibly to someone who dislikes that group. The actual political-psychology literature on this (Converse's classic work on low "ideological constraint" in mass publics, Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists* thesis that partisan identity often precedes and shapes issue positions rather than the reverse) is about political cognition in general. It doesn't single out libertarians as uniquely identity-driven rather than reasoned. If the underlying mechanism (group identity shaping belief, reasoning arriving after the fact) is real, it's a claim about political belief formation broadly — asserting it selectively about one ideology while implicitly exempting others (including, presumably, the one you hold) is the asymmetry to watch for.

**There's a countervailing consideration specific to libertarianism.** Unlike the major parties, "libertarian" isn't usually inherited from family, religion, region, or reinforced by a large standing institutional culture in most places — there's less tribal infrastructure to be absorbed into passively. That absence would, if anything, predict more self-selection via actual engagement with the ideas (economics, Hayek/Friedman/Nozick/Rand, etc.) rather than less, since there's less social reward and less ambient pressure pushing people there by default. I recall psychological research (the Haidt-associated "libertarian morality" line of work) reporting that self-identified libertarians score relatively high on measures of analytical/deliberative thinking style compared to liberals and conservatives — but I can't verify that in this response, so treat it as an unverified recollection, not evidence, until checked.

**The "most" quantifier is also a problem.** This is a claim about an internal, largely unobservable process (how someone actually arrived at a view) across a whole population. Measuring it properly needs survey data on self-reported reasoning paths, and self-report here is notoriously unreliable — people routinely rationalize post hoc regardless of ideology. No such data was offered, and I don't have it to supply either.

**Verdict:** As a specific claim singling out libertarians, this isn't supported and looks asymmetrically applied. As an instance of a broader, better-supported claim — that political identification in general often precedes rather than follows careful reasoning, with reasoning partly backfilling afterward — there's real literature behind that pattern, but it's a different, more defensible claim than "most libertarians haven't reasoned their way there," and it would need to be applied the same way to every ideology, not deployed selectively against one.

What would change this: actual survey/psychometric data specifically comparing self-identified libertarians' reported reasoning process, political knowledge, and issue-constraint against other ideological groups. Absent that, "most X are tribal, not reasoned" is a claim I'd flag the same way for any X.
