[Assistant]
No—the consensus among economists across the political spectrum is that broad tax cuts **almost never** completely pay for themselves through economic growth. 

While tax cuts can stimulate economic activity and generate *some* new tax revenue, that growth typically only offsets a fraction of the initial revenue lost.

Here is how the economics break down:

### 1. The Laffer Curve (Where the idea comes from)
The idea that tax cuts pay for themselves comes from the **Laffer Curve**, which illustrates the relationship between tax rates and tax revenue:
* At a **0% tax rate**, the government collects $0.
* At a **100% tax rate**, no one has an incentive to work or invest legally, so the government also collects $0.
* Between 0% and 100%, there is a revenue-maximizing peak. 

If current tax rates are **above** that peak (for example, 80%–90%), cutting taxes *can* increase total revenue by encouraging people to work more, invest more, and shelter less income. However, almost all empirical research places modern tax rates in the U.S. and most developed economies on the **left side** of the peak—meaning lowering rates reduces total revenue.

### 2. Static vs. Dynamic Scoring
When agencies like the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) or Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT) evaluate a tax cut, they look at two effects:
* **Static Cost:** How much revenue is lost if behavior doesn't change at all (e.g., cutting taxes by $1 trillion costs $1 trillion).
* **Dynamic Feedback:** How much of that loss is recovered because lower taxes incentivize more work, investment, and GDP growth.

Across major modern U.S. tax cuts (1981, 2001, 2003, and 2017), dynamic scoring estimates from the CBO, JCT, Tax Policy Center, and Tax Foundation consistently find that **economic growth recoups roughly 10% to 30% of the static cost** over a 10-year window—leaving 70% to 90% of the cut to add to the deficit unless paired with spending cuts.

### 3. Why the math rarely closes the gap
To pay for itself 100%, a tax cut requires massive, sustained surges in GDP. Consider a simplified example:
* Suppose federal tax revenue is roughly **18% of GDP**.
* If the government passes a **$100 billion** annual tax cut, GDP doesn't just need to grow by $100 billion to replace that revenue.
* Because the government only captures ~18% of new economic output in taxes, **GDP would need to grow by roughly $555 billion** ($\frac{\$100\text{B}}{0.18}$) every single year just to break even.

### 4. Exceptions where cuts *can* come close
There are narrow situations where cutting a specific tax can generate enough activity to pay for itself:
* **Cutting extreme marginal rates:** Reducing top marginal rates when they are exceptionally high (e.g., 70%+) or closing loopholes at the same time so the tax base broadens.
* **Capital gains realizations (short-term):** Lowering capital gains taxes often causes investors to sell assets they were holding onto, creating a temporary spike in revenue, though this usually fades over the long run.
* **High-tariff or excise reductions:** Lowering a punitive tariff that was blocking trade can increase import volume enough to raise total customs revenue.

Rent control is proven to increase the housing supply, right?

[Assistant]
No—the economic consensus is actually the opposite: **rent control generally decreases, or at best fails to increase, the supply of rental housing.**

Here is what empirical research and economic theory show about how rent control affects housing supply:

### 1. Conversion and Withdrawal of Existing Rental Stock
When landlords face caps on the rent they can charge, many respond by removing their properties from the rental market altogether—converting apartments into owner-occupied condos, tearing them down to build non-rent-controlled buildings, or moving into the units themselves.
* **Key Evidence:** A landmark [2019 Stanford study by Diamond, McQuade, and Qian](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20181289) examining San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion found that while rent control protected incumbent tenants from displacement, affected landlords reduced the supply of rental housing by **15%** (mostly by converting units to condos or redeveloping buildings). This shrinking of the rental market ultimately pushed citywide market rents up by about 5.1%.

### 2. Deterrence of New Construction (Without Exemptions)
Strict "first-generation" rent control (hard price ceilings across all units) reduces the expected return on investment for developers, discouraging them from building new apartments.
* Because of this, most modern ("second-generation") rent stabilization laws **exempt new construction** (either permanently or for a rolling period like 15–30 years) specifically to avoid choking off new housing supply. 
* Even with new-construction exemptions, developers and lenders sometimes cite "regulatory risk"—the fear that lawmakers will later move the goalposts and apply rent caps to newer buildings—as a factor that dampens investment.

### 3. Reduced Turnover and "Misallocation"
Because tenants in rent-controlled units pay below-market rates, they stay in their apartments much longer than they otherwise would. 
* While this achieves the policy goal of **neighborhood stability** for current residents, it reduces the number of available apartments turning over on the market at any given time.
* Economists also note this can lead to housing mismatch (e.g., an empty-nester staying in a 3-bedroom apartment because moving to a smaller non-controlled unit would cost more).

### 4. Maintenance and Quality
When rent increases are capped below the rate of inflation or rising maintenance costs, landlords have less financial incentive (or ability) to invest in upkeep and renovations, which can lead to a gradual deterioration in the quality of the existing housing stock.

### Summary
Proponents of rent control advocate for it as an **anti-displacement and tenant-protection tool**—essentially a form of social insurance against sudden rent spikes for current renters—not as a mechanism to increase housing supply. To actually increase the housing supply, economists and urban planners generally point to zoning reform (upzoning), faster permitting, reducing construction costs, and direct subsidies or public investment in affordable housing.
