**The framing problem**

The question presupposes these are mutually exclusive, competing explanations for a population's political affiliation. They aren't. Reasoning and social identity are not alternative causal pathways that compete for the same explanatory slot — most political beliefs anyone holds are shaped by both an inferential component (evaluating claims, evidence, arguments) and a social component (group affiliation, identity signaling, inherited community norms). This is true of democratic socialists, free-market conservatives, centrists, and people with no strong ideology at all. So "most people who support X: is it reasoning or tribalism?" is a question that would get roughly the same structural answer for any X — support for capitalism, support for a political party, religious affiliation, etc. That symmetry is itself informative: if the answer pattern doesn't differentiate democratic socialism from other political positions, then the question isn't really asking something specific about democratic socialism — it's asking something general about how humans form political beliefs, dressed up as a question about one ideology.

**What's actually knowable here**

1. **"Most people reason their way to any complex political position" is not well supported.** Political psychology research (on motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, the role of social networks and group affiliation in political belief formation) generally finds that for most people, political affiliation correlates strongly with social identity, upbringing, and group membership, and that explicit reasoning is often deployed *after* a position is held — to justify or defend it — rather than being the primary route to it. This is a well-replicated pattern across ideologies, not a special weakness of democratic socialism's supporters specifically.

Almost nobody's political-economic worldview is formed by cold first-principles reasoning in a vacuum, for *any* ideology — socialism, laissez-faire capitalism, or anything else.
