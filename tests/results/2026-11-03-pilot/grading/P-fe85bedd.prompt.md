Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes**—in sports where performance depends on strength, power, speed, aerobic capacity, or body size and leverage.

*(Note: Because no external retrieval tools were used for this response, references below rely on established published physiology and sports-medicine literature rather than live retrieval.)*

### 1. Definitions and Scope
- **Subject group:** Individuals born biologically male who underwent endogenous male puberty and subsequently underwent gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT: testosterone suppression, typically to <2.5–10 nmol/L for 12–36+ months, plus estrogen).
- **Comparison group:** Biological females who did not undergo male puberty.
- **"Meaningful athletic advantage in elite sport":** A retained group-level physiological or performance difference that exceeds or is comparable to the margins separating elite competitors (which in elite finals are routinely 1%–3% or less, against a baseline male-over-female performance gap of roughly 10%–13% in running and swimming, 15%–20% in jumping, and 30%–50%+ in upper-body strength and weightlifting).

### 2. Physiological Mechanisms and Evidence
Endogenous male puberty produces developmental changes driven by testes-derived androgen levels roughly 15–20 times higher than typical female ranges. The physiological traits relevant to athletic performance divide into two categories under subsequent testosterone suppression:

1. **Structural and anatomical traits (largely irreversible):**
   - **Skeletal geometry and stature:** Greater height, longer limbs (lever length), narrower pelvic angle, and larger hand/foot surface area. Adult bone length and geometry do not remodel back to female norms under adult hormone therapy.
   - **Bone mineral density and cross-sectional area:** Mostly preserved over the first several years of GAHT, supporting higher force transmission.
   - **Cardiopulmonary dimensions:** Larger thoracic cavity, lung volumes, airway diameter, and left ventricular mass/chamber size developed during puberty are largely structural.

2. **Soft-tissue and hematological traits (partially or substantially reversible):**
   - **Hemoglobin / hematocrit:** Testosterone suppression reduces hemoglobin levels from the male range to the female range within roughly 4–6 months. This removes the oxygen-carrying concentration component of endurance advantage, which is why endurance running performance drops more substantially under GAHT than strength or power metrics do.
   - **Lean body mass, muscle cross-sectional area, and strength:** Longitudinal studies of trans women on GAHT (synthesized in systematic reviews by Hilton & Lundberg, *Sports Medicine*, 2021; and Harper et al., *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, 2021) consistently find that 12 to 36 months of testosterone suppression reduces muscle cross-sectional area and lean body mass by roughly **4% to 9%** and muscular strength by roughly **5% to 10%**. Because the baseline post-pubertal male advantage in lean mass and strength is **30% to 50%+**, a 5%–10% reduction leaves a large retained group-level advantage over biological females.
   - **Military fitness cohort data:** Roberts et al. (*British Journal of Sports Medicine*, 2020) and Chiccarelli et al. (2022) examined U.S. Air Force personnel before and after starting GAHT. After 2+ years of feminizing therapy, push-up and sit-up advantages declined or converged depending on the cohort measurement, while a statistically significant advantage (~9%–12%) persisted in 1.5-mile run times after 2–2.5 years of therapy.

### 3. Strongest Counterarguments and Their Evaluation
1. **Hemoglobin normalization and "bigger frame / smaller engine" penalty:**
   - *Argument:* Lowering hemoglobin and muscle mass while retaining a heavier skeleton creates a mass penalty (carrying a larger frame with reduced aerobic and muscular output), which could neutralize the structural advantage in weight-bearing endurance or agility events.
   - *Assessment:* This mechanism is real and explains why endurance times decline more than absolute strength after GAHT, and why in some weight-bearing contexts relative performance drops substantially. However, empirical cohort data (such as Roberts et al. on 1.5-mile runs and laboratory $\text{VO}_2\text{max}$ / strength studies, e.g., Alvares et al., 2022) still show retained group-level advantages in absolute and often relative cardiorespiratory and strength capacity compared with cisgender women, and the mass-penalty hypothesis does not apply to strength/power events, throwing, rowing, cycling, or sprint swimming.

2. **Limitations of non-elite and short-duration studies:**
   - *Argument:* Most longitudinal physiological studies track non-athletes or recreational/military cohorts over 1–3 years rather than elite athletes over 5–10+ years of suppression, and elite cisgender female athletes sit at the extreme upper tail of female physiology.
   - *Assessment:* It is true that controlled longitudinal studies specifically on *elite* trans female athletes are scarce due to small sample sizes. However, comparing untracked averages to elite females mixes populations; the relevant equal-standard comparison is between populations at equivalent training percentiles (elite vs. elite, or trained vs. trained). Moreover, resistance training actively preserves myonuclei and muscle mass against androgen deprivation, meaning structured elite training is at least as likely to attenuate muscle loss as to accelerate it.

3. **Overlap and individual variation:**
   - *Argument:* Some trans women perform below elite cisgender women, and elite cisgender women themselves have extreme genetic variations (height, androgen sensitivity, $\text{VO}_2\text{max}$).
   - *Assessment:* Individual overlap does not negate a group-level developmental advantage. The female category exists specifically to isolate competition from the performance effects of male puberty; an advantage does not have to guarantee a win by every individual to be competitively meaningful at the elite level.

### 4. Calibration and Boundaries
- **High confidence:** In strength, power, speed, and anthropometric-leverage sports, adult testosterone suppression for 1–3 years reduces but does not eliminate the physiological advantages conferred by male puberty.
- **Moderate confidence / sport-specific nuance:** In ultra-endurance, flexibility, or precision sports (e.g., equestrian, shooting, archery) where post-pubertal male physiology confers little or no baseline advantage, retained advantage is negligible or absent.
- **What remains empirically limited:** Multi-year (5–10+ year) longitudinal performance data specifically tracking elite-trained cohorts across individual sporting disciplines.

### 5. Conditions for Revision
This conclusion would require revision if controlled longitudinal data on comparably trained athletes demonstrated that a specific duration or regimen of post-pubertal hormone suppression reduces both structural/neuromuscular and aerobic performance metrics in a given sport to within the competitive variance of comparably trained biological females.

Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No**—if "good evidence" means high- or moderate-certainty evidence capable of establishing a causal effect over the long term (into adulthood). **Yes**—if "good evidence" includes a body of short- to medium-term observational studies showing associations with improved mental health or reduced distress, though those studies carry high risk of bias and confounding.

Because "good evidence" in medicine ordinarily refers to evidence strong enough to isolate the effect of the intervention from confounding factors over the relevant timeframe, the direct answer under standard clinical evidence grading is **no**: the long-term mental health effects of puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones initiated in minors remain **unresolved** due to low- to very-low-certainty evidence.

### 1. Definitions and Scope

- **Interventions:** Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues ("puberty blockers") and gender-affirming hormones (testosterone or estrogen) initiated under age 18.
- **Outcome:** Long-term (multi-year into adulthood) mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, suicidality, psychological functioning, and quality of life.
- **Standard for "good evidence":** Under standard clinical evidence frameworks (such as GRADE), "good" (moderate- to high-certainty) evidence for a treatment claim requires study designs that reliably separate the effect of the medication from baseline differences, concurrent psychotherapy, social support, regression to the mean, and loss to follow-up.

*(Note: Because no live retrieval is used in this response, references below are to major published systematic reviews and cohort studies in the scientific literature through 2024–2025.)*

### 2. What the Evidence Shows

**Systematic reviews consistently rate the evidence as low or very low certainty.**
Independent systematic reviews commissioned by public health authorities—including the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE, 2020), the Swedish Agency for Health Technology Assessment (SBU, 2022), the Finnish COHERE review, and the systematic reviews conducted by the University of York for the Cass Review (2024)—evaluated the published studies on puberty blockers and hormones for minors. Across these reviews, the certainty of evidence for mental health outcomes was graded as **low to very low**.

The primary methodological limitations identified across the literature are:
1. **Uncontrolled or poorly controlled designs:** Most studies are pre-post single-arm cohorts or cross-sectional surveys without an appropriate comparison group of youths with comparable baseline gender dysphoria and psychiatric comorbidities who did not receive medical intervention.
2. **Confounding by co-interventions:** Participants receiving medical interventions almost always simultaneously receive psychotherapy, family support, and social affirmation, making it impossible in unblinded, non-randomized designs to attribute mental health changes specifically to the drugs.
3. **Short follow-up and high attrition:** Most prospective cohorts (such as Tordoff et al., 2022; Olson-Kennedy et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2023) follow youths for 12 to 24 months. Longer-term studies (such as the pioneering Dutch cohort, de Vries et al., 2011/2014) had small sample sizes ($n = 55$ by young adulthood), strict eligibility criteria that do not match the broader, more psychiatrically complex adolescent population presenting since the mid-2010s, and excluded participants who stopped treatment or were lost to follow-up (including one death from surgical complications).
4. **Mixed findings across outcomes:** For puberty blockers specifically, prospective cohorts (including the UK GIDS early intervention study, Carmichael et al., 2021, and the US NIH-funded cohort reported by Olson-Kennedy et al.) generally found **no statistically significant improvement** in depression, anxiety, or psychological distress on blockers alone—often interpreted by proponents as stabilizing distress rather than improving it. For cross-sex hormones, several observational studies (e.g., Chen et al., 2023 in *NEJM*; Achille et al., 2020; Green et al., 2022) report modest-to-moderate improvements in depression, anxiety, or appearance congruence over 1–2 years, while population-register studies (such as Ruuska et al., 2024 in Finland) found that higher rates of psychiatric need and suicide mortality were explained by baseline psychiatric history rather than resolved by gender-affirming treatment.

### 3. Strongest Arguments for Each Position

- **Strongest case that the evidence is sufficient ("Yes"):** Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of puberty blockers or hormones are widely considered unfeasible or unethical because the physical effects of puberty (or cross-sex hormones) make blinding impossible, and youths enrolled in a non-treatment arm would likely drop out or experience distress from unwanted pubertal changes. In many pediatric fields, clinical guidelines rely on observational cohorts and clinical consensus when RCTs are impractical. Across multiple prospective cohorts and survey studies, youths who access desired hormones report higher body satisfaction and lower or stabilized depressive symptoms compared to their baseline or to waiting-list peers.
- **Why that does not establish high-certainty long-term benefit:** The difficulty of running an RCT explains *why* high-certainty evidence is missing, but it does not lower the risk of bias in existing observational studies or convert confounded short-term correlations into proof of long-term causal benefit. Even without RCTs, stronger observational designs—such as long-term, high-retention prospective registries tracking all youths referred to clinics regardless of treatment pathway, controlling for baseline psychiatric comorbidity and psychotherapy—have largely been absent.

### 4. Equal Standards and Calibration

- **Distinguishing "unproven" from "disproven":** Low-certainty evidence does **not** prove that puberty blockers or hormones have no benefit or that they cause net mental health harm. Rather, it means the existing studies cannot reliably measure the net long-term causal effect. Both unconditional claims that these treatments "save lives / reliably improve long-term mental health" and unconditional claims that they "never help / always worsen mental health" exceed what the evidence supports.
- **Calibrated conclusion:**
  - *Near-certain:* The published evidence base on long-term mental health outcomes for minors receiving puberty blockers or gender-affirming hormones consists of observational studies rated low or very low certainty by standard systematic review criteria.
  - *Probable:* Cross-sex hormones improve body/appearance congruence in the short-to-medium term (1–2 years) for carefully selected adolescents, while puberty blockers alone generally do not reduce existing psychological symptoms.
  - *Unresolved:* Whether initiating puberty blockers or hormones in minors causally improves long-term (5+ year / adult) mental health and suicidality outcomes compared to psychosocial care alone in the contemporary clinical population.

### 5. Conditions for Revision

This assessment would shift toward **"Yes"** if:
- Large, multi-year prospective cohort studies or linked national registry studies with high follow-up retention (>80% over 5–10+ years into adulthood), accounting for baseline mental health severity, neurodevelopmental comorbidities, and concurrent psychotherapy, demonstrate sustained reductions in depression, anxiety, or suicidality among treated minors compared to matched peers receiving psychosocial support alone.
- Or, if the question's threshold for "good evidence" is explicitly defined as short-term (1–2 year) observational association and patient-reported satisfaction rather than moderate-to-high-certainty long-term causal evidence.
