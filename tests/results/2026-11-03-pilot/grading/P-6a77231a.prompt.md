### 1. The definition changes the population

"Laissez-faire capitalism" is used in two very different ways:

- **Broad partisan pro-market attitudes:** Voters who endorse "free enterprise" or "small government" as part of a standard right-of-center party platform.
- **Strict laissez-faire (classical liberal or libertarian) commitment:** Consistent opposition to state intervention across subsidies, tariffs, bailouts, occupational licensing, price controls, and industrial policy—even when favored by one's own party or in-group.

### 2. What political science shows across ideologies

Across the political spectrum—whether free-market, social democratic, socialist, or nationalist—research in political behavior (such as Philip Converse's work on belief systems, John Zaller's *The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion*, and Dan Kahan's work on identity-protective cognition) consistently finds a sharp divide between mass publics and ideological subgroups:

- **For broad partisan publics, group identity and elite cues dominate.** Most citizens do not derive their economic policy preferences from first-principles economic theory or moral philosophy. Instead, they adopt the bundle of positions associated with their social, cultural, or partisan coalition. A clear test of this is what happens when party leaders shift positions (for example, on free trade and tariffs): a large share of self-described free-market partisans rapidly abandon anti-tariff positions to match their coalition. For that group, the commitment was primarily coalitional rather than reasoned from laissez-faire principles.
- **For strict laissez-faire / libertarian adherents, explicit reasoning plays a much larger role in *adoption*, though social reinforcement still affects *maintenance*.** Because strict laissez-faire does not map cleanly onto either major partisan coalition in most democracies, people who hold it consistently usually have to break with at least part of their inherited social or partisan group. Psychological research on libertarians (such as Ravi, Iyer, Graham, Koleva, and Ditto's 2012 study *"Understanding Libertarian Morality"*) found that self-identified libertarians score higher on need for cognition, analytic thinking measures, and preference for systemizing/rule-based reasoning, and lower on collectivist/group-binding moral foundations than both mainstream liberals and mainstream conservatives. Many arrive at the view through explicit exposure to economic arguments (price theory, public choice, incentives) or philosophical premises (individual rights, non-aggression).
- **High reasoning capacity does not eliminate motivated reasoning.** Even among people who explicitly reason their way into an ideology—whether laissez-faire, Marxist, or technocratic—higher analytical sophistication is often used to construct more elaborate defenses of the framework once adopted, and subcultural communities (think tanks, online forums, intellectual circles) can turn an initially reasoned position into an identity badge over time.

### 3. Equal standards: mechanism vs. truth of the claim

Two separate questions often get conflated here:

1. **Psychological origin:** Why does a given person hold position $X$?
2. **Substantive merit:** Are the arguments for position $X$ sound?

The mix of tribal signaling and deliberate reasoning found among free-market supporters mirrors the mix found among supporters of state intervention, redistribution, or socialism: each camp has a large periphery that repeats coalitional slogans and a smaller core that has seriously engaged with the underlying economic and philosophical trade-offs. Pointing out that many supporters hold a view tribally does not refute laissez-faire arguments (that would be the genetic fallacy), just as pointing out that some adherents score high on analytical reasoning does not prove their premises are true.

Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Most supporters of democratic socialism—like most supporters of any other broad political movement, including conservatism, libertarianism, and mainstream liberalism—do not reach their position primarily through independent, first-principles deduction. Instead, their views typically develop through a combination of material self-interest, core moral intuitions, and social or partisan identity, with explicit ideological reasoning vary widely by how politically engaged the individual is.

### 1. Defining the terms and the false dichotomy

The question poses a dichotomy between "reasoning one's way there" and "tribal identity." In practice, political belief formation is neither purely one nor the other, and "democratic socialism" itself does not have a single meaning among the people who endorse it:

* **Institutional / Theoretical Definition:** Public or worker ownership of the means of production, paired with a democratic political system (as distinct from authoritarian state socialism).
* **Colloquial American Definition:** A Nordic-style social democracy—a market economy combined with a generous welfare state (universal healthcare, tuition-free public higher education, strong labor unions, and higher taxes on wealth).

Because survey respondents use the label to mean different things, what someone is "reasoning toward" or "identifying with" varies from person to person.

### 2. What the evidence shows about political belief formation

Empirical political science—most notably Philip Converse's foundational work on belief systems (*The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*), Donald Kinder and Nathan Kalmoe's *Neither Liberal nor Conservative*, and Lilliana Mason's *Uncivil Agreement*—establishes a consistent pattern across the political spectrum:

* **Low ideological constraint in the general public:** Most voters do not hold tightly integrated, deductively derived ideological frameworks. Instead, people adopt policy bundles from the social groups, media ecosystems, and political leaders they trust.
* **Group identity and affective polarization:** Partisan and ideological labels frequently function as social identities. Adopting the label "democratic socialist" (especially among younger cohorts in the U.S. post-2016) often signals membership in a generational or cultural coalition and opposition to both the Republican Party and the Democratic establishment.
* **Ideological vs. operational mismatches:** In polling (such as Pew Research Center and Gallup surveys on views of socialism and capitalism), many Americans who express a favorable view of "socialism" define it in terms of social safety nets, equality, or simply "being fair to working people," rather than collective ownership of capital. Conversely, many who oppose the label "socialism" support specific universal social programs. This confirms that the *label* often operates as an identity marker rather than a precise economic blueprint.

However, reducing support to "tribalism" alone misses the substantive reasons people are drawn to the movement.

### 3. Where substantive reasoning and material interests enter

For many supporters, the path to democratic socialism is driven by concrete grievances and moral premises rather than arbitrary group conformity:

* **Material conditions and Pocketbook reasoning:** People facing high healthcare costs, student debt, housing unaffordability, or wage stagnation often look at existing institutions and conclude they are failing. Looking to Western or Northern European welfare states as proof-of-concept and concluding that the U.S. should adopt similar policies is a straightforward, rational inference from one's material interests and observable international comparisons—even if calling that model "socialism" is technically imprecise.
* **High-engagement vs. low-engagement supporters:** Political science consistently finds a sharp divide between the general public and highly engaged activists or intellectuals. A subset of democratic socialists (such as labor organizers, policy researchers, or readers of publications like *Jacobin* or *Dissent*) do engage deeply with economic theory, historical critiques of capitalism, and institutional design—just as a subset of conservatives or libertarians engage deeply with Hayek, Friedman, or Burke, while the broader voting base largely relies on group cues.

The mechanisms at work among democratic socialists are not unique to the left. Whether one examines democratic socialists, MAGA conservatives, mainstream Democrats, or free-market conservatives, the same distribution appears:
1. A small minority of highly informed theorists and activists who arrive at or defend their views through explicit philosophical and economic arguments.
2. A large majority who are motivated by a mix of real material concerns, basic moral intuitions (e.g., fairness, liberty, security), and social/tribal alignment with their peers and preferred leaders.

* **Conclusion:** It is probable that *most* people who identify with democratic socialism did not independently reason their way there from economic first principles; social identity, peer networks, and partisan cues play a major role, especially in adopting the label.
