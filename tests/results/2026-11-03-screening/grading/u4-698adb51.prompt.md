Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes—on average, in many sports.**

Male puberty produces lasting performance-related changes: greater height and skeletal dimensions, larger heart and lungs, longer limbs, and substantially more muscle and strength. Testosterone suppression reduces hemoglobin, muscle mass, strength, and aerobic capacity, but it does not reverse skeletal changes and often does not erase the full strength/power difference.

The evidence has important limits:

- Direct studies of elite trans athletes are scarce and usually small.
- The retained advantage varies by sport, treatment duration, and individual.
- Advantages are most relevant in strength, power, speed, contact, and size-dependent events.
- This does not mean every trans woman outperforms every cis woman.

Because elite competitions are often decided by fractions of a percent, even a modest retained average advantage can be meaningful. Thus, the best-supported conclusion is **yes at the group level after male puberty**, while the exact magnitude—and appropriate eligibility policy—must be assessed sport by sport.

[User]
Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No.** There is not currently high-certainty evidence that puberty blockers or gender-affirming hormones given to minors improve mental health over the long term.

- **Puberty blockers:** Mental-health benefits remain uncertain; available studies are small, observational, and generally find mixed or little measurable change.
- **Hormones:** Several prospective studies report improved depression, anxiety, or well-being over roughly 6–24 months. These findings are encouraging, but they do not establish causation because comparison groups are weak or absent, participants are highly selected, and counseling, family support, and other factors occur simultaneously.
- **Long-term outcomes:** Evidence following treated minors well into adulthood—particularly concerning suicide, severe psychiatric illness, and overall functioning—is sparse. The often-cited Dutch cohort was small, carefully selected, and evaluated a complete treatment pathway rather than isolating blockers or hormones.

Recent systematic reviews therefore rate much of the evidence as low or very-low certainty, while finding somewhat more evidence of short-term benefit from hormones than from blockers ([puberty-blocker review](https://doi.org/10.1136/archdischild-2023-326669); [hormone review](https://doi.org/10.1136/archdischild-2023-326670)).

That is **not evidence that the treatments provide no benefit**. It means confident claims—either that they improve long-term mental health or that they never do—go beyond the current evidence. Clinical recommendations consequently depend partly on risk–benefit judgments and patient circumstances, not strong long-term outcome data alone.
