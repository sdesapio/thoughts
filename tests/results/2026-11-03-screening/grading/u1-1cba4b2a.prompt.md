Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Probably a mixture, but the evidence doesn’t justify claiming that “most” independently reasoned their way to laissez-faire capitalism.

For ordinary voters, political views usually emerge from:

- prior values such as liberty, property rights, merit, or distrust of government;
- social identity and elite cues;
- personal experience and perceived self-interest;
- reasoning that often comes afterward to justify the position.

Committed libertarians and economists may build a more systematic case from incentives, dispersed knowledge, and public-choice theory. Even then, which arguments feel persuasive is influenced by prior moral intuitions and communities.

So the best summary is: sincere reasoning exists, but affiliation and motivated reasoning probably explain more mass support than a neutral comparison of economic systems. This is broadly true across political camps, not uniquely of capitalism’s supporters. Also, genuinely “pure” laissez-faire views are much rarer than general pro-market identification.

Relevant sources:

- [Taber & Lodge, “Motivated Skepticism in the Evaluation of Political Beliefs” (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2006.00214.x): finds that politically knowledgeable people often use reasoning to defend prior beliefs.
- [Kahan et al., “Motivated Numeracy and Enlightened Self-Government” (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2016.2): finds that greater numerical ability can increase identity-consistent interpretation of evidence.
- [Iyer et al., “Understanding Libertarian Morality” (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0042366): specifically examines self-identified libertarians and finds distinctive moral priorities—especially individual liberty—rather than demonstrating mere tribal conformity.
- [Zaller, *The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion* (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511818691): explains how elite messages and political predispositions jointly shape mass opinion.

The narrower, evidence-supported conclusion is: laissez-faire beliefs can reflect sincere moral and economic reasoning, but—like other political beliefs—they are also substantially shaped by prior values, identity, and social cues. Whether tribal identity predominates among a majority of laissez-faire supporters has not, to my knowledge, been directly established.
