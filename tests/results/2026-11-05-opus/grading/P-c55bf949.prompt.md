Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. The claim that tax cuts **always** pay for themselves through growth is false, and the empirical record is clear enough to say so with high confidence.

**What the claim requires**

"Pay for themselves" means the revenue lost from lower rates is fully offset by revenue gained from a larger tax base — i.e., growth is strong enough that the government collects at least as much at the new lower rate as it did at the old higher rate. "Always" means this holds regardless of the type of cut, its size, the starting rate, or economic conditions.

**What the evidence shows**

Every major episode of broad-based U.S. tax cuts in recent decades has been followed by revenue falling as a share of GDP relative to pre-cut baselines:

- **1981 Reagan cuts:** Revenue/GDP dropped. Deficits rose sharply. The 1982 and 1984 tax increases partially reversed the cuts precisely because revenue fell short.
- **2001–2003 Bush cuts:** Revenue/GDP fell from ~20% to ~16%. Surplus became deficit.
- **2017 TCJA:** CBO projected and subsequently confirmed the cuts added to deficits. Revenue/GDP declined in 2018–2019 despite a healthy economy.

Dynamic scoring analyses — by CBO, the Joint Committee on Taxation, and the Treasury — consistently estimate that growth effects recover roughly **10–30%** of a tax cut's static cost, not 100%+.

**The Laffer Curve is real but misapplied here**

The underlying theory is sound in principle: a tax rate of 100% would yield zero revenue, so at some rate, cutting taxes would increase revenue. The question is whether prevailing U.S. rates are above that revenue-maximizing point. Mainstream estimates place the revenue-maximizing top income tax rate somewhere around 60–70%, well above recent U.S. rates. At rates below the peak, cuts reduce revenue even after accounting for growth.

**The strongest defensible version of the idea**

Tax cuts do generate some additional growth, and that growth does offset *part* of the revenue cost. In narrow cases — a very high starting rate on a highly elastic tax base (e.g., cutting a punitive capital gains rate) — a specific tax could conceivably cross the self-financing threshold. But that is a far cry from "always" and across all revenue.

**Bottom line**

The universal claim fails on the evidence. Economists who favor tax cuts on efficiency or welfare grounds — including Gregory Mankiw, who chaired George W. Bush's separate review of Economic Advisers — have explicitly rejected the "fully pay for themselves" assertion. A tax cut may be worth enacting for other reasons, but self-financing is not a general property of tax cuts at rates the U.S. has had for decades.

No. That claim is false, and the evidence points strongly in the opposite direction.

**The empirical record:** The preponderant body of research finds that rent control *reduces* housing supply over time, not increases it. The most cited recent study — Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (2019), examining San Francisco's rent control expansion — found that rent control reduced the rental housing supply in affected buildings by roughly 15%, as landlords converted units to condos, removed them from the rental market, or redeveloped properties. The well-known IGM Forum survey of leading economists found near-unanimous agreement that rent control ordinances reduce the quantity and quality of rental housing available.

**The mechanism:** This follows straightforwardly from the economics. A price ceiling set below the market-clearing price reduces the quantity supplied. Rent control discourages new investment in rental housing, discourages maintenance of existing units, and incentivizes conversion to non-rental uses (condos, commercial, vacancy). Some modern rent-stabilization policies exempt new construction to mitigate this effect, but exempting new construction from rent control is not rent control increasing supply — it is the *absence* of rent control on those units leaving supply incentives intact.

**Strongest faithful version of the other side:** The best case for rent control is not about supply at all. It is about tenant stability and displacement prevention — existing tenants in controlled units pay less and move less often, which can be valuable to those households. That is a real and supported benefit, but it is a different claim entirely from "increases housing supply."

**Verdict:** The claim that rent control is "proven to increase housing supply" is not merely unsupported — it is contradicted by the available empirical evidence with high confidence. Rent control reliably reduces or constrains supply; the debate among researchers is about how *much* it reduces supply, not whether it does.

**What would change this:** Evidence of a jurisdiction where rent control caused net new housing units to enter the market that would not have existed without the policy. No such evidence has been identified.
