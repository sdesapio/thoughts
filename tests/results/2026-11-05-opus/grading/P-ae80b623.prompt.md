Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No.** There is not good evidence that puberty blockers or hormones for minors improve long-term mental health outcomes. This is a statement about the quality and duration of the evidence, not a claim that the treatments are ineffective or harmful.

---

**What the evidence base looks like.** The existing studies are predominantly observational, with small samples, no untreated control groups, short follow-up (typically months to a few years), and high attrition. Several show short-term reductions in distress or improvements in functioning during treatment, but these designs cannot establish long-term causal benefit.

**What independent systematic reviews found.** Multiple national health authorities commissioned independent evidence reviews using standard grading methodology (GRADE), and they converged:

- **NICE evidence reviews (2020–2021):** Rated the evidence for puberty blockers on mental health outcomes as "very low certainty." Same for cross-sex hormones.
- **The Cass Review / York systematic reviews (2024):** Found "low" or "very low" quality evidence across the board. Noted that most studies could not establish whether observed changes were caused by treatment versus concurrent support, maturation, or selection effects.
- **Swedish SBU (2022):** Concluded the evidence base was insufficient to draw reliable conclusions about benefits and harms.
- **Finnish COHERE:** Found evidence insufficient and recommended psychosocial support as first-line treatment.

These reviews led the UK, Sweden, Finland, and Denmark to restrict these interventions for minors or limit them to formal research settings.

**The strongest case for "yes," and why it falls short.** The most-cited evidence is the Dutch protocol studies (de Vries et al., 2011, 2014), which showed improved psychological functioning. However: the sample completing the full protocol was small (~55), there was no control group, the patients were highly selected (childhood-onset dysphoria, no major comorbidities), outcomes were measured after the full protocol including surgery (not isolating blockers or hormones), and follow-up was short. Other frequently cited studies (Turban et al., 2020; Tordoff et al., 2022) are cross-sectional or short-duration observational studies with similar limitations. Consistency of direction across studies sharing the same methodological weaknesses does not cure those weaknesses.

**An important distinction.** "Low-quality evidence" is not the same as "evidence of no effect." It is possible these treatments provide long-term benefit; the point is that the existing research cannot establish this with reasonable confidence. The ethical difficulty of conducting RCTs is real, but it does not make the available evidence stronger than it is — it means we may lack the ideal study designs, which is itself a reason for caution about confident claims in either direction.

**What would change this assessment.** Well-designed longitudinal studies with adequate comparison groups, large samples, and follow-up extending well into adulthood (5–10+ years) that demonstrated sustained mental health improvement would shift the evidence from low to moderate or high quality.

Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes.** The current body of evidence indicates that trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over cisgender females in most elite sport contexts, even after prolonged hormone therapy.

## Key evidence

**Structural changes that do not reverse.** Male puberty permanently establishes greater height, longer limbs, broader shoulders, larger hands and feet, a larger rib cage, greater lung volume, and a larger heart. Hormone therapy does not alter skeletal dimensions or organ size. In elite sport, where margins of victory are often 1–3%, these structural differences are competitively significant.

**Muscle and strength: reduced but not equalized.** Roberts et al. (2021, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*), studying U.S. Air Force members, found that after two years of testosterone suppression, trans women still ran 1.5 miles approximately 9% faster than cisgender women — down from roughly 21% pre-transition, but still a large gap by elite standards. Advantages in push-up and sit-up performance also persisted. Hilton and Lundberg (2021, *Sports Medicine*) reviewed the male performance advantage across events (10–50% depending on discipline) and concluded that hormone therapy produces only partial reversal.

**Hemoglobin decreases; cardiovascular structure does not.** Testosterone suppression brings hemoglobin into typical female ranges, but the larger heart and lung capacity developed during male puberty persist, maintaining a residual cardiovascular advantage.

**Governing body conclusions.** The convergence of evidence drove major federations toward increasingly restrictive policies. World Aquatics (2022) restricted the women's category to those who transitioned before age 12. World Athletics (2023) effectively excluded post-male-puberty trans women from international female competition. The IOC's 2021 framework acknowledged that no single testosterone threshold could ensure fairness and deferred to individual sports.

## Strongest opposing consideration

Hormone therapy substantially reduces — and in some parameters nearly eliminates — certain components of the advantage, particularly hemoglobin and some lean mass. In specific endurance events, the residual gap may be smaller than in power or strength events. Some earlier, smaller studies (Harper 2015, n=8) suggested age-graded performance could be comparable for distance runners.

This argument is about degree, not direction. It concedes a residual advantage exists and contends it might be small enough in certain contexts. But even the most favorable data (Roberts 2021) shows a ~9% running speed gap after two years — enormous at the elite level — and does not address the irreversible skeletal and cardiopulmonary differences.

## Conclusion

The advantage is reduced by hormone therapy but not eliminated. Structural and residual neuromuscular differences remain large enough to be competitively decisive in elite contexts. The magnitude varies by sport — likely largest in events dependent on strength, power, height, and frame size — but a meaningful advantage persists across the evidence base. High confidence; this reflects the convergence of multiple studies, known physiology, and the conclusions reached independently by major sports governing bodies.

**Revision conditions:** Large, longitudinal studies showing complete performance equalization after extended hormone therapy; identification of a mechanism by which skeletal and organ-size differences reverse; or elite competition data showing post-male-puberty trans women performing at or below cisgender female norms across multiple sports. None are currently met.
