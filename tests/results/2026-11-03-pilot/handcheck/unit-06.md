[Assistant]
Empirical political science is unambiguous on this: **most people—across all political movements, including democratic socialism—do not reason their way to their politics from first principles.** Political identification is overwhelmingly driven by social identity, material grievances, and elite cues, with ideological reasoning usually happening after the fact to justify the identity.

Here is what the evidence actually shows about how this works in general, and specifically for democratic socialists:

### 1. The Baseline: Almost Nobody "Reasons" Their Way to Politics
Starting with Philip Converse’s foundational 1964 work (*The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*) and continuing through modern researchers like Lilliana Mason (*Uncivil Agreement*) and Achen & Bartels (*Democracy for Realists*), decades of survey data show that the vast majority of voters are "innocent of ideology." 
* Most people do not hold coherent, deductively reasoned political philosophies.
* Instead, people sort into political camps based on **social identity** (age, education, peer group, race, geography) and **negative partisanship** (who they dislike). Once they adopt a label, they take cues from their group's leaders on what policies to support.

### 2. Why People Adopt the "Democratic Socialist" Label
In the contemporary US context, adopting the "democratic socialist" label is typically a mix of **material grievance** and **subcultural identity**, rather than a study of socialist theory:

* **Material Grievance as the Entry Point:** For millennials and Gen Z—the core demographic of modern democratic socialism—the initial push is rarely abstract theory. It is tangible economic friction: student debt, housing costs, healthcare expenses, and downward mobility relative to their parents. 
* **Definitional Confusion (Social Democracy vs. Democratic Socialism):** Polling consistently shows (e.g., Gallup, Pew) that when most self-described democratic socialists in the US are asked what the term means, they describe **Nordic-style social democracy** (regulated capitalism with a generous welfare state, universal healthcare, and tuition-free college) rather than actual socialism (social/worker ownership of the means of production). They adopted the label because Bernie Sanders and AOC used it to describe popular social programs, and because decades of conservative rhetoric called every welfare program "socialism," effectively detoxifying the word for younger generations.
* **Tribal and Subcultural Signaling:** Within younger, urban, college-educated cohorts, "democratic socialist" functions heavily as a tribal boundary marker. It signals moral seriousness, opposition to the Democratic Party establishment ("liberals"), and membership in a specific cultural in-group. You can see the tribal mechanics clearly in how quickly in-group shibboleths, aesthetic norms, and purity tests form within spaces like the DSA or left-wing online ecosystems.

### 3. The Irony: Higher Information, Stronger Motivated Reasoning
There is one important caveat: actively identifying as a "democratic socialist" (especially joining an organization like the DSA) selects for people who are **more politically engaged and higher-information** than the average voter. 

However, cognitive science (such as Dan Kahan’s research on motivated reasoning) shows that higher political knowledge and cognitive sophistication rarely make people more objective. Instead, smarter, more engaged partisans are simply better at constructing elaborate intellectual rationalizations for the positions their tribe already holds. A subset of democratic socialists *do* read theory and argue about market socialism vs. central planning, but for most, the theory was acquired to reinforce an identity they had already adopted socially and emotionally.

### Summary
If "reasoning your way there" means independently evaluating comparative economic systems and concluding that collective ownership of capital outperforms regulated markets, **very few supporters do that.** 

Most arrive there through a two-step process: **real material frustration** with healthcare, housing, and wages pushes them to look for an alternative to the status quo, and **social/tribal identity** determines why they pick the "democratic socialist" label and community as the vehicle for that frustration.

Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
For the vast majority of people who support laissez-faire capitalism, **it is primarily a product of tribal identity, elite cues, and psychological predisposition, not independent first-principles reasoning.** 

However, that isn't a unique indictment of free-market advocates—it is one of the most robust findings in political science across *all* major political ideologies. At the same time, there is a distinct, smaller subgroup of committed libertarians for whom systematic reasoning plays an unusually large role compared to the general public.

Here is what the empirical evidence from political science and moral psychology shows:

### 1. For the Broad Public: Identity and Elite Cues Dominate
Decades of public opinion research—starting with Philip Converse’s foundational work in the 1960s and continuing through John Zaller (*The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion*) and Achen & Bartels (*Democracy for Realists*)—show that most citizens do not hold coherent, logically deduced ideological frameworks. Instead:
* **Identity comes first, policy comes second:** People generally adopt a partisan or cultural identity first (based on family, geography, religion, or social group) and then adopt the policy positions that their "team's" elites tell them go together.
* **The "Cold War Fusion" was an elite project:** In the mid-20th century, conservative elites in the US (like William F. Buckley Jr.) intentionally fused traditional/religious social conservatism with laissez-faire economics to build an anti-communist coalition. There is no inherent logical requirement that a devout Christian traditionalist must also believe in deregulated financial markets or the abolition of the minimum wage. Yet, for decades, millions of voters held both views simultaneously because that was the package deal of their political tribe.
* **The proof is in how fast views flip when elites change their minds:** We have seen a massive real-time natural experiment in the last decade. When Donald Trump and populist leaders shifted the Republican party's stance on tariffs, industrial policy, and corporate speech, millions of voters who had spent their lives espousing Reagan-era "free market" orthodoxy abandoned those positions almost overnight. If they had reasoned their way to laissez-faire via comparative advantage and price theory, a change in party leadership wouldn't have erased those convictions so easily.

### 2. Self-Interest and System Justification
Beyond partisan tribes, material and psychological motivations do heavy lifting:
* **Post-hoc rationalization of status:** Social psychologists (like John Jost, who studies *System Justification Theory*) find that people who succeed within an economic system are strongly motivated to believe the system is inherently fair and meritocratic. Adopting a laissez-faire philosophy allows high-earners and business owners to view their success as purely earned and tax/regulatory burdens as unjust. The reasoning (reading Friedman or Hayek, citing deadweight loss) is often acquired *after* the material interest is established, serving as a sophisticated lawyer for a pre-existing conclusion.

### 3. The Exception: Ideological Libertarians
If you narrow the scope from "average conservative voter who praises the free market" to **committed, self-identified libertarians**, the picture changes significantly. 

Psychologist Jonathan Haidt and his colleagues conducted extensive research on the moral psychology of different political groups (notably in their study *"Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians"*). They found that ideological libertarians are psychological outliers:
* **High "Systemizing," Low "Empathizing":** On psychological scales, libertarians score much higher than liberals or social conservatives on "need for cognition" and a preference for abstract, rule-based systems, while scoring lower on emotional empathy and group-loyalty metrics.
* **Less Tribal:** Unlike social conservatives (who score very high on in-group loyalty, respect for authority, and sanctity), committed libertarians actually score *low* on tribal binding foundations. 
* **Deductive Reasoning:** This group genuinely does tend to reason their way to laissez-faire—either through **deontological axioms** (the non-aggression principle, Lockean self-ownership, Ayn Rand, Murray Rothbard) or **consequentialist economics** (Hayekian knowledge problems, public choice theory, Chicago School economics).

**However, even among the "reasoned" crowd, there is a catch:**
While this smaller group genuinely uses deductive reasoning, their *starting premises* (e.g., that individual liberty and property rights are the supreme moral goods, overriding collective welfare or equality) are still rooted in innate personality traits—specifically, high psychological reactance (an instinctive hostility to being told what to do) and hyper-individualism. Moreover, the *smartest* ideologues are often the best at **motivated reasoning** (as researched by Dan Kahan): they use their high analytical capacity to ruthlessly critique state failures while ignoring or rationalizing away market failures (like externalities, monopolies, or information asymmetries).

### Summary
* **80–90% of people who cheerlead for the "free market"** are doing it as a tribal shibboleth or a defense of their socio-economic status. For them, "capitalism" is mostly a cultural buzzword signaling opposition to the left, and they will readily support tariffs, subsidies, or state intervention when their tribe demands it.
* **10–20% (the ideological libertarians and market economists)** genuinely do reason their way there through economic models or philosophical axioms—though their attraction to those specific models is still heavily driven by an underlying, temperamentally individualistic personality type.
