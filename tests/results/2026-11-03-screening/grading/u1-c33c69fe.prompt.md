Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
In political science and psychology, the short answer is **both—and that applies to almost every political ideology**, not just democratic socialism. 

Researchers who study how people form political beliefs generally find that human beings rarely sit down with economics textbooks and reason their way to an ideology from scratch. Instead, political beliefs emerge from a mix of **material self-interest, moral values, elite cues, and social identity.**

Here is how those factors break down specifically for supporters of democratic socialism:

### 1. The "Reasoning" Side: Material Conditions and Policy Preferences
Many people arrive at democratic socialism through lived economic experience and practical reasoning about specific problems:
* **Material grievances:** Rising costs of healthcare, housing, and higher education, combined with stagnant wages and student debt, lead many people (especially Millennials and Gen Z) to conclude that the current market system isn't working for them.
* **Comparative reasoning:** In the US, many people who call themselves "democratic socialists" point to the Nordic model (Denmark, Sweden, Norway). Strictly speaking, political scientists classify those countries as *social democracies* (capitalist economies with strong welfare states and labor unions), not socialist economies. However, for many voters, adopting the label is a reasoned response: *"Those countries have universal healthcare and paid family leave; they call that socialism here, so I guess I'm a democratic socialist."*
* **Moral foundations:** Psychologist Jonathan Haidt’s research shows that left-leaning individuals prioritize the moral foundations of **care/harm** and **fairness/reciprocity** (specifically defined as equality of outcomes/needs). If those are your core axioms, supporting a robust welfare state and workplace democracy is a logically consistent conclusion.

### 2. The "Tribal Identity" Side: Expressive Politics and Group Belonging
At the same time, political scientists (like Lilliana Mason in *Uncivil Agreement* and Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels in *Democracy for Realists*) have shown that **most politics is identity-first and reasoning-second**:
* **Social sorting and peer networks:** For many young, urban, college-educated people, identifying as a democratic socialist functions as a social badge. It signals belonging to an in-group that values compassion, anti-establishment politics, and solidarity, while distinguishing oneself from both conservatives and "establishment" liberals.
* **Motivated reasoning:** Once someone adopts a label—whether "democratic socialist," "libertarian," or "MAGA conservative"—they tend to engage in post-hoc rationalization. They consume media, follow influencers, and adopt policy positions that reinforce their group's worldview while dismissing counterarguments.
* **Semantic drift as a tribal marker:** Surveys (such as those by Pew Research and Gallup) consistently show that Americans have wildly different definitions of "socialism." For many supporters, the term means "government provides healthcare and billionaires pay higher taxes" rather than "worker or state ownership of the means of production." Using a historically radical label for relatively mainstream social-democratic policies often serves an **expressive** purpose—signaling dissatisfaction with the status quo—rather than reflecting a deep dive into Marxist or socialist economic theory.

### 3. Does this make democratic socialists unique?
No. Empirical research into public opinion (going back to Philip Converse’s famous 1964 study *The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*) shows that across the entire political spectrum:
* **A small minority (~10–15%)** are "ideologues" who deeply study political philosophy, economics, and policy trade-offs, and hold internally consistent views.
* **The vast majority** rely on heuristics (mental shortcuts), cultural identity, generational norms, and trusted figures (like Bernie Sanders, AOC, Ronald Reagan, or Donald Trump) to decide what they support.

**Summary:**
A subset of democratic socialists have genuinely reasoned their way there through political theory or economic critique. Another large subset reasoned their way to wanting specific policies (like Medicare for All) and adopted the label because both critics and champions called those policies "socialist." And for many, the label is primarily a cultural and tribal identity that expresses shared values and generational frustration. In practice, most supporters are a blend of all three.

Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Research in political science and moral psychology suggests that for *most* people—across all political ideologies—beliefs are primarily driven by **social identity, cultural cues, and moral intuitions**, with explicit reasoning often acting as post-hoc justification. However, laissez-faire supporters do show a distinct psychological profile compared to other groups, where abstract, system-level reasoning plays a somewhat larger role for a specific subset.

Here is how the research breaks this down:

### 1. The General Rule: Identity and Elite Cues First
In political science (notably the work of Philip Converse, John Zaller, and later Christopher Achen and Larry Bartels in *Democracy for Realists*), the consensus is that most voters do not deduce policy preferences from first-principles economic theory. Instead:
* **Partisan sorting:** People adopt the economic views of their cultural or political "team." For example, as the Republican party in the US fused free-market economics with social conservatism in the mid-20th century, many voters adopted laissez-faire rhetoric because it was bundled with their religious, regional, or cultural identity.
* **Motivated reasoning:** When people *do* know the economic arguments (e.g., Friedrich Hayek’s knowledge problem or Milton Friedman’s critiques of regulation), they have often learned them to defend an identity or material interest they already held.
* **Empirical tell:** When party elites shift their stance on free markets (such as recent populist shifts toward tariffs, industrial policy, and immigration restrictions), a large portion of the base shifts with them almost overnight—indicating their prior commitment was to the coalition, not laissez-faire principles.

### 2. The Exception: The "High-Systemizing" Libertarian Subset
Moral psychologist Jonathan Haidt (along with researchers Ravi Iyer, Spassena Koleva, and Matt Motyl) studied self-identified libertarians—the purest advocates of laissez-faire capitalism—and found they actually look quite different from both liberals and traditional conservatives:
* **Lower tribalism:** Libertarians scored significantly lower on "in-group loyalty" and collectivism than both liberals and social conservatives.
* **Need for Cognition:** They scored higher on psychological measures of "need for cognition" (enjoying abstract thinking) and "systemizing" (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems), and lower on empathy/emotional reactivity.
* **First-principles deduction:** For this specific subgroup, many genuinely *do* reason their way into the position—often starting from a single axiom (like the non-aggression principle or individual liberty) and following it to logical extremes, even when the conclusions are socially unpopular.

*(Note: Even here, Haidt's "intuitionist" model still applies—their starting axiom is an intuitive preference for individual autonomy over group cohesion, which their high-systemizing minds then build an elaborate intellectual framework around.)*

### 3. Material Self-Interest and "Just-World" Intuitions
Beyond pure tribalism and pure theory, two other psychological factors drive support:
* **Economic self-interest:** Business owners, high earners, and people in heavily regulated industries often arrive at laissez-faire views through direct personal friction with taxes and bureaucracy, then generalize that experience into a broader philosophy.
* **Proportionality intuition:** People vary in how strongly they value *proportionality* (the feeling that people should get out what they put in) versus *equality of outcome*. Those with a strong intuitive preference for proportionality naturally gravitate toward market narratives.

### Summary
If you are looking at **the broad voting public** that cheers for free-market slogans, it is mostly tribal identity and partisan bundling. If you are looking at **ideological libertarians and market purists**, they are disproportionately likely to have reasoned their way there through abstract, rule-based thinking—though still anchored by an underlying personality trait that prizes autonomy over group attachment.
