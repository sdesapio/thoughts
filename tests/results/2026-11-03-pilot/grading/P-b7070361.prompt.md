Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No—the broad consensus among economists across the political spectrum is that tax cuts **almost never** fully pay for themselves through economic growth under typical conditions in developed economies.

While tax cuts often do stimulate economic growth, the extra tax revenue generated from that new growth is usually much smaller than the initial revenue lost from lowering the rates.

Here is how the economics break down:

### 1. The Laffer Curve
The idea that tax cuts can pay for themselves comes from the **Laffer Curve**, which illustrates the relationship between tax rates and tax revenue:
* At a **0% tax rate**, the government collects $0.
* At a **100% tax rate**, no one has an incentive to work or invest legally, so the government also collects $0 (or close to it).
* Somewhere in between is the **revenue-maximizing tax rate** (often estimated by economists to be around 60%–70% when accounting for all taxes).

If current tax rates are *above* that peak (the "prohibitive range"), cutting taxes will spur enough work, investment, and reduced tax avoidance to actually **increase** total revenue. However, if tax rates are *below* that peak—which is where the U.S. and most developed economies normally sit—cutting taxes reduces government revenue.

### 2. Partial Offset ("Dynamic Scoring")
When organizations like the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), the Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT), or the Tax Policy Center evaluate major tax cuts using "dynamic scoring" (which accounts for macroeconomic growth effects), they typically find that:
* **Tax cuts partially offset their cost:** Depending on how the tax cut is structured, economic growth usually recoups **10% to 35%** of the lost revenue over a 10-year window.
* **The remainder adds to the deficit:** Unless paired with spending cuts or base-broadening (eliminating deductions/loopholes), the remaining 65% to 90% reduces net government revenue.

### 3. Why Don't They Fully Pay for Themselves?
* **The math requires massive growth:** If the effective tax rate is 20%, every $1 of lost tax revenue requires **$5 of brand-new GDP** just to break even ($5 × 20% = $1). Generating that much additional output solely from a marginal rate change is very rare.
* **Financing effects (Crowding out):** If a tax cut isn't paired with spending cuts, the government has to borrow more money. Increased government borrowing can push up interest rates, which "crowds out" some private business investment and dampens the growth boost from the tax cut.
* **Income vs. Substitution effects:** Lower taxes increase the reward for working an extra hour (the *substitution effect*, which encourages work), but they also make people hit their target take-home pay with fewer hours (the *income effect*, which can reduce work).

### 4. Exceptions: When *Can* a Tax Cut Pay for Itself?
There are specific, narrow scenarios where cutting a tax can increase or maintain revenue:
* **Extreme marginal rates:** Cutting top marginal income tax rates when they are extremely high (e.g., 70%–90%, as in the U.S. mid-20th century) can reduce tax shelters and avoidance enough to recoup a large share of revenue.
* **Capital gains taxes (short term):** Lowering capital gains rates can cause investors who were holding onto assets to sell them immediately ("unlocking" gains), creating a temporary spike in tax revenue—though this often fades over the long run.
* **Tariffs and excise taxes:** If a tariff or sin tax is set so high that it kills trade or pushes sales into the black market, lowering it can bring legal activity back and raise revenue.
