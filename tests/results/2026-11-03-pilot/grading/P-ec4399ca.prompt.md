Yes, that is largely right—though the crucial caveat from political science is that it applies to **almost everyone in politics**, not just progressives, while playing out in a few distinct ways on the modern left.

Here is what the empirical evidence shows:

### 1. The baseline: Almost nobody reasons their way to their politics
For decades, political scientists (starting with Philip Converse in 1964, through Donald Kinder, Lilliana Mason, and Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels’ *Democracy for Realists*) have tested how people form political beliefs. The findings are lopsided and consistent:

* **Identity comes first, policy views follow.** Most people do not evaluate policies from first principles and then pick the party or movement that matches. Instead, they inherit or adopt a social identity (through family, university, geography, peer group, or demographics) and then take cues from their group’s elites and peers on what to believe.
* **Bundling proves it.** There is no strict logical connection that requires someone’s view on nuclear energy, marginal tax rates, Middle East foreign policy, and gender identity to all align in a single predictable package. When millions of people adopt the exact same bundle of 20 unrelated issue positions, they did not independently reason their way to all 20; they adopted a package deal from their tribe.
* **Education makes people better rationalizers, not more objective.** Studies by Dan Kahan and others on "identity-protective cognition" show that more educated, politically engaged people are actually *more* likely to engage in motivated reasoning. They use their cognitive skills to defend their group’s orthodoxy and filter out disconfirming evidence.

### 2. What is distinct about progressive tribalism?
While conservatives are just as tribal (often organized around religion, nationalism, and counter-cultural grievance), empirical research highlights a few ways progressive identity functions uniquely today:

* **High ideological uniformity and social enforcement:** The *Hidden Tribes* study (More in Common) found that "Progressive Activists"—who are disproportionately white, highly educated, secular, and affluent—are one of the most ideologically uniform groups in the country and the most likely to cut off friendships or socially penalize peers over political disagreements.
* **Secular moral signaling:** As traditional religious affiliation has declined among the educated left, politics has increasingly absorbed the psychological functions of religion: purity norms, heresy, public confession, and rapidly shifting linguistic shibboleths. * **Institutional concentration:** Because progressive norms dominate knowledge-economy institutions (academia, media, NGOs, arts, HR), adopting progressive views in those environments is often the path of least social resistance. Those traits genuinely push someone toward the left before peer pressure even starts.
* **Core values vs. specific dogmas:** Many progressives genuinely hold a reasoned (or deeply felt) commitment to reducing inequality or protecting marginalized groups. What is tribal is usually not that broad moral impulse, but the *specific, rapidly changing policy orthodoxies and taboos* that get attached to it at any given moment.

If you look at the empirical political science and moral psychology literature, libertarians are arguably the *least* purely tribal and *most* explicitly "reasoned" (or at least cognitive/systemizing) ideological group—though that comes with its own set of blind spots.

Here is what the evidence actually shows:

### 1. Where the claim is right: Almost everyone is tribal
In political science (going back to Philip Converse in 1964 and reinforced by modern researchers like Lilliana Mason and Donald Kinder), the overwhelming consensus is that **most people do not reason their way to their politics.** Most citizens adopt the political identity of their social group, family, or cultural tribe, and then use motivated reasoning to rationalize those beliefs after the fact. 

To the extent that libertarians are human beings participating in politics—especially those who only adopt the "libertarian" label because they are culturally conservative Republicans who like weed or dislike the GOP establishment—tribalism and identity signaling play a huge role.

### 2. Where the claim is wrong: Libertarians are uniquely "reasoned" compared to others
The largest empirical study of libertarian psychology—conducted by Jonathan Haidt, Ravi Iyer, and colleagues (*"Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians"*, 2012, studying over 11,000 libertarians)—found that libertarians have a distinct psychological profile that is **literally the opposite of tribal**:

* **High Cognitive Reflection / Need for Cognition:** Libertarians score significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on the Cognitive Reflection Test (measuring the tendency to override gut intuition and use deliberative, analytical reasoning) and on "Need for Cognition" (enjoying abstract, system-level thinking).
* **High "Systemizing" over "Empathizing":** On Simon Baron-Cohen’s scales, libertarians score much higher than other groups on systemizing (wanting to understand and build rule-based systems) and lower on empathizing. They are drawn to first-principles axioms (like the non-aggression principle or price theory) and follow them deductively, even when the conclusions feel socially cold or counterintuitive.
* **Exceptionally Low Social Connectedness and In-Group Loyalty:** On Moral Foundations questionnaires, libertarians score very low on *Loyalty/In-group* and *Authority* (unlike conservatives) and low on *Care/Harm* (unlike liberals). Psychologically, they report weaker ties to communities, nations, and groups. They are terrible at forming tribes, which is why libertarian political organizations are notoriously fractious.

### 3. You Have to Distinguish Two Groups of "Libertarians"
Public opinion research (such as Pew Research and Cato/YouGov studies) shows a sharp divide between two groups:

1. **Self-Identified / Ideological Libertarians (~3–5% of the population):** These people *did* largely reason (or read) their way there—usually via economics, Ayn Rand, Milton Friedman, or Friedrich Hayek. Because you rarely grow up in a "libertarian neighborhood" or "libertarian church," becoming a committed libertarian almost always requires an active, cognitive break from the mainstream two-party consensus.
2. **Cultural / "Don't Tread on Me" Libertarians (~10–15% of the population):** In the US, there is a much larger group of people who use libertarian aesthetics (Gadsden flags, anti-government rhetoric) as a cultural identity. Empirical polling (like Lee Drutman’s voter study data) shows many of these people are not actually libertarian on policy—they often support trade protectionism, strict immigration restrictions, or state intervention in culture wars. For *this* group, your premise is right: it is a tribal/cultural identity rooted in anti-institutional resentment or rugged-individualist aesthetics rather than principled reasoning.

### The Real Flaw in Libertarian Thinking
If you want to critique how libertarians arrive at their views, the evidence points to a different flaw than "tribalism." 

For most liberals and conservatives, the flaw is **emotional/tribal conformity**: adopting the group's consensus and rationalizing it.
For ideological libertarians, the flaw is **hyper-rationalist autism / deductive overreach**: taking a simplified set of axioms about human behavior, property, and markets, and deducing a totalizing worldview from them while ignoring messy empirical realities, human psychology, and the value of social cohesion. They didn't skip the reasoning step—they over-indexed on abstract reasoning at the expense of social and empirical reality.
