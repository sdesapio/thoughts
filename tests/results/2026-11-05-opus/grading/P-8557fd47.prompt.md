No, that's essentially the opposite of the mainstream economic consensus. Most empirical research and the broad majority of economists find that rent control tends to **reduce** housing supply over time, not increase it. Here's a quick summary of why:

1. **Reduced incentive to build** — When rents are capped, the expected return on new construction falls, discouraging developers from building new rental housing.

2. **Conversion and withdrawal** — Landlords may convert rent-controlled units to condos, short-term rentals, or other uses not covered by the controls, shrinking the rental stock. A well-known 2019 Stanford study (Diamond, McQuade, & Qian) on San Francisco's rent control found that landlords reduced rental supply by about 15% in response.

3. **Reduced maintenance and quality** — With limited ability to raise rents, landlords have less financial incentive (and sometimes less ability) to maintain or improve properties, leading to deterioration of existing stock.

4. **Misallocation** — Tenants in rent-controlled units tend to stay longer than they otherwise would (even when the unit no longer fits their needs), reducing turnover and availability for new renters.

Rent control can achieve other goals — like protecting *current* tenants from displacement and providing short-term affordability — and those are legitimate policy considerations. But increasing overall housing supply is not among its demonstrated effects. Policies more reliably linked to increased supply include **zoning reform**, **streamlined permitting**, and **construction subsidies**.

Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No, that's a common claim but it's not supported by the broad consensus of economists or by historical evidence.

**The idea** (often called "supply-side" or "Laffer Curve" reasoning) is that cutting tax rates boosts economic growth so much that the government actually collects *more* revenue at the lower rate. While the Laffer Curve itself is a real conceptual framework — at some extreme tax rate, cutting rates *would* increase revenue — the key question is always **where on the curve you currently sit**.

**What the evidence generally shows:**

- **Partial offset, not full payback.** Tax cuts can stimulate some additional growth, which recoups *part* of the lost revenue — but typically only a fraction (commonly estimated at 10–30% of the static cost, depending on the type of cut and economic conditions).
- **Major historical test cases** — the 1981 Reagan cuts, the 2001/2003 Bush cuts, and the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act — all resulted in revenues falling well short of pre-cut projections, even after accounting for growth effects. Deficits grew.
- **The CBO, JCT, and most mainstream economists** (including many conservative-leaning ones like Greg Mankiw) have consistently found that tax cuts do not fully pay for themselves under realistic modeling assumptions.

**Nuances:**

- The *type* of tax cut matters. Cuts that directly incentivize investment or labor supply have stronger growth effects than, say, broad income tax cuts tilted toward high earners.
- Economic conditions matter. A cut during a recession with slack in the economy behaves differently than one at full employment.
- Revenue feedback is real — just not 100%+.

So the short answer: tax cuts can promote growth and recoup *some* revenue, but the claim that they *always* fully pay for themselves is more political talking point than economic reality.
