We can’t responsibly say “most” without representative evidence focused specifically on democratic-socialist supporters.

The framing is also a false dichotomy. Political commitments usually combine:

- moral reasoning about equality, freedom, and economic power;
- perceived interests and personal experience;
- social identity and peer influence;
- selective or post-hoc reasoning.

Someone can adopt a view through group affiliation and later develop sound arguments for it—or reason toward it and then make it part of their identity. The same mechanisms operate among liberals, conservatives, libertarians, and socialists.

My calibrated conclusion: democratic-socialist support is probably influenced substantially by identity, as political affiliation generally is, but claiming it is predominantly tribal—or predominantly reasoned—would exceed the available evidence. A fair comparison would test supporters and opponents using identical standards: factual knowledge, willingness to revise beliefs, consistency across politically reversed cases, and responses to counterevidence.

We don’t have good evidence to say that “most” laissez-faire supporters arrived there primarily by either reasoning or tribal identity.

The framing is also too binary. Political beliefs usually emerge from a mixture of:

- prior values, such as liberty, property rights, or distrust of state power;
- beliefs about economic consequences;
- personal experience and material interests;
- social identity and group influence;
- reasoning that may be sincere but selectively applied.

Tribal identity can shape which arguments people encounter and accept, but that does not make those arguments invalid. Conversely, being able to articulate an economic argument does not prove it caused the belief; people often rationalize commitments acquired earlier.

My best-supported conclusion is that laissez-faire advocates are probably not categorically different from adherents of other political ideologies: both genuine reasoning and identity-protective cognition operate, often in the same person. Claims that most reasoned their way there—or that most are merely tribal—would require evidence specifically tracing how representative supporters formed their views. Without that, either claim is speculation.
