1. **Very few people in *any* political movement reason from first principles.** Across the general public, only a small, highly politically engaged minority constructs a coherent ideological system first and derives its group loyalties afterward. 2. **Social identity and elite cues are major drivers across the board.** People strongly gravitate toward the political labels of their social networks, age cohorts, educational environments, and trusted leaders. Connecting a material problem to a proposed policy solution is a form of practical reasoning, even when a voter's use of the term "socialism" is historically or technically imprecise.

- **Probable:** Most supporters of democratic socialism arrive at the position through a **combination of practical/material reasoning and social-identity sorting**, rather than either pure philosophical deduction or pure mindless tribalism. ### 1. Clarifying the terms and the populations

Both mechanisms exist, and they are not mutually exclusive: a person can arrive at a position through explicit reasoning and later reinforce it through group identity, or adopt a group label first and learn the arguments later. To answer what drives "most" supporters, you have to distinguish two very different groups:

1. **Broad survey respondents** who favor "free enterprise," "less government," or "capitalism" on standard polling items.
2. **Explicit advocates of strict laissez-faire**—people who consistently oppose not only welfare transfers and labor regulation, but also tariffs, corporate subsidies, occupational licensing, bailouts, and industrial policy.

### 2. Broad supporters vs. explicit laissez-faire advocates

**Among broad mass-public supporters, partisan and cultural identity usually leads.**
In general public-opinion research (descending from Philip Converse's work on belief systems in mass publics and modern studies of partisan sorting and motivated reasoning), most citizens across the political spectrum do not derive policy preferences from first-principles economic or philosophical theory. Instead, they take cues from partisan elites, cultural in-groups, or media ecosystems. 
- When "free markets" acts primarily as a partisan badge, policy preferences shift when party leaders shift—for example, self-described free-market conservatives becoming supportive of tariffs or state intervention when their coalition's leadership favors them.

**Among strict, consistent laissez-faire supporters, explicit reasoning is much more common—and conventional "tribal" incentives are weak.**
Strict laissez-faire (classical liberal, libertarian, or Austrian/Chicago-school market radicalism) is a small minority position in almost every modern electorate. For this narrower group, the "tribal identity" explanation is much weaker for three reasons:
1. **Cross-pressured positions:** Consistent laissez-faire requires opposing policies that are popular with both major partisan coalitions (opposing tariffs, farm subsidies, zoning restrictions, and bailouts on the right; opposing price controls, heavy regulation, and redistribution on the left). Adopting a view that alienates both major political tribes is poorly explained by mass conformity.
2. **Counter-intuitive mechanisms:** Core laissez-faire arguments—such as comparative advantage, the price system as a dispersed-information mechanism (Hayek), or public-choice critiques of regulatory capture (Stigler, Buchanan)—are abstract and often counter-intuitive. People rarely hold those specific positions without having engaged with the arguments, books, or economic models behind them.
3. **Psychological profiles:** Research on self-identified libertarians (such as Ravi Iyer, Jonathan Haidt, and colleagues' 2012 study *Understanding Libertarian Morality*) found that compared to both liberals and conservatives, libertarians scored lower on collectivism and group-loyalty measures and higher on need for cognition and preference for analytical/systemizing reasoning over empathetic or group-binding moral foundations.

Even within this smaller ideological group, subcultural identity still plays a role once someone joins a movement, think tank, or online community—group jargon, boundary-policing over who is a "real" libertarian, and motivated defense of market outcomes all occur. Being unusual or analytical at entry does not immunize a group against tribal dynamics later.

### 3. Does this differ from other ideologies?

The same pattern holds across the ideological spectrum—including socialism, social democracy, and traditional conservatism:
- **At the mass level**, most people who adopt a broad label ("capitalist," "socialist," "progressive," "conservative") do so largely through social identity, moral intuition, and coalition cues rather than rigorous independent derivation.
- **At the committed ideological or intellectual level**, adherents usually have reasoned through explicit premises (whether Hayekian price theory, Rawlsian justice, or Marxist critiques of capital), even if social reinforcement later helps maintain those beliefs.

And importantly, whether a person arrived at laissez-faire via careful economic reasoning or via partisan conformity tells you **nothing about whether laissez-faire claims are true or false**. Evaluating the merits of laissez-faire requires testing its economic premises and empirical track record directly, not diagnosing the psychology of its supporters.

### Conclusion

- **Probable conclusion:** If "people who support laissez-faire" means the broad bloc of voters who cheer for "free markets" as part of a partisan platform, **tribal and partisan identity is the primary driver for most of them**—just as it is for mass-public supporters of competing ideologies. If it means people who actually hold a consistent, strict laissez-faire position across issues, **explicit reasoning and ideological self-selection play a much larger role**, because there is no large, mainstream tribe that rewards consistent laissez-faire, though subcultural conformity can still set in afterward.
- **What would revise this assessment:** Representative survey data specifically isolating consistent laissez-faire supporters (rather than general conservatives or Republicans) and measuring their policy stability under shifting elite cues, compared directly against matched ideological groups on the left and right.
