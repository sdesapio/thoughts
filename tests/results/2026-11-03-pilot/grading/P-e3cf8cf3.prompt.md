Whether "socialism is the best system" depends heavily on what you mean by "socialism," because the historical and economic evidence diverges sharply depending on the definition:

**1. Command-economy socialism (state ownership of the means of production and central planning)**
The empirical track record here is overwhelmingly negative. 20th-century centrally planned economies (the USSR, Maoist China, Eastern Bloc states, Cuba, Venezuela) consistently struggled with:
- **The knowledge and calculation problem:** Central planners lack the dispersed, real-time information that market prices convey, leading to chronic shortages of basic goods, surpluses of unwanted goods, and misallocated capital.
- **Incentive failures and stagnation:** Without competitive markets and private property rights, productivity growth and innovation lagged far behind market-based peers (compare East vs. West Germany, or North vs. South Korea).
- **Political authoritarianism:** Concentrating all economic power in the state meant the state controlled employment, housing, and resources, removing counterweights to political power.

China and Vietnam only achieved rapid poverty reduction and growth *after* abandoning central planning in favor of market reforms, private enterprise, and global trade integration.

**2. Social democracy / mixed economies (often colloquially called "socialism" in modern politics)**
If by "socialism" you mean the Nordic model (Denmark, Sweden, Norway)—or broadly, a robust welfare state, universal healthcare, strong labor unions, and high redistribution—those countries are **not** socialist in the classical sense. They are **capitalist market economies** with strong property rights, free trade, ease of doing business, and mostly private ownership of firms, paired with high taxes and a generous social safety net.

And on the evidence, **mixed market economies**—combining competitive capitalist markets for wealth creation with state regulation, public goods, and social insurance to correct market failures and reduce poverty—have produced the highest sustained living standards, life expectancy, innovation, and political freedom in human history.

**Bottom line:**
Pure socialism (replacing markets and private enterprise with state ownership) has consistently failed to deliver broad prosperity or freedom. Pure laissez-faire capitalism leaves major market failures (monopolies, externalities, extreme poverty, lack of public goods) unaddressed.
