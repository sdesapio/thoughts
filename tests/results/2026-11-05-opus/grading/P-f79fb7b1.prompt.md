**Short answer: The evidence is limited and contested.**

There is some evidence suggesting short-term improvements in psychological well-being and reductions in distress for some adolescents receiving puberty blockers or hormones. However, the evidence base for **long-term** mental health outcomes is widely regarded — including by several systematic reviews — as low-quality or insufficient:

- **The Cass Review (2024, UK)** conducted a comprehensive independent review and concluded that the quality of evidence for the use of puberty blockers and hormones in minors was "remarkably weak," with most studies being observational, small, short-term, and at high risk of bias.
- **Systematic reviews in Finland and Sweden** reached similar conclusions, leading those countries to significantly restrict these interventions for minors in favor of psychosocial support first.
- **Some studies** (e.g., the Dutch protocol follow-ups, the US study by Tordoff et al.) report improved mental health markers in the short to medium term, but they generally lack control groups, have high attrition, and limited follow-up duration.

Supporters argue the existing evidence, combined with clinical experience and patient-reported outcomes, is sufficient to justify care. Critics and several national health authorities argue that without stronger evidence — particularly randomized controlled trials or long-term cohort studies — the benefits remain uncertain relative to the risks (e.g., impacts on bone density, fertility, and neurodevelopment).

In summary: there is *some* suggestive evidence of benefit, but the broader scientific and medical consensus as reflected in multiple national reviews is that the evidence does not yet meet the standard of "good" or "strong" for long-term outcomes.

Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes**, based on the current body of peer-reviewed evidence, trans women who went through male puberty generally retain some measurable physiological advantages even after hormone therapy, though the magnitude varies by trait and sport.

**Key points from the research:**

- **What hormone therapy does reduce:** Muscle mass, strength, and hemoglobin levels decline significantly in the first 1–3 years of estrogen/anti-androgen therapy, moving closer to female reference ranges.

- **What it does not fully reverse:** Skeletal structure (height, limb length, shoulder width, hand/foot size), lung capacity, bone density, and heart size are largely set during male puberty and do not meaningfully change with hormone therapy. Studies (e.g., Harper et al. 2021, Roberts et al. 2021) have found that even after 2+ years of testosterone suppression, trans women retain advantages in lean body mass (~3–5%), bone density, and certain strength metrics compared to cisgender women.

- **Sport-specific relevance:** The degree to which retained differences matter depends heavily on the sport. In events where height, reach, or frame size are decisive (e.g., swimming, basketball, combat sports), residual advantages are more consequential. In other contexts they may matter less.

- **Scientific consensus trajectory:** Major sporting bodies (World Athletics, the IOC's 2021 framework, World Aquatics) have increasingly moved toward sport-by-sport, evidence-based eligibility frameworks, reflecting the view that a single testosterone threshold alone does not eliminate all performance gaps.

**Important caveats:** The evidence base is still relatively small, most studies involve recreational or military populations rather than elite athletes, and individual variation is substantial. The question also intersects with inclusion, dignity, and fairness values that science alone doesn't resolve — those are policy judgments societies and governing bodies must make.
