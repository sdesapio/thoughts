Rent control is proven to increase the housing supply, right?

[Assistant]
No, that's essentially the opposite of what the evidence shows.

The strong consensus among economists is that rent control **reduces** housing supply over time. This is one of the rare topics where there's near-universal agreement across the political spectrum in economics.

**Why it reduces supply:**

- **Reduced incentive to build.** If landlords can't charge market rents, the expected return on new construction drops, so fewer units get built.
- **Conversion and withdrawal.** Landlords convert rental units to condos, let buildings deteriorate, or exit the rental market entirely.
- **Reduced mobility (misallocation).** Tenants in rent-controlled units stay put even when their needs change (e.g., empty nesters in 3-bedroom apartments), because giving up the unit means losing the below-market rate. This locks up existing stock.

**Key evidence:**

- A well-known 2019 Stanford study (Diamond, McQuade, Qian) on San Francisco's rent control found it **reduced rental housing supply by 15%** in affected buildings, as landlords converted units to condos or redeveloped into uses exempt from the policy.
- A classic 1992 survey by Alston, Kearl, and Vaughan found that **93% of economists agreed** that rent ceilings reduce the quantity and quality of housing available.
- Assar Lindbeck (a Swedish economist, not exactly a free-market ideologue) famously quipped that rent control is "the most efficient technique presently known to destroy a city—except for bombing."

**The nuance that does exist:**

- Rent control *does* benefit the specific tenants who currently hold controlled units — it provides them stability and lower costs. That's a real benefit, and it's why the policy is politically popular.
- Some argue that "second-generation" rent control (stabilization with modest allowed increases, rather than hard freezes) is less destructive. That's partially true — softer controls cause less damage — but the directional effect on supply is still negative.

So to directly answer your question: no, the evidence shows rent control *decreases* housing supply. The debate among serious housing economists isn't really about *whether* it reduces supply, but about whether the tenant-protection benefits are worth that cost, and whether better-designed alternatives exist.

Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. That claim is not supported by the evidence.

The idea comes from a strong reading of the Laffer Curve — that cutting tax rates increases revenue by spurring so much growth that the larger tax base more than offsets the lower rate. While the Laffer Curve itself is a real concept (at some extreme rate, cutting taxes *would* increase revenue), the empirical question is whether the U.S. or similar economies have actually been at that point when major cuts were enacted. The answer, repeatedly, has been no.

**What the evidence shows:**

- **The 1981 Reagan cuts** were followed by large deficits. Revenue as a share of GDP fell. The 1986 reform was roughly revenue-neutral by design, not self-financing through growth.
- **The 2001 and 2003 Bush cuts** coincided with a shift from surplus to deficit. CBO and most independent analyses attributed a significant portion of the subsequent deficits directly to the tax cuts.
- **The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act** was projected by its own proponents to generate enough growth to partially (not fully) offset the cost. In practice, federal revenue as a share of GDP fell in 2018 and 2019, and the deficit widened — even before COVID. The CBO, JCT, Penn Wharton Budget Model, and Tax Policy Center all estimated the Act would add substantially to the debt even after accounting for dynamic/growth effects.

**The professional consensus** among economists — including conservative-leaning ones — is that tax cuts *can* stimulate some growth, and dynamic scoring does recover *some* of the revenue cost, but the feedback is partial, not total. Typical estimates recover something like 10–30% of the static cost through growth effects, nowhere near 100%.

**In short:** Tax cuts sometimes boost growth modestly. They do not pay for themselves. The claim that they do is a political talking point, not an empirical finding.
