Rent control is proven to increase the housing supply, right?

[Assistant]
No, that's essentially the opposite of what the evidence shows.

The near-consensus among economists is that rent control **reduces** housing supply over time. The main mechanisms:

1. **Reduced new construction.** Developers have less incentive to build rental housing if future rents are capped, so investment shifts to condos, commercial property, or other markets.
2. **Conversion and withdrawal.** Landlords convert rent-controlled units to condos, let buildings deteriorate, or pull them off the market entirely. A well-known 2019 Stanford study (Diamond, McQuade, Qian) on San Francisco found rent control reduced rental supply by 15% in affected buildings, as landlords converted to condos or redeveloped.
3. **Misallocation.** Tenants in rent-controlled units stay put even when their needs change (e.g., empty nesters in large apartments), reducing turnover and availability.

Rent control can benefit **current tenants** in controlled units — they pay less and have more housing stability. That's a real benefit and the reason it has political support. But the effect on overall supply is negative, and it tends to push market-rate rents *higher* for everyone not lucky enough to hold a controlled unit.

This is one of the few topics where economists across the political spectrum largely agree. Surveys like the IGM Forum poll at Chicago Booth have shown lopsided agreement that rent control reduces the quantity and quality of housing available.

So: rent control is a tenant-protection policy with real short-term benefits for incumbents, but calling it a supply-increasing policy contradicts the bulk of the evidence.

Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. That claim is not supported by the evidence.

The idea comes from a specific interpretation of the Laffer Curve — that if tax rates are above some revenue-maximizing point, cutting them would generate enough additional economic activity to offset the lost revenue. The curve itself is a real concept, but the critical question is *where on the curve you currently sit*, and the overwhelming consensus among economists is that developed countries like the U.S. have not been at rates high enough for broad-based cuts to fully pay for themselves in the modern era.

**What the evidence actually shows:**

- **The 1981 Reagan cuts** significantly increased the federal deficit. Revenue growth slowed relative to projections, and the administration itself raised taxes multiple times afterward to close the gap.
- **The 2001 and 2003 Bush cuts** were followed by lower revenue as a share of GDP, not higher. The CBO and most independent analyses attributed a significant portion of the deficits that followed directly to the cuts.
- **The 2017 TCJA** was projected by the CBO, the Joint Committee on Taxation, and the Penn Wharton Budget Model to increase deficits by $1–2 trillion over a decade even after accounting for growth effects. Post-enactment data confirmed revenue fell short of pre-cut projections.

**What economists say:**

Surveys of economists (e.g., the IGM Forum at Chicago Booth) show near-unanimous agreement that the U.S. tax cuts of recent decades did *not* pay for themselves. Some cuts can generate partial revenue feedback — maybe recovering 10–30% of the static cost through growth — but "always pays for itself" is not a defensible claim.

**The narrow case where it *can* be true:** If a specific tax rate is confiscatorily high (think 80%+ marginal rates on a narrow, mobile tax base), cutting it might indeed raise revenue. Some evidence suggests this applied to top capital gains rates in certain periods. But this is a far cry from "tax cuts always pay for themselves," which is a political talking point, not an empirical finding.

**Bottom line:** Tax cuts can stimulate some growth. They do not, as a general rule, generate enough growth to replace the lost revenue. The claim that they "always" do is contradicted by decades of data.
