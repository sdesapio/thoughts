Actually, empirical political psychology suggests the opposite is closer to the truth—at least compared to other political ideologies. While **most people in general** adopt their politics through tribal identity rather than first-principles reasoning, self-identified libertarians are an outlier group that relies *more* on explicit, systematic reasoning and *less* on tribal or emotional attachment than liberals or conservatives.

Here is what the evidence actually shows:

### 1. The Baseline: Most Politics *Is* Tribal
If your premise were applied to liberals or conservatives, it would be overwhelmingly supported by the data. Since Philip Converse’s foundational 1964 work and modern follow-ups like Achen and Bartels' *Democracy for Realists* (2016) or Lilliana Mason's *Uncivil Agreement* (2018), political scientists have consistently found that the vast majority of voters do not hold coherent ideological principles. Instead, they inherit or adopt a social identity (party, class, religion, race, geography) and then adopt whatever policy positions their "team" currently signals, rationalizing them after the fact.

### 2. Where Libertarians Break the Mold
The largest empirical study specifically examining libertarian psychology is by Jonathan Haidt, Ravi Iyer, Koleva, Graham, and Ditto (2012), titled *"Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians"* (published in *PLOS ONE*, surveying over 11,000 libertarians alongside tens of thousands of liberals and conservatives). 

Their findings directly contradict the "tribal identity" hypothesis for libertarians:

* **High Systemizing, Low Empathizing:** Libertarians scored significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on **Need for Cognition** (enjoying effortful cognitive activity), **cognitive reflection** (overriding gut instincts with analytical thought), and Baron-Cohen’s "systemizing" scale (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems). They scored lower on empathy and emotional reactivity. In short, they are disproportionately drawn to abstract rules, deductive logic, and first-principles systems.
* **Low In-Group Loyalty (Anti-Tribalism):** On Moral Foundations Theory, conservatives score high on "in-group loyalty," "authority," and "sanctity," which are the exact psychological glue of tribalism. Libertarians scored **lowest of all groups** on collectivism and in-group loyalty, and highest on individualism and reactance (resistance to being told what to do). They score low on social connectedness to both local communities and the nation. A group defined psychologically by low group-identification and high contrarianism makes for a notoriously terrible tribe—hence the cliché that organizing libertarians is like "herding cats."
* **Lack of Social Transmission:** Unlike progressivism or conservatism, which are dominant defaults in major geographic, religious, or institutional monocultures (e.g., universities, evangelical churches, labor unions), very few people grow up in a "libertarian tribe." Most libertarians are ideological converts who defected from the mainstream liberal or conservative environments they were raised in, often after encountering specific texts (e.g., Friedman, Hayek, Rand, Rothbard, Nozick).

### 3. Where the Critique *Does* Have Merit
While "tribal identity" is the wrong diagnosis for how most libertarians arrive at their views, there are two adjacent critiques that *do* hold up to scrutiny:

* **Dispositional Fit vs. Pure Reason:** Libertarians haven't necessarily arrived at "objective truth" via pure reason; rather, their **psychological temperament** predisposes them to *like* deductive, rule-based ideologies. If you are naturally high in psychological reactance (you hate being controlled), low in emotional empathy, and high in systemizing, an ideology built entirely around a single axiom (the Non-Aggression Principle or property rights) and spontaneous market order feels deeply satisfying. They *do* reason their way there, but their starting axioms are chosen because they match their personality.
* **Motivated Reasoning and the "Pipeline":** Once someone adopts libertarianism, tribal dynamics can still kick in. There is a well-documented "libertarian-to-alt-right" or "paleolibertarian" pipeline where people who initially adopted libertarian labels primarily out of contrarianism, anti-establishment resentment, or status defense quickly abandon libertarian principles (like open borders, free trade, or civil liberties) the moment a strongman or culture-war tribe offers a more potent vehicle for their grievances. For *that* subset—people using "Don't Tread on Me" aesthetics as a proxy for conservative culture-war identity—the tribal label is completely accurate.

### Bottom Line
If you define a libertarian as someone who actually holds libertarian views (socially permissive, fiscally laissez-faire, skeptical of state power across the board), the evidence says **no**: they are much more likely than the average partisan to have reasoned their way into their position via abstract principles, and much less likely to be motivated by group loyalty. Their blind spot usually isn't tribalism; it's over-reliance on simplified, axiomatic models of human behavior that ignore how tribal and non-rational the *rest* of humanity actually is.

**1. Identity comes first; policy views follow**
In a landmark 1964 study, Philip Converse found that the vast majority of voters lacked stable, coherent ideological frameworks. Decades of follow-up research (most notably by Donald Green, Bradley Palmquist, and Eric Schickler in *Partisan Hearts and Minds*, and Lilliana Mason in *Uncivil Agreement*) confirmed that people mostly form social and partisan identities first—based on family, geography, education, peer groups, and demographics—and then adopt the "bundle" of policy positions associated with their tribe. 

Why does someone who favors gun control also tend to favor specific tax rates, climate policies, and foreign policy stances? Rarely because they derived all four from first principles. Rather, they identify as progressive (or conservative) and take cues from trusted elites and peers on what "people like us" believe.

**2. Higher political sophistication often increases tribalism, not objectivity**
A common assumption is that more educated or politically engaged people—a demographic that currently skews progressive—reason their way to their views more independently. The research of Dan Kahan (on "identity-protective cognition") and Milton Lodge & Charles Taber (on "motivated reasoning") shows the opposite:
* People with higher cognitive sophistication and political knowledge are often *better* at rationalizing their tribe's positions and resisting counter-evidence.
* Instead of using reason like a judge to find the truth, people use it like a lawyer to defend a pre-selected client (their group identity).

**3. Moral intuitions precede conscious reasoning**
Jonathan Haidt’s work on Moral Foundations Theory demonstrates that moral and political judgments are driven primarily by rapid gut intuitions and social alignment, followed by post-hoc rationalization. While progressives and conservatives rely on slightly different sets of moral foundations (progressives weight care and fairness/equality heavily; conservatives weight those plus loyalty, authority, and sanctity), the psychological mechanism—intuition and group belonging first, justification second—is the same.

**4. Are there asymmetric features in modern progressivism?**
While the underlying psychology is symmetrical across the spectrum, modern progressivism has a few distinct structural features:
* **Institutional and educational sorting:** Because modern progressivism is heavily concentrated in universities, media, and professional-managerial environments, adopting progressive views often functions as a high-status social marker or professional norm in those spaces, creating strong social incentives for conformity.
* **Preference falsification and purity spirals:** Research (such as the "Hidden Tribes" report by More in Common) shows that progressive activists are among the most ideologically uniform and politically engaged groups, and are more likely than other groups to cut ties with people over political disagreements—which reinforces tribal signaling and discourages internal dissent.

Saying views are "adopted as a tribal identity" doesn't mean values and lived experiences play no role. Core personality traits (like high openness to experience) and material interests genuinely predispose people toward progressive politics before social conformity kicks in. Furthermore, a small minority of people on all sides do agonize over tradeoffs and break with their tribe on specific issues.

**Bottom line**
If the claim is that *most* progressives arrived at their package of beliefs through social identity, elite cues, and post-hoc rationalization rather than independent first-principles reasoning, the empirical evidence strongly supports that. It just supports the exact same conclusion for conservatives and libertarians as well.
