[Assistant]
No. As stated, it's not well-supported, and it fails a basic consistency check: the same claim, with equal plausibility and on the same evidence base, could be made about almost any political identity — progressive, conservative, nationalist, etc. If you'd reject "most progressives haven't reasoned their way there; it's tribal identity" as an unsupported generalization, you need a reason to treat libertarians differently, and the claim as given doesn't supply one.

Breaking it into its two parts:

**"Most political belief isn't arrived at through careful individual reasoning."** This general claim has real support. Classic political psychology (Converse's work on belief systems in mass publics, and more recent work like Achen & Bartels's *Democracy for Realists*) shows that for most people, political affiliation is substantially downstream of group identity, socialization, and social belonging, with policy reasoning often reconstructed afterward to fit the identity rather than driving it. But this applies across the ideological spectrum, not specifically to libertarians. So if this is the intended claim, it's true but not a distinctive fact about libertarians — the "most people" and the mechanism described hold for conservatives and progressives too.

**"Libertarians specifically adopted it as tribal identity rather than reasoning."** This is the stronger, subject-specific claim, and the available evidence cuts somewhat against it rather than for it. The best relevant data point is Iyer, Koleva, Graham, Iyer & Haidt's 2012 study "Understanding Libertarian Morality," which found self-identified libertarians score *higher* than liberals or conservatives on need for cognition (self-reported preference for reasoning over emotion/intuition) and *lower* on moral-foundations measures tied to group loyalty and in-group favoritism — the opposite of what you'd expect if they were a distinctively tribal, non-reasoning group. That's correlational self-report data, not proof libertarians reason correctly or that any given individual arrived there logically, but it's evidence against the specific "more tribal than others" framing.

There's also a structural reason to doubt the tribal-transmission story for libertarians specifically: unlike Democrat/Republican identity, which correlates strongly with family, religion, region, and race — the classic markers of tribal inheritance — libertarian identification in the US doesn't have strong intergenerational or community transmission. People usually arrive at it individually, via specific exposure (Rand, Hayek, Mises, economics coursework, certain online subcultures), which is at least a different causal pathway than inherited tribal identity, even if it isn't rigorous first-principles reasoning either.

That points to a false dichotomy in the question itself: "reasoned their way there" and "adopted as tribal identity" aren't mutually exclusive. Someone can be exposed to a one-sided set of arguments, find them persuasive for reasons including temperament (e.g., individualism, contrarianism, distrust of authority), and then have the resulting view function as an identity marker afterward. That's a real phenomenon, but it's not what "tribal identity, not reasoning" usually implies — it implies no real argumentative content was involved, which doesn't match the typical path into libertarianism as well as it might match inherited party identity.

**Bottom line:** the general point about most political belief being identity-driven rather than individually reasoned has genuine support. The specific claim that libertarians are distinctively or predominantly tribal rather than reasoned isn't supported by the evidence I can point to, and some of it points the other way. I'd also flag that "most people haven't reasoned their way there" is nearly unfalsifiable as stated — there's no clean measurement of the median person's belief-formation process — so treat it as a plausible-sounding generalization rather than an established fact, for libertarians or anyone else.

Short answer: no, not as stated — and the framing itself is the main problem.

**The dichotomy is false.** "Reasoned their way there" vs. "adopted as tribal identity" aren't mutually exclusive, let alone jointly exhaustive. For any complex set of political positions, almost nobody derives their views from first principles alone. Belief formation for virtually everyone mixes socialization, trust in sources/communities, personal experience, and some reasoning — with identity shaping *which* arguments feel persuasive (motivated reasoning), not necessarily replacing reasoning altogether. So "haven't reasoned their way there, adopted as tribal identity" smuggles in an either/or that doesn't match how belief formation actually works for anyone, progressive or otherwise.

**The claim singles out progressives without a stated reason to.** Apply the same lens to any other group: "Most people who hold conservative/libertarian/religious views haven't reasoned their way there — they adopted them as tribal identity." That version is at least as defensible on the same logic. The political-psychology literature on this (motivated reasoning, cultural cognition, partisan social identity — e.g., work associated with Dan Kahan, Lilliana Mason, Achen & Bartels) generally finds identity-driven political cognition to be a broad human feature across the ideological spectrum, not a progressive-specific pathology. If you want to claim progressives are *more* identity-driven than other groups, that's a distinct, stronger empirical claim, and I'm not aware of solid evidence establishing that asymmetry — I'd flag it as unsupported rather than false, since I haven't verified it against current data here.

**On the "most" claim itself:** This is an empirical claim about an internal cognitive/causal process (why people hold the views they hold) across a huge, heterogeneous population. I don't have a way to verify a specific quantified claim like "most" from here — no search was done, and the question is genuinely hard to measure even in principle (self-reports about one's own reasoning process are unreliable, and "reasoning" vs. "identity" shade into each other). So the honest position is: plausible-sounding, not demonstrated, and probably more applicable to political cognition generally than to progressives specifically.
