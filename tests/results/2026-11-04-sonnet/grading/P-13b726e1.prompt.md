Most people who hold progressive political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
Short answer: the general mechanism you're describing is real and well-documented, but singling out progressives as the group this especially applies to isn't supported — the evidence points to this being roughly symmetric across the political spectrum.

**Breaking the claim into parts:**

1. An empirical claim about *how* people arrive at political views (not via explicit reasoning).
2. An empirical claim about *what* views function as (tribal/social identity markers).
3. An implicit comparative claim: that this is distinctively or disproportionately true of progressives.

**What's well supported:** Political psychology has substantial evidence for (1) and (2) as a description of *most people, across ideology*. Philip Converse's classic finding (1964) was that most of the mass public shows little "ideological constraint" — people don't derive positions from a coherent reasoned framework. Achen and Bartels' *Democracy for Realists* argues most voters adopt policy positions *because of* group/partisan identity and then rationalize them, not the reverse. Haidt's social-intuitionist model (*The Righteous Mind*) holds that moral/political intuitions come first and reasoning serves as post-hoc justification — for liberals and conservatives alike. Dan Kahan's "cultural cognition" research finds identity-protective reasoning operating symmetrically: people on the left motivated-reason about guns or GMOs, people on the right motivated-reason about climate or evolution, each defending their group's position rather than evaluating evidence neutrally.

**Where the claim fails:** Applying the equal-standards check — would "most conservatives haven't reasoned their way to their views, they adopted them as tribal identity" be equally supportable with the same evidence? Yes, about equally. Nothing in the literature establishes that progressives specifically are more tribal or less reasoned than other ideological groups. There is a contested academic debate (e.g., Jost's system-justification work suggesting conservatives have higher needs for cognitive closure) about asymmetries, but it cuts in various directions depending on the study and measure, and Kahan's symmetric findings are at least as well-supported. So the mechanism is real; the asymmetric attribution to one side is an unsupported extra step, not something the evidence backs.

**One more thing worth flagging:** even granting the premise fully — that most progressives (like most people generally) absorbed their views socially rather than deriving them from argument — that alone wouldn't tell you whether the views are true or false. How a belief was acquired and whether it's correct are separate questions; treating the former as settling the latter is a genetic-fallacy move worth watching for.

**Bottom line:** As a claim about political cognition in general (most people's politics is socially/identity-driven more than reasoned), it's well-supported and would be worth stating that way. As a claim that singles out progressives specifically, it's not supported by the comparative evidence and should be revised or dropped unless you have evidence of an actual left/right asymmetry that the general literature doesn't show.

What would change this: a study specifically comparing degree of "reasoned derivation vs. identity adoption" across ideological groups, controlling for education/engagement, showing progressives score reliably lower than comparable conservative or other-ideology samples.

Short answer: as stated, this doesn't hold up — not because tribal belief-formation isn't real, but because the claim singles out libertarians without a reason to think they're special, and it sets up a false choice between "reasoned" and "tribal."

**The empirical core is unsupported as asserted.** "Most people who hold libertarian views haven't reasoned their way there" is a quantified claim about how a population actually formed its beliefs — that's a psychology/survey question, and I don't have data in front of me that measures libertarians' reasoning process specifically and finds it deficient relative to some baseline. Saying "most" without that evidence is an assertion, not a finding.

**The dichotomy is false.** "Reasoned their way there" vs. "adopted as tribal identity" aren't exhaustive or even mutually exclusive. Most political belief formation is some mix of upbringing, social milieu, intuition, self-interest, and reasoning — including reasoning that runs backward from a gut commitment to justify it (motivated reasoning). "Not a pure independent derivation from first principles" isn't the same as "no reasoning involved," and the claim as worded collapses that distinction.

**Equal-standards check: swap the subject.** Run the same claim on progressives, conservatives, or socialists: "most people who hold [X] views haven't reasoned their way there, they adopted them as tribal identity." That version is at least as plausible, and it's backed by actual political-psychology work — Kahan's identity-protective cognition, Lodge & Taber's motivated-reasoning model, Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists*, Haidt's intuitionist model — none of which singles out libertarians. If the general mechanism (social identity shapes political belief more than people realize, then reasoning rationalizes it) applies broadly across ideologies, picking out libertarians specifically needs a stated, relevant difference. None has been offered here.

**Is there anything distinctive about libertarians that would supply that difference?** There's a plausible observation worth separating from the unsupported claim: libertarian identification correlates less with parental party ID than mainstream D/R identification does (it's rarer to inherit from family), which cuts *against* the classic inherited-tribe story — but it's consistent with a newer tribal vector: subcultural/online identity (certain forums, memes, authors cited reflexively, aesthetic markers like the Gadsden flag or "taxation is theft"). That's a real phenomenon, but it's anecdotal as I'm stating it here, and the same subcultural-identity dynamic shows up in other ideological communities too — so it doesn't establish that libertarians are unusually tribal, just that they're not immune to a pattern that seems close to universal in politics.

**Where this leaves the claim:**
- "Most political belief, across ideologies, is substantially shaped by social identity rather than independent step-by-step reasoning" — plausible and has real support, but it's a claim about politics generally, not libertarians specifically.
- "Libertarians in particular are disproportionately tribal rather than reasoned" — unsupported; no relevant difference from other groups has been shown, and the question as posed doesn't supply the quantitative evidence ("most") it would need.

So: probably true in spirit if you strip the "libertarians specifically" framing and the hard reasoning/tribal binary; not established as a claim singling out libertarians. What would change this: actual comparative survey or experimental data showing libertarians arrive at their views with less engagement with argument/evidence than comparable ideological groups, controlling for the fact that *every* ideology's adherents mostly report "I thought it through" regardless of mechanism.
