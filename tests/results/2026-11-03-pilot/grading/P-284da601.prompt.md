Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Empirically, for the vast majority of people who support laissez-faire capitalism—just like the vast majority of people who support any other broad political or economic ideology—it is primarily driven by **tribal identity, elite cues, and moral intuitions**, with explicit reasoning mostly serving as post-hoc rationalization. However, there is a small, distinct subset of doctrinaire libertarians and economists for whom systematic reasoning plays a much larger role.

Here is what the evidence from political science and moral psychology shows:

### 1. The General Public: Identity and Elite Cues First
Decades of public opinion research (starting with Philip Converse and John Zaller, and reinforced by modern researchers like Lilliana Mason and Donald Kinder) show that most citizens do not hold coherent, deductively reasoned ideological frameworks:
* **Partisan Cues:** Most self-described supporters of "free-market capitalism" in the general public adopt the label because it is a core marker of their political coalition (e.g., mainstream conservatism in the US). When party elites shift their positions—such as the rapid shift among Republican voters from supporting free trade to supporting tariffs and industrial policy over the last decade—most voters follow the tribal cue rather than sticking to laissez-faire principles.
* **Inconsistent Policy Preferences:** In surveys, large majorities of voters who endorse "free enterprise" and "small government" in the abstract simultaneously support massive government interventions when asked about specific policies (e.g., Medicare, Social Security, price caps, tariffs, or agricultural subsidies), provided those policies benefit their perceived in-group.
* **System Justification & Self-Interest:** For others, support is driven by status-quo bias, cultural individualism, or straightforward material self-interest (e.g., high earners or business owners opposing taxes and regulations), which is then dressed up in philosophical language.

### 2. The Ideological Exception: Doctrinaire Libertarians
If you narrow the focus from "mainstream voters who praise the free market" to **committed, philosophical advocates of laissez-faire** (libertarians, classical liberals, Objectivists, Austrian/Chicago school adherents), the picture changes significantly:
* **High Cognitive Systematizing:** Research by moral psychologists (notably Jonathan Haidt, Ravi Iyer, and Matt Motyl) specifically studying libertarians found that they score significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on **need for cognition**, **systematizing** (enjoying rule-based systems), and **analytical vs. intuitive thinking**.
* **Deductive Consistency:** Unlike mainstream partisans, ideological libertarians actually do tend to reason deductively from a few core axioms (such as self-ownership, the non-aggression principle, or price-theory efficiency) and follow them to counter-intuitive or socially unpopular conclusions—such as legalizing all drugs, open borders, abolishing corporate welfare, or opposing military intervention.

### 3. Even Among the Reasoners, Intuition Sets the Starting Point
Even for that small, highly analytical group, they rarely "reason their way there" from a blank slate:
* **Psychological Predispositions:** Iyer and Haidt’s research showed that libertarians have a distinct psychological profile: they score much lower on collectivist moral foundations (loyalty, authority, sanctity) and lower on empathetic concern/care than liberals, while scoring overwhelmingly high on **liberty/autonomy**.
* **Axiom Selection vs. Deduction:** You cannot reason your way into valuing individual liberty or economic efficiency above egalitarian outcomes or communal solidarity; those are foundational value judgments. People with high individualist temperaments gravitate toward laissez-faire philosophy because its starting axioms match their gut intuitions, and they then use their high analytical capacity to build an internally consistent worldview around those axioms.

### Bottom Line
If by "most people" you mean **everyday voters** who champion the free market, it is overwhelmingly a **tribal identity and cultural signal**, easily abandoned when their political tribe shifts or when a government program benefits them. If you mean **hardcore laissez-faire ideologues**, they genuinely do engage in much more explicit, deductive reasoning than average voters—but that reasoning is still built atop an underlying temperament that prizes autonomy and system-building over collective care or tradition.

Based on empirical political science, the direct answer is **both—and that is true of virtually every mass political ideology, not uniquely democratic socialism.** 

 Decades of political psychology and public opinion research show that very few citizens of *any* political persuasion arrive at their views by sitting down and reasoning from abstract philosophical principles. Instead, people arrive at ideologies through a mix of **material self-interest, core value alignment, and social/tribal sorting.**

Here is what the evidence actually shows about how people come to support democratic socialism:

### 1. Most Voters Don't Do "Grand Theory" (Converse's Law)
Beginning with Philip Converse’s foundational 1964 research (*The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*) and confirmed repeatedly since (e.g., Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists*), political scientists have found that only a small fraction of the public—roughly 10% to 15% of highly politically engaged people—hold tightly reasoned, textbook ideological systems. 

For the average person who tells a pollster they view "democratic socialism" favorably, they have rarely read Karl Kautsky, Rosa Luxemburg, or Michael Harrington, nor have they worked out the mechanics of worker-owned means of production versus Nordic social democracy.

### 2. "Reasoning" Mostly Happens via Material Grievances and Concrete Policies
To the extent that supporters *reason* their way to the label, it is usually pragmatic and bottom-up rather than theoretical:
* **Material conditions:** Downward mobility, student debt, housing costs, and healthcare expenses lead many—especially younger cohorts—to conclude that the current economic system isn't working for them.
* **Semantic shift:** Polling (such as Pew and Gallup) consistently shows that when most Americans say they support "democratic socialism," what they actually mean is **social democracy**—specifically, universal healthcare, tuition-free public college, stronger labor unions, and a Nordic-style welfare state. They reason: *"I want universal healthcare; opponents call that socialism; Bernie Sanders calls it democratic socialism; therefore, I'm a democratic socialist."*

### 3. Tribal Identity and Expressive Politics Play a Huge Role
As Lilliana Mason (*Uncivil Agreement*) and other researchers on "expressive partisanship" have documented, modern politics functions heavily as social identity. For many supporters, "democratic socialist" operates as a tribal marker:
* **In-group signaling:** Among younger, urban, college-educated, or online cohorts, the label signals moral values, solidarity, and belonging to a specific social milieu.
* **Differentiation from the establishment:** Often, the primary tribal boundary isn't against conservatives, but against mainstream establishment liberals. Adopting the "democratic socialist" label is a way to signal that one is more committed, morally serious, or anti-establishment than a standard Democrat.
* **Elite cues:** People adopt the frameworks of the leaders and media ecosystems they trust. When charismatic figures or peer networks normalize a label, members of that group adopt the label first and backfill the justifications later (a process psychologists call *motivated reasoning*).

### The Bottom Line
If by "reason their way there" you mean *systematically evaluating economic models and concluding that social ownership of capital is superior to regulated capitalism*, only a tiny, highly ideological minority of supporters do that. 

Most supporters arrive there through a combination of **concrete material frustration** (wanting healthcare, affordability, and a stronger safety net) and **tribal sorting** (adopting the label worn by their peers and preferred political figures to signal opposition to the status quo). In this respect, they behave almost identically to self-described "free-market conservatives" or "libertarians," most of whom also haven't read Hayek or Friedman, but use the label to express a mix of concrete grievances, basic values, and social identity.
