Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes, in most sports—particularly those relying on strength, power, speed, and VO2 max—transgender women who went through male puberty generally retain a meaningful athletic advantage over cisgender women, even after testosterone suppression, though the degree varies by sport and the evidence is still evolving.

Key points behind this:

**What the evidence shows:**
- Male puberty produces durable physical changes: greater height, longer limbs, larger hearts and lungs, higher bone density, greater muscle mass, and a different fat distribution. These structural changes don't fully reverse with hormone therapy.
- Studies (e.g., Hilton & Lundberg, 2021; Harper et al.) have found that after 1-3 years of testosterone suppression, trans women typically retain significant advantages in muscle mass, strength, and grip strength compared to cisgender women—often losing only a fraction of their original advantage.
- Performance-based analyses (e.g., of competitive running times) show that even "slower" male-bodied athletes often still outperform elite female athletes in events like running, swimming, and weightlifting.

**Where it's more contested or variable:**
- The magnitude of retained advantage differs by sport. Sports relying heavily on raw strength/power (weightlifting, throwing events, boxing) tend to show larger retained advantages than sports with more technical/skill components.
- Duration of suppression matters somewhat—longer suppression reduces (but doesn't eliminate) strength and muscle mass advantages.
- Starting age of transition matters a lot: someone who transitions before puberty doesn't go through male puberty at all, which is a very different scenario than post-pubertal transition.
- Sample sizes in direct studies are small, since elite trans athletes are rare, so much of the data is inferential (based on physiological markers rather than head-to-head competitive outcomes).

**Current policy landscape:**
This evidence is why many international sports federations (World Athletics, FINA/World Aquatics, British Cycling, rugby bodies) have moved toward excluding trans women who transitioned after male puberty from female elite categories, while others (IOC's 2021 framework) left it to individual sport federations to decide, reflecting the lack of full scientific consensus on exact policy thresholds even though the directional finding (retained advantage exists) is fairly well supported.

The genuinely contested question isn't really "is there an advantage" so much as "how much is enough to disqualify," "how long does suppression need to be," and "how should sports balance inclusion versus competitive fairness"—those are value judgments on top of the physiological evidence, not purely scientific ones.
