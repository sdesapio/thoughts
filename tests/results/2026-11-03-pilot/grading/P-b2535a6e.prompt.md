Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. Under modern tax rates in developed economies, broad tax cuts almost never pay for themselves, and there is a strong consensus among economists across the political spectrum on this point.

Here is what the evidence shows:

### 1. The Laffer Curve is real, but we are on the wrong side of it
The theoretical basis for self-financing tax cuts is the **Laffer Curve**: if tax rates are near 100%, nobody has an incentive to work or invest, so cutting rates increases taxable economic activity enough to raise revenue. 

However, empirical research typically estimates the revenue-maximizing top income tax rate to be quite high—often between **60% and 75%** (depending on how easily high earners can shelter income or relocate). Because current U.S. and most OECD tax rates are well below that peak, cutting rates reduces total revenue.

### 2. Partial offset vs. full offset ("Dynamic Scoring")
Tax cuts can stimulate economic growth—by increasing short-term demand or improving long-term incentives to work and invest—which generates *some* new tax revenue. However, this growth only offsets a fraction of the direct revenue loss:
* **Nonpartisan estimates** from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT), and Penn Wharton Budget Model typically find that broad income or corporate tax cuts generate enough growth to offset **10% to 30%** of their static cost.
* The remaining **70% to 90%** adds directly to the fiscal deficit unless paired with spending cuts.
* Moreover, when tax cuts are deficit-financed, the resulting increase in government borrowing can push up interest rates and "crowd out" private investment, dampening the very growth the tax cuts were meant to create.

### 3. The Historical Record
Major U.S. tax cuts consistently resulted in lower federal revenues relative to the size of the economy (GDP) and baseline projections:
* **1981 (Reagan):** Top marginal rates were cut from 70% to 50% (and later 28%). Federal income tax revenue fell significantly as a share of GDP, prompting Congress and Reagan to pass several smaller tax increases between 1982 and 1987 to curb ballooning deficits.
* **2001 & 2003 (Bush):** Federal revenues dropped from 20.0% of GDP in 2000 to 15.6% in 2004, turning projected surpluses into persistent deficits even before the 2008 financial crisis.
* **2017 (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act):** Despite strong promises that corporate rate cuts (from 35% to 21%) would pay for themselves, the CBO and JCT estimated the law would add roughly $1.5 to $1.9 trillion to the deficit over ten years, even after accounting for macroeconomic growth. Subsequent revenue data confirmed a sharp drop in corporate tax receipts relative to GDP immediately following enactment.

### Are there any exceptions?
A tax cut can only pay for itself in narrow, specific circumstances:
* **Extreme marginal rates:** Cutting a punitive rate (e.g., the U.K.'s post-WWII top rates exceeding 90%, or cutting top capital gains rates when they are so high that investors simply refuse to sell assets—the "lock-in effect").
* **Targeted enforcement/evasion dynamics:** In developing economies with weak tax enforcement, lowering high statutory rates while broadening the base can sometimes pull informal ("black market") activity into the formal taxable economy.

For broad federal income, payroll, or corporate tax cuts in modern advanced economies, the math does not work out: they reduce government revenue and increase deficits.

No. The empirical consensus in economics is the opposite: rent control generally **reduces** the supply of rental housing.

Here is what the evidence shows:

* **Reduction of existing rental stock:** Landlords subject to rent control frequently pull units off the rental market by converting them into owner-occupied condominiums, selling them, or redeveloping them to qualify for exemptions. For example, a landmark Stanford study (Diamond, McQuade, and Qian, 2019) on San Francisco’s 1994 rent control expansion found that while it kept incumbent tenants in place, affected landlords reduced the supply of rental housing by **15%**, which ultimately pushed citywide market rents up by about 5.1%.
* **Chilling effect on new construction:** Even when laws exempt newly built apartments, developers and lenders often reduce investment out of fear that caps will eventually be expanded to cover newer buildings (regulatory risk), or because depressed rents in the broader market lower expected returns.
* **Reduced mobility and maintenance:** Because tenants in rent-controlled units stay much longer than they otherwise would, fewer units turn over and become available to new renters. Landlords also tend to invest less in maintaining and upgrading price-controlled properties.

**Nuance on modern policies:**
"Second-generation" rent control (often called rent stabilization)—which exempts new construction, allows rent resets between tenants (vacancy decontrol), and caps annual increases at inflation plus a fixed percentage (e.g., CPI + 5%)—is designed to minimize these supply losses. While mild stabilization acts more like an anti-gouging buffer and causes significantly less damage to new construction than strict caps, there is still no empirical evidence that it *increases* housing supply. 

Rent control functions as a tenant-protection and anti-displacement policy for current residents, not a supply-building policy.
