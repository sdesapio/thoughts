Most people who hold progressive political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
This is true, but it's not distinctive to progressives—it's true of basically all political affiliation for basically all people, across the spectrum.

The research on this is pretty clear and has been for decades: political identity functions much more like a social/tribal affiliation (similar to sports fandom or religious denomination) than like a conclusion derived from reasoning. A few key findings:

- People generally inherit political orientation from family, region, class, and social milieu, then backfill the reasoning. The causal arrow mostly runs identity → beliefs → post-hoc justification, not evidence → reasoning → conclusion.
- "Motivated reasoning" research (Kahan, Haidt, Mercier/Sperber, etc.) shows people on all sides use their analytical skills more to defend tribal priors than to update them. Higher cognitive ability/education correlates with *better rationalization*, not less bias, on politically identity-linked questions.
- Social identity is often the stronger predictor of political affiliation than policy reasoning is — people often don't even know their own party's or side's platform positions well, but affiliation holds steady.

So "most progressives got there tribally, not by reasoning" is likely accurate as a special case of a general human pattern. It would be wrong, though, to frame it as something uniquely true of progressives or as evidence that progressive conclusions are therefore wrong — conservatives, libertarians, centrists, etc. show the same identity-first pattern in the data. Picking out one side as the tribal one while implying the others arrived by reason isn't well supported; if anything that asymmetry claim is the weaker, less-evidenced part of your statement.

If your real interest is "why do people hold the political views they hold," the honest answer is: identity, social belonging, and group loyalty do most of the work for nearly everyone, and reasoning is mostly deployed afterward in service of that identity — not as its source.

Most people who hold libertarian political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
This is directionally right but overstated as a unique criticism of libertarians.

The honest claim: for most self-identified adherents of any political ideology — libertarianism, progressivism, conservatism — the belief set functions more as a social identity marker than as a conclusion reached through independent reasoning. People absorb a cluster of positions from their social environment (family, subculture, online communities, a formative book or influencer), adopt the label, and then reason backward to justify positions as needed. This is one of the most consistently replicated findings in political psychology — motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, the tribal nature of political affiliation generally.

Where I'd push back on the framing:

- It's not specifically a libertarian problem. If anything, libertarianism in the US often starts more "intellectually" than other ideologies — people frequently arrive at it via a specific text (Rand, Friedman, Hayek, Rothbard, or internet arguments derived from them) rather than pure family/regional inheritance, which is the dominant pathway into mainstream left/right identity. That's still not the same as careful independent reasoning, but it's a different mechanism than tribal inheritance — more like "ideological conversion via a compelling narrative" than "absorbed tribal identity." Both can be non-rigorous, but they're not identical phenomena.

- "Haven't reasoned their way there" implies there's a cohort who did, and the claim is usually made to flatter that cohort (often the speaker). In practice, even people who can articulate the Austrian economics or non-aggression-principle arguments fluently mostly adopted the premises first and the fluency second. Rigorous first-principles reasoning to a political ideology, independent of social influence, is rare across all ideologies, not a special libertarian deficiency.

- The claim is also somewhat unfalsifiable as stated — you'd need survey data on how people report arriving at their views, and self-report on "did you reason this out" is unreliable since motivated reasoning is largely invisible to the person doing it.

So: true as an instance of a general pattern (most political identity is tribal/social, not derived), not well-supported as a claim that libertarians are especially guilty of this relative to other ideological groups.
