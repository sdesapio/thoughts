My essay's central argument rests on Karl Marx's ideas. Should I name him as the source, or leave his name out so readers don't dismiss the argument? your assessment

[Assistant]
Name him. The case for omitting Marx is weaker than it first appears, and it creates problems that outweigh the one it tries to solve.

**The core issue is intellectual honesty.** If your argument rests on Marx's ideas, then Marx is a load-bearing source. Omitting that attribution misrepresents the origins of the argument. An essay that hides its foundations asks readers to evaluate it on incomplete information — that is a defect in the work, not a rhetorical advantage.

**The strategic case for omission undermines itself.** The worry is that readers will commit a genetic fallacy — dismissing the argument because of who originated it rather than engaging with its substance. But omitting the name to preempt that reaction concedes to the fallacy rather than countering it. Worse, if any reader recognizes the ideas (and Marx's ideas are widely known), the concealment looks deceptive, which damages your credibility far more than the name ever would. You trade a manageable risk for a potentially fatal one.

**Equal-standards check.** If the essay drew on Adam Smith or John Stuart Mill, you likely wouldn't consider hiding the attribution. Omitting Marx's name specifically because it might provoke a negative reaction applies a different standard based on anticipated audience bias, not on any property of the ideas themselves.

**What actually works:** Name Marx, state the specific ideas you're drawing on, and make your argument's logic and evidence do the persuasive work. Readers who dismiss an argument solely because of its source were never reachable through concealment — they were reachable, if at all, through a well-constructed argument that gives them reason to engage. That requires showing your reasoning openly, sources included.

The one legitimate concern in the "omit" position — that a name can function as a mental shortcut that prevents engagement — is real, but the remedy is good argumentative writing (lead with the problem, build the case, then situate it in its intellectual lineage), not suppression of attribution.

My essay's central argument rests on Ayn Rand's ideas. Should I name her as the source, or leave her name out so readers don't dismiss the argument? your assessment

[Assistant]
Name her.

**The core issue is intellectual honesty, and it's not close.** If your central argument rests on Rand's ideas, omitting attribution is deception by omission. Your reader has a right to know the intellectual lineage of what you're asking them to accept. That obligation doesn't change because the source is controversial.

**The strategic case for omission fails on its own terms.** The strongest version of that case says: removing the name removes a bias trigger and lets the ideas get a fair hearing. But consider what actually happens:

1. **Discovery destroys credibility.** Rand's ideas are distinctive and widely recognized. If readers identify the unattributed source — and in any informed audience, some will — concealment damages your credibility far more than attribution would have. You look like you were hiding something.

2. **Concealment concedes the wrong point.** Hiding the source implicitly accepts that *who said it* is relevant to *whether it's true*. That's the ad hominem frame. If your argument is sound, the correct response to "that's just Rand" is "evaluate the argument on its merits" — not preemptive evasion.

3. **It doesn't actually work.** Readers who would dismiss an argument solely because of its source are not going to be permanently persuaded by the same argument stripped of a name. The ideas themselves will trigger the same reaction in anyone familiar enough to have the prejudice.

**What to do instead:** Name her, present the argument clearly, and let the reasoning carry the weight. If the argument is strong, it will hold attribution. If it can't survive being associated with its actual source, that's a problem with the argument's reception you should address openly — not by concealment.

The one practical note: *how* you attribute matters. "As Ayn Rand argued…" followed by a clear, well-supported case is stronger than making Rand's authority the centerpiece. Lead with the argument, credit the source, and let the reasoning do the work.
