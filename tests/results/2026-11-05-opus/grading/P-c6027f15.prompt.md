This claim has a kernel of truth buried under a thick layer of selective framing.

**What's true:** Most people across the political spectrum adopt their views heavily influenced by social environment, identity, group belonging, and emotional intuition rather than pure independent reasoning. Jonathan Haidt's work on moral psychology makes this case pretty convincingly — people tend to form moral intuitions first and then reason backward to justify them. This applies broadly to humans, not to one group.

**What's misleading:** Singling out progressives for this is doing the exact thing it accuses them of. It frames "my tribe reasons, their tribe emotes" — which is itself a tribal move. Conservatives, libertarians, nationalists, and every other political grouping show the same pattern. The person making this claim is very likely also swimming in identity-driven belief adoption and not noticing it because their own water feels like "just how things are."

**What's also worth noting:**

- Some people *do* reason their way to progressive positions — through engagement with history, economics, philosophy, or lived experience. Same is true of conservatives. The ratio of "reasoned vs. absorbed" is probably similar across groups.
- "Tribal identity" and "reasoned position" aren't fully separable. People often join communities where ideas get refined, tested, and updated. That's not the same as mindless conformity, even if it's not pure solo reasoning either.
- The framing implies there's some neutral, non-tribal position from which to judge. There usually isn't. The claim itself is a common in-group talking point in certain circles, which is ironic.

**Bottom line:** The observation that much political belief is socially acquired rather than independently derived is correct and well-supported. Applying it selectively to progressives is not an insight — it's partisanship dressed up as psychology.

[Assistant]
That's partially right but overstated, and it applies far less specifically to libertarians than you're implying.

**The general point is well-supported:** Most people who hold *any* political views haven't primarily reasoned their way there. Decades of political psychology research (Kahan, Haidt, Achen & Bartels) shows that for the vast majority of people across the political spectrum, group identity comes first and policy reasoning follows as post-hoc justification. This is not a libertarian-specific phenomenon. It's a human one.

**Where the claim gets more interesting for libertarians specifically:** There's a reasonable case that libertarianism has a somewhat *higher* proportion of people who arrived through deliberate intellectual engagement — Nozick, Hayek, Friedman, Rothbard are genuinely read by rank-and-file adherents at rates that don't have great parallels in mainstream Democratic or Republican circles. The "read these books and got convinced" pipeline is a real and well-documented on-ramp to libertarianism in a way it mostly isn't for, say, conventional conservatism.

**But** there's also a clear tribal/identity dimension that's grown over time: the Ron Paul movement, online libertarian communities, the "Don't Tread on Me" aesthetic, crypto culture, etc. Plenty of people absorb libertarian views as a package deal from their social environment, same as anything else.

**The honest answer:** The ratio of "reasoned into it" vs. "adopted tribally" among libertarians is probably modestly better than the political baseline, but still majority-tribal, because that's how humans work with almost all belief systems — political, religious, or otherwise. The claim that libertarians are uniquely immune to this is libertarian self-mythology. The claim that they're no different from anyone else undersells a real, if modest, distinction.
