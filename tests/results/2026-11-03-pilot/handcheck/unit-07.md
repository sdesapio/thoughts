[Assistant]
No, tax cuts generally do not pay for themselves. The broad consensus among economists across the political spectrum is that most broad-based tax cuts reduce net government revenue and increase deficits, even after accounting for the economic growth they generate.

Here is how the mechanics work:

### 1. The Laffer Curve
The idea that tax cuts can pay for themselves is based on the **Laffer Curve**, which illustrates that at a 0% tax rate, the government collects no revenue, and at a 100% tax rate, it also collects little to no revenue because people lose the incentive to work or invest. 
- **On the right side of the peak** (where rates are extremely high, e.g., 70%–90%+), cutting rates can spur enough new economic activity and reduce tax avoidance enough to actually *increase* total revenue.
- **On the left side of the peak** (where modern tax rates in the U.S. and most developed nations generally sit), cutting tax rates stimulates some growth, but not enough to offset the direct loss of revenue from the lower rate.

### 2. Dynamic Scoring vs. Static Scoring
When organizations like the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) or the Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT) evaluate tax cuts using **dynamic scoring** (which accounts for macroeconomic feedback like increased work, investment, and GDP growth), they typically find that growth offsets only **10% to 30%** of the initial revenue loss—meaning 70% to 90% of the cut still adds to the deficit unless paired with spending cuts.

### 3. Historical Evidence in the U.S.
Major federal tax cuts over the past several decades—including the 1981 Reagan tax cuts, the 2001 and 2003 Bush tax cuts, and the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA)—all resulted in lower federal revenues as a share of GDP compared to pre-cut baselines, contributing to larger federal deficits.

### Exceptions where tax cuts *can* come closer to paying for themselves:
- **Cutting extreme marginal rates:** Lowering very high top marginal rates or punitive capital gains rates where tax avoidance is rampant.
- **Targeted investment incentives:** Cuts specifically aimed at business investment or R&D have a higher growth multiplier per dollar of lost revenue than broad consumption-oriented cuts.
- **Economic slack:** Temporary tax cuts during a recession can boost aggregate demand and bring idle workers and capital back into the economy faster.
