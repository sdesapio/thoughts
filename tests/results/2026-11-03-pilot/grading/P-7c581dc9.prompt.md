Yes, that is largely right—though the crucial caveat from empirical political science is that it isn’t a uniquely progressive phenomenon. It is the standard operating procedure for how almost all human beings form political beliefs across the ideological spectrum.

Here is what the evidence from political science and moral psychology shows:

### 1. Identity and Elite Cues Drive Policy Views (Not Vice Versa)
Decades of research—beginning with Philip Converse in the 1960s and expanded by scholars like Donald Kinder, Lilliana Mason, and Christopher Achen and Larry Bartels (*Democracy for Realists*)—show that the vast majority of voters do not have a coherent, first-principles ideological framework from which they deduce policy positions. 

Instead, the causal arrow usually runs in reverse:
1. People sort into social, cultural, educational, or geographic groups.
2. Political affiliation becomes fused with that social identity (what Mason calls **"mega-identities"**).
3. People then adopt the bundled "package" of views signaled by their group's elites, media, and peers to remain in good standing and differentiate themselves from the out-group.

For example, there is no strictly logical, first-principles deduction that requires someone who favors carbon taxes to also hold a specific view on Middle Eastern foreign policy, gun control, and gender theory—or, conversely, for someone who favors deregulation to also oppose abortion and climate treaties. Those positions cluster together because they are tribal markers, not because each individual reasoned through each issue independently.

### 2. Higher Education and Information Increase Tribal Rationalization
A common assumption among progressives (who today skew more heavily toward holding college and postgraduate degrees) is that higher education leads to more independent, reasoned political views. The empirical evidence shows almost the exact opposite.

Research by psychologist Dan Kahan on **identity-protective cognition** and political scientist Charles Taber on **motivated reasoning** demonstrates that people with higher political knowledge, higher education, and higher cognitive sophistication are *more* partisan and *more* likely to conform to their tribe's orthodoxy, not less. 
* Why? Because they are more attuned to elite cues (knowing what "someone like me" is supposed to believe on a new issue) and they use their cognitive skills like a lawyer defending a client rather than a scientist seeking truth.

### 3. Are There Any Asymmetries Specific to Modern Progressivism?
While tribal adoption applies to both left and right, modern progressivism has a few distinct structural features in how its tribalism operates:

* **Concentration in cultural and knowledge institutions:** Because progressive norms dominate universities, media, non-profits, and HR/corporate environments, adopting progressive views in those spaces often carries high social rewards—and dissenting carries high social or professional friction. This creates strong incentives for **preference falsification** and conformist adoption.
* **Rapidly shifting moral vocabulary:** Modern progressive norms around language and social issues often update very quickly. The speed at which large groups simultaneously adopt new terminology or flip on issues (e.g., shifting from "colorblindness" to anti-racist frameworks, or changing views on free speech and institutional trust) is a strong indicator of social contagion and in-group signaling rather than millions of individuals independently arriving at the same philosophical conclusion at the exact same time.
* **Purity spirals:** Because progressive identity is heavily moralized around care/harm and oppressor/oppressed frameworks, in-group status is often demonstrated by staking out the most morally stringent position, which accelerates conformity among peers who fear being labeled as aligned with the out-group.

### 4. Where the "Purely Tribal" Thesis Goes Too Far
To be accurate to the evidence, saying people didn't reason their way to their specific policy bundle doesn't mean their politics are *entirely* arbitrary:
* **Psychological and moral predispositions:** Work by Jonathan Haidt (*The Righteous Mind*) and twin/genetic studies show that underlying traits—like openness to experience, sensitivity to disgust/threat, and weighting of care/fairness vs. loyalty/authority—have heritable and temperamental roots. People with high openness and high empathy/harm-sensitivity naturally gravitate toward the left; people higher in orderliness and norm-preservation gravitate toward the right.
* **Material and social self-interest:** For some groups, aligning with a coalition reflects real material or status interests rather than blind conformity.

If your claim is that most progressives adopted their specific bundle of beliefs through socialization, peer signaling, and tribal identity rather than independent philosophical reasoning—**yes, the evidence strongly backs that up.**

The premise is mostly right about political beliefs in general, partly right about libertarians in practice, and actually gets the distinctive psychology of libertarians somewhat backwards compared to other groups.

Here is what the empirical literature in political science and moral psychology shows:

### 1. For almost everyone, identity comes before reasoning
If your baseline is the average voter, yes: people rarely reason their way to *any* ideology from first principles. Decades of political science (from Philip Converse in the 1960s to Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists* and Lilliana Mason's *Uncivil Agreement*) show that most people adopt political views through social sorting, family, class, and group identity, and then use reasoning mainly for post-hoc rationalization. In that broad sense, libertarians are humans doing what humans do.

### 2. Many self-described "libertarians" are just tribal conservatives
In the US, polling and voter studies consistently find a large gap between **philosophical libertarians** (socially liberal / anti-authoritarian, fiscally conservative) and **self-identified libertarians**. 
* Survey research (e.g., Pew, PRRI, and Voter Study Group data) shows that a large share of Americans who adopt the "libertarian" label, fly Gadsden flags, or use "small government" rhetoric actually hold standard right-wing populist or socially conservative views on immigration, policing, nationalism, and cultural issues.
* For this group, "libertarian" functions primarily as an aesthetic or tribal identity—a cooler, more independent-sounding brand of conservatism—rather than a worked-out commitment to the non-aggression principle or consistent anti-statism.

### 3. However, consistent libertarians are the *least* tribal and *most* systemizing group in politics
Where your premise breaks down is when you look at people who actually hold consistent libertarian policy views (pro-market *and* pro-civil liberties/socially permissive).

The landmark empirical study on libertarian psychology is Iyer, Koleva, Graham, Ditto, and Haidt (2012), *"Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians"* (published in *PLOS ONE*, $N > 15,000$). Their findings directly contradict the idea that libertarians are primarily driven by tribalism or unreflective emotion:

* **Higher cognitive reflection and need for cognition:** Libertarians scored higher than both liberals and conservatives on the Cognitive Reflection Test (a measure of overriding gut intuition with analytical thought) and on "Need for Cognition" (enjoying abstract, effortful thinking).
* **Systemizing over empathizing:** Using Baron-Cohen's framework, libertarians scored unusually high on *systemizing* (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems) and lowest on *empathizing*. They are disproportionately drawn to deductive, axiomatic reasoning—which explains why figures like Ayn Rand, Murray Rothbard, and Milton Friedman appeal to them.
* **Exceptionally low tribalism / group loyalty:** On Moral Foundations questionnaires, libertarians scored very low on the "binding" foundations—**Ingroup Loyalty**, **Authority**, and **Purity**—and low on collectivism and social identification across the board. Psychologically, they are weak joiners with low attachment to community or tribe.

### 4. The catch: Deductive reasoning is still driven by underlying disposition
Does high analytical reasoning mean libertarians "reasoned their way there" objectively? Not quite. Haidt's broader model (*The Righteous Mind*) still applies: **intuition comes first, strategic reasoning second.**

Consistent libertarians usually start with a distinct psychological temperament: a high psychological reactance (visceral dislike of being told what to do), low emotional empathy, high individualism, and a preference for tidy, rule-based systems. Because they have a high need for cognition, they don't just stop at a vibe—they build or adopt elaborate deductive frameworks (natural rights, Austrian economics, public choice theory) to justify that temperament. They *do* reason a lot, and they care about logical consistency much more than the average partisan, but the axioms they start from are chosen because they fit their personality.

### Summary
* **If you mean the broader "Don't Tread on Me" / Tea Party / right-populist crowd:** Yes, that is largely a tribal conservative identity wearing libertarian aesthetics.
* **If you mean actual ideological libertarians:** No. Compared to liberals and conservatives, they are *less* tribal, *more* socially atomized, and *more* likely to have arrived at their views through explicit, rule-based, deductive reasoning—even if that reasoning is ultimately scaffolding for an underlying hyper-individualist temperament.
