### 1. Where the claim is right: Identity-First Politics
A massive body of political science research (such as the work of Lilliana Mason in *Uncivil Agreement*, Christopher Achen and Larry Bartels in *Democracy for Realists*, and Dan Kahan on *identity-protective cognition*) shows that for most people, **social identity precedes policy views.**

* **Cue-taking:** Most citizens do not have the time or specialized knowledge to independently research complex macroeconomic or sociological policy. Instead, they rely on heuristics—looking to trusted elites, media, and peer groups ("their tribe") to see what position aligns with their group's values.
* **Motivated reasoning:** As Jonathan Haidt argues in *The Righteous Mind*, human intuition comes first, and strategic reasoning comes second. Smarter or more educated partisans aren't necessarily more objective; they are often just better at acting as "lawyers" for conclusions their social group already holds.

### 2. Why this isn't unique to progressives
While the observation is largely true of many progressives, framing it as a specifically progressive phenomenon is inaccurate:
* **Conservatives and other groups do the exact same thing:** Studies consistently show that conservatives, moderates, and libertarians rely just as heavily on tribal cues, in-group loyalty, and elite signaling. For instance, public opinion among partisan groups on both sides frequently flips rapidly on issues (like free trade, foreign intervention, or trust in specific institutions) depending on which party holds the presidency.

When any worldview has near-monopoly status in a social environment, social conformity, status-signaling, and fear of ostracism become much more noticeable drivers of behavior.

Reducing progressive (or conservative) views *entirely* to mindless tribalism overlooks genuine psychological and philosophical roots:

* **Intellectual traditions:** Just like conservatism or libertarianism, progressivism has a deep history of serious political philosophy (from John Stuart Mill and John Dewey to John Rawls). While the average voter hasn't read Rawls, many people have genuinely reflected on concepts of systemic inequality, market failures, and human rights to arrive at their worldview.

People gravitate toward a political tribe because of their core temperament, values, and environment, and once inside that tribe, social identity does much of the heavy lifting in shaping their specific policy opinions.

Here is a breakdown of what political science and psychology suggest about how people arrive at libertarian views.

### 1. Where the premise is right: Tribalism is the human default
In political science, the idea that people mostly adopt beliefs through group identity rather than independent reasoning is well-supported across the entire political spectrum (e.g., Achen and Bartels’ *Democracy for Realists*). 
* **Socialization and Subcultures:** Many people arrive at libertarianism through cultural milieu—such as tech and crypto communities, certain business networks, gun-rights groups, or anti-establishment internet subcultures—where skepticism of the state is the group norm.
* **Post-hoc Rationalization:** Psychologist Jonathan Haidt argues in *The Righteous Mind* that human beings generally start with gut intuitions and social identities, and then use explicit reasoning mainly as a "press secretary" to justify what they already feel or what their tribe believes. Libertarians are no immune to this; slogans like "taxation is theft" or "don't tread on me" often function as tribal shibboleths rather than conclusions reached after reading Hayek or Nozick.
* **"Conservatives who like weed" / Aesthetic Libertarianism:** Survey data (such as Pew Research studies on self-identified libertarians) has repeatedly shown that a significant portion of Americans who call themselves "libertarian" do not actually hold consistent libertarian policy views, or even know the textbook definition of the term. For many, it serves as an identity badge signaling independence, contrarianism, or dissatisfaction with the two major parties, while their actual voting behavior remains solidly partisan (usually Republican).

### 2. Where the premise misses the mark: Libertarians are disproportionately "systemizers"
Ironically, if you compare self-identified libertarians to liberals and conservatives, there is empirical evidence that libertarians rely *more* on abstract reasoning and *less* on tribal identity than other groups.

In a major 2012 study by Iyer, Koleva, Graham, Ditto, and Haidt (*"Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians"*), researchers studied over 11,000 libertarians and found distinct psychological traits:
* **High Need for Cognition and Systemizing:** Libertarians scored significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on cognitive reflection tests, need for cognition (enjoying abstract, effortful thinking), and Baron-Cohen’s "systemizing" scale (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems), while scoring lower on empathizing.
* **Weak In-Group Loyalty:** On Moral Foundations Theory tests, conservatives score high on "loyalty/in-group" and "authority" foundations; liberals score high on "care" and "fairness." Libertarians scored very low on almost all moral foundations—especially in-group loyalty and community attachment—except for one: **liberty/autonomy**.
* **Explicit Conversion via Arguments:** Because libertarianism is not a dominant mainstream culture in most families, fewer people are simply "born into" it compared to liberalism or conservatism. Many libertarians genuinely do arrive at the position by encountering an abstract axiom (like the Non-Aggression Principle) or an economic argument (like spontaneous order or public choice theory) and following it deductively—sometimes to rigid or counterintuitive conclusions precisely *because* they are prioritizing abstract rules over social norms.

### Summary
It helps to distinguish between two groups:
1. **Cultural/Identity Libertarians:** People who adopt the label and aesthetics of individual ruggedness as part of a broader cultural tribe (often right-populist or anti-institution), without much interest in philosophical consistency. For this group, your premise is largely right.
2. **Ideological/Philosophical Libertarians:** People drawn to formal, rule-based systems who genuinely *did* reason their way into the ideology from first principles. If anything, the common critique of this second group isn't that they didn't reason their way there, but that they **over-rely** on deductive, axiomatic reasoning at the expense of messy human and social realities.
