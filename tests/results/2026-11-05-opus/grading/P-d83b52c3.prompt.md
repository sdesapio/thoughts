Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes.** The balance of evidence supports that trans women who underwent male puberty retain meaningful athletic advantages over cisgender women in most sports, even after prolonged testosterone suppression.

## The reasoning

**What male puberty builds.** Testosterone-driven puberty produces structural and physiological changes that are well-established in exercise physiology: greater height and limb length, broader shoulders, larger hands and feet, higher bone density, a larger heart and greater lung capacity, more muscle mass and strength, and higher hemoglobin. These differences are the reason sport is sex-separated in the first place.

**What hormone therapy reverses — and what it does not.**

- *Reversed or substantially reduced:* Hemoglobin falls to female-typical range within months. Muscle mass and strength decline significantly, though studies consistently find they do not reach female-typical levels even after 2–3+ years of suppression (Roberts et al. 2021 found a ~9% running-speed advantage persisted after two years, down from ~21% pre-transition).
- *Not reversed:* Skeletal dimensions — height, limb length, shoulder width, hand size, foot size — are permanent once established. Heart and lung size do not shrink. These structural advantages persist indefinitely.

**Why the residual matters at elite level.** Elite competition operates on thin margins, often under 1%. A retained structural advantage in frame, reach, cardiovascular capacity, or residual strength — even after substantial muscle-mass loss — is large relative to the margins that separate medalists from non-finalists. The skeletal advantages alone, which are permanent and not seriously disputed, are sufficient to establish a meaningful competitive difference in most events.

**Strongest counterargument and its limits.** The best objection is that the evidence base is small, drawn mostly from military and recreational populations rather than elite athletes, and that individual variation is large. These points are real but affect the *precision* of the magnitude estimate, not the *direction*. The permanence of skeletal and cardiopulmonary changes is established developmental biology, not a contested empirical finding. The "individual variation" argument also conflates overlap in population distributions with competitive outcomes at the extreme tail, where sport is decided.

**Institutional response tracks this conclusion.** World Athletics (2023) and World Aquatics (2022) excluded trans women who went through male puberty from elite female competition, citing retained physiological advantages. The IOC's 2021 framework moved away from a universal testosterone threshold and deferred to individual federations, implicitly acknowledging that suppression alone does not resolve the question.

**Calibration.** High confidence in the direction: advantages are retained. Moderate-to-high confidence they are competitively meaningful at elite level. The magnitude varies by sport — events depending heavily on skeletal leverage, reach, or cardiovascular output show larger retained advantages than those less dependent on these traits. More research on elite trans women athletes specifically would sharpen the estimate, but the structural permanence of puberty-driven changes makes it unlikely that additional data would reverse the conclusion.

**Revision conditions.** Evidence that prolonged hormone therapy reverses skeletal dimensions, heart size, or lung capacity — or that these structural features do not translate to elite performance advantages — would require reassessment. No such evidence currently exists.

Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No.** There is not good evidence by standard evidence-based medicine criteria that gender-affirming medical care for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes.

## What the evidence base actually shows

Multiple independent national systematic reviews — the most rigorous method for assessing a body of research — have reached the same conclusion about evidence quality:

- **NICE systematic reviews (UK, 2020):** Rated the evidence for both puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones in minors as **very low certainty** (the lowest GRADE rating).
- **Finnish COHERE (2020):** Found insufficient evidence of effectiveness; Finland subsequently restricted hormonal treatment, prioritizing psychotherapy.
- **Swedish SBU (2022):** Found the evidence base insufficient and of low quality; Sweden restricted hormonal treatment for minors to research settings.
- **The Cass Review with University of York systematic reviews (UK, 2024):** Found low to very low quality evidence across the board.

These are not advocacy groups. They are national health authorities applying the same evidence-grading standards used for any other medical intervention.

## Why the evidence is weak

The recurring problems across the literature are:

1. **Almost no controlled studies.** Most are single-arm observational designs — patients receive treatment and are measured over time, with no comparison group receiving an alternative or no treatment. This makes it impossible to attribute changes to the intervention rather than to time, concurrent psychosocial support, or natural development.
2. **Short follow-up.** "Long-term" is the specific weakness. Most studies measure outcomes at 1–2 years. Very few follow patients beyond that. The question of what happens at 5, 10, or 20 years is largely unanswered.
3. **Small samples and high attrition.** The foundational Dutch studies followed 55 patients. Many subsequent studies lose significant portions of their cohort to follow-up, introducing survivorship bias.
4. **Selection effects.** Patients who remain in studies and continue treatment may systematically differ from those who discontinue or are lost to follow-up.

## What this does and does not mean

**This is a claim about evidence quality, not a claim that the treatments are harmful or ineffective.** "Insufficient evidence of benefit" and "evidence of no benefit" are different statements. The treatments may well help — the research designs used so far are simply not strong enough to establish that for long-term outcomes.

There *are* short-term observational signals: several studies show reductions in depression and anxiety scores at 12–24 months. The direction of effect across imperfect studies is generally positive. Major medical organizations (AAP, Endocrine Society, WPATH) endorse these treatments based on clinical judgment and the available evidence. But applying equal standards — the same evidence-grading criteria used for any other pediatric medical intervention — these signals do not constitute good evidence for long-term outcomes.
