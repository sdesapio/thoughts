Most people who hold progressive political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
There's real evidence behind part of this, but the claim as stated overshoots and is applied asymmetrically in a way that doesn't survive scrutiny.

What's true: political scientists (Converse's work on "belief systems," more recent work by folks like Achen and Bartels in *Democracy for Realists*) have shown pretty convincingly that most voters across the spectrum don't hold coherent, independently-reasoned ideological positions. People often pick a party/tribe first (based on identity, social group, upbringing) and then adopt the policy positions associated with that group, rather than reasoning from principles to conclusions. Political identity functions a lot like a social identity — sports team, religion, subculture. This is a robust finding in political psychology.

Where your claim overshoots: this dynamic is not unique to progressives. The same research applies at least as strongly to conservatives, moderates, libertarians, and every other political identity group. There's no good evidence that progressives in particular are more tribal or less reasoned than people holding other political views. If anything, some studies (e.g., on motivated reasoning, Kahan's work on cultural cognition) find the effect is roughly symmetric across left and right, with each side showing strong motivated reasoning on facts that are inconvenient to their tribe (climate science skepticism on the right, exaggerated risk perception on things like GMOs or nuclear power on parts of the left, as examples).

So the honest version of the claim is: "Most people who hold political views of any kind, progressive or otherwise, arrived at them substantially through social/tribal identification rather than independent reasoning." That's well-supported. The version singling out progressives specifically isn't — it reads more like a claim motivated by criticizing one group than one built from the evidence, since the identical argument structure could be and often is pointed at the right with equal plausibility.

If you have a specific reason to think progressivism is more tribal than other ideologies (e.g., a particular study), I'd genuinely want to see it — but I'm not aware of one, and the base-rate research suggests this is a general feature of political cognition, not a progressive-specific pathology.

Most people who hold libertarian political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
This mostly applies to political identity in general, libertarianism isn't especially unusual in this regard — but there's a specific, defensible version of the claim that holds up.

**Where you're right:** For most self-identified adherents of any sufficiently developed political ideology (libertarian, socialist, conservative, progressive), the modal path is socialization and identity-signaling, not independent derivation from first principles. People pick up a cluster of views from their social environment, a formative book or figure, or reaction against something they disliked (often: restrictive parents, a bad experience with bureaucracy, being online in certain internet subcultures), and then backfill justification. This is well-supported by political psychology — motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, the fact that policy positions cluster far more tightly than you'd expect if people were reasoning issue-by-issue from principles. Most people couldn't actually derive their position on, say, occupational licensing from their stated axioms if you pressed them; they're pattern-matching to "what my side believes."

**Where "libertarian" might be a special case rather than just an instance of the general pattern:**

- Libertarianism in the US is numerically small and not reinforced by the same density of institutions (churches, unions, major parties, ethnic/regional identity) that sustain tribal attachment to liberalism or conservatism. That cuts against the "tribal identity" framing somewhat — it's a weaker tribe with fewer social rewards for belonging, so sorting into it for pure identity reasons is a less efficient explanation than it is for, say, partisan red/blue identity.
- At the same time, libertarianism does have an unusually strong "I read it and had a conversion experience" entry pattern (Rand, Hayek, Mises, Friedman, or just certain online communities/subreddits), and that story people tell themselves as "I reasoned my way here" often functions exactly like a conversion narrative — a single text or figure validated a pre-existing disposition (often a dislike of being told what to do, or a prior tech/engineering disposition toward systems thinking) rather than being the product of weighing evidence against alternatives.
- There's also real selection: people with certain temperaments (high in reactance, lower in need for social conformity, often technical/STEM-adjacent) are overrepresented, which again looks like "found a tribe that matches a preexisting disposition" rather than "reasoned from premises."

**Net assessment:** I'd phrase it as: most libertarians, like most adherents of any ideology, hold their specific bundle of positions for reasons that are substantially social/dispositional rather than the product of independent reasoning from axioms to conclusions across the whole platform — even when they have a genuine "I read a book and it clicked" story, because that click is itself explained by prior disposition, not argument quality. But I wouldn't frame this as uniquely true of libertarians, or as evidence the views are wrong — the genetic fallacy point matters here: how someone arrived at a belief doesn't tell you whether the belief is correct. It's a claim about sociology of belief formation, true to varying degrees of essentially every political identity, not a specific indictment of libertarianism's merits.
