Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
No — the evidence is weak.

What exists is mostly observational: small sample sizes, short follow-up, high dropout, no randomized controlled trials, and most studies lack long-term (years-to-decades) outcome data on suicidality, depression, or overall wellbeing specifically attributable to puberty blockers or hormones rather than concurrent psychosocial support. Several of the more rigorous systematic reviews commissioned by health authorities (notably the UK's Cass Review, plus reviews in Sweden, Finland, and Norway) concluded that the evidence base for mental health benefit is of low or very low certainty, and that it does not reliably support confident claims of long-term psychological improvement. This is why several European countries have scaled back and restricted these interventions for minors in recent years, shifting toward psychotherapy-first approaches in most cases.

On the other side, some guideline bodies (e.g., WPATH, American Academy of Pediatrics) and some observational studies (e.g., certain cohorts from the Netherlands "Dutch protocol" and some U.S. studies) report reduced depression/anxiety or improved psychosocial functioning in the short-to-medium term. But these studies are frequently criticized for methodological weaknesses: no control groups, selection bias (patients who continue treatment vs. those lost to follow-up), conflation of social transition with medical intervention, and short observation windows.

So the honest summary: there's some evidence of short-term psychosocial benefit in select cohorts, but no solid, high-certainty evidence of improved long-term mental health outcomes from puberty blockers or hormones in minors. The field itself — including proponents — largely acknowledges the evidence is low-quality and more rigorous long-term research is needed.

Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes.

The weight of evidence from exercise physiology supports that male puberty confers durable athletic advantages that are not fully erased by testosterone suppression:

- **Height, limb length, bone structure**: fixed by puberty, unaffected by hormone therapy.
- **Muscle mass and fiber type**: studies (e.g., Hilton & Lundberg 2021) show that after 1–3 years of testosterone suppression, trans women retain significantly more lean mass, muscle cross-sectional area, and strength than cisgender female comparators — typically losing only a fraction of the initial gap.
- **Lung volume, heart size, hemoglobin advantages from male puberty**: some of these (e.g., hemoglobin/VO2 max-related) shrink with suppressed testosterone, but structural ones (lung and heart size relative to body size) persist.
- **Performance gaps**: the male-female performance gap in most sports is large (often 10-12% in running, more in power/throwing events) — far larger than the margins separating elite competitors. Even a partially reduced advantage is often still decisive at the elite level.

This is why most sport-governing bodies that have reviewed the physiological literature in depth (World Athletics, British Cycling, World Aquatics, World Rugby) have moved toward requiring transition before male puberty for female-category eligibility, rather than relying on testosterone suppression alone — a policy shift driven by the evidence, not political pressure in the opposite direction.

The genuine scientific debate is about *magnitude* (how much advantage remains, how fast certain components decay, how this varies by sport) and about trade-offs with inclusion, not about whether a meaningful residual advantage exists at all. Claims that testosterone suppression fully equalizes performance are not well supported by the current data.
