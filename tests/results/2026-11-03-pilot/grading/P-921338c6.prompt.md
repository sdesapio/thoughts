Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. Broad tax cuts almost never pay for themselves, and there is a strong consensus across the ideological spectrum of economists on this point.

### Why they don't pay for themselves
While tax cuts can stimulate economic growth by increasing consumer demand and improving incentives to work and invest, the resulting new tax revenue only offsets a fraction of the initial revenue loss. 

For a tax cut to be self-financing, an economy must be on the far side of the **Laffer Curve**—where tax rates are so punitively high (typically estimated by economists to be well above 60%–70%) that cutting them sparks a massive surge in taxable activity or a sharp drop in tax evasion. Modern developed economies, including the United States, operate on the left side of the Laffer Curve, where cutting rates reduces total revenue.

### What the evidence shows
Major U.S. tax cuts—including the Reagan cuts (1981), the Bush cuts (2001/2003), and the Trump Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017)—all significantly reduced federal revenue relative to baseline projections and increased the deficit.

Nonpartisan scoring agencies and researchers consistently find that **dynamic scoring** (accounting for the economic growth generated by the cuts) recovers only a portion of the lost revenue:
* **Individual income tax cuts** typically recoup roughly **5% to 20%** of their static revenue cost through growth.
* **Corporate rate cuts** (which target investment more directly) tend to have larger growth feedback effects, recouping roughly **20% to 35%** of their cost over a decade—still far short of 100%.

### The rare exceptions
A tax cut can occasionally increase revenue in narrow, specific cases:
* **Cutting punitively high top marginal rates** (e.g., when top rates were 70%+ and drove income into tax shelters, though even then the effect was mostly shifting how income was reported rather than pure GDP growth).
* **Targeted taxes on highly elastic behavior**, such as certain capital gains realignments or local excise taxes where consumers can easily cross borders to avoid the tax.

For broad federal income or corporate tax cuts at modern rates, however, the empirical record is clear: they reduce government revenue and must be financed either through spending cuts or increased borrowing.

### What the Evidence Shows

* **Conversion and withdrawal of existing units:** Landlords subject to rent caps often convert rental units into owner-occupied condominiums, redevelop buildings to be exempt from the law, or move into the units themselves. For example, a landmark 2019 Stanford study (Diamond, McQuade, and Qian) on San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion found that while rent control protected incumbent tenants from displacement, it reduced the supply of rental housing by **15%** in affected buildings as landlords converted units to condos or redeveloped them—which ultimately drove citywide market rents up by about 5.1%.
* **Reduced new construction (if not exempted):** Strict "first-generation" rent controls that cap rents on all units discourage developers from building new rental housing by lowering expected returns.
* **Second-generation exemptions:** Because the negative impact on construction is so well-established, most modern ("second-generation") rent control laws—sometimes called rent stabilization—explicitly exempt newly constructed buildings (or exempt them for 15–30 years) specifically to avoid choking off new supply. Even with new construction exempted, studies generally find a net decrease in total rental stock due to condo conversions of older, controlled buildings, along with reduced maintenance and mobility.

### What Rent Control *Does* Do
The evidence shows rent control functions as an **anti-displacement and insurance policy for current tenants** in controlled units, giving them lower rents and greater stability. However, it comes at the cost of a smaller, more expensive rental market for future renters and newcomers.
