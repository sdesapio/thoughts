No — that claim is false, and most economic evidence points the opposite direction.

**Decompose the claim**

"Proven to increase supply" contains two load-bearing parts: an empirical claim (effect on housing supply) and an implicit certainty claim ("proven"). Both need checking.

**What the evidence shows**

The most-cited natural experiment is Diamond, McQuillan, and Moretti's study of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion (American Economic Review, 2019). They found rent control reduced the rental housing supply in affected buildings by about 15%, as landlords converted units to condos, redeveloped, or otherwise withdrew them from the rental market — even though rent control did help existing tenants stay housed longer.

This result is consistent with the broader empirical and theoretical literature:

- A 2012 IGM Forum survey of top economists found overwhelming disagreement with the claim that rent control has had a positive effect on housing availability/affordability over the past three decades — essentially zero economists agreed.
- Standard microeconomic theory predicts the same direction: a binding price ceiling below market-clearing price reduces the quantity supplied (less new construction, more withdrawal of existing units via conversion, abandonment, or owner-occupancy) while increasing quantity demanded — producing a shortage, not a surplus.
- Other studies (e.g., on Cambridge, MA rent control ending in 1995) find the reverse effect — removing rent control increased investment and housing quality/supply responsiveness in affected buildings.

**Strongest counterargument, addressed**

The honest complication: rent control can sometimes preserve *existing* affordable units from being lost to gentrification-driven redevelopment in the short run, and some argue well-designed, narrowly targeted versions (e.g., exemptions for new construction) cause less supply damage than blanket rent control. That's a real nuance about *magnitude and mitigation*, not a case that rent control *increases* supply. No study I'm aware of finds net positive supply effects from binding rent control.

**Verdict**

The claim as stated is false. The near-consensus empirical and theoretical finding is that rent control reduces housing supply (through disinvestment, conversion, and reduced new construction), not increases it. **What would change this:** a methodologically sound study showing binding rent control (not just targeted exemptions-plus-incentives) led to a net increase in rental units in the affected market, accounting for substitution effects (conversions, new construction incentives elsewhere). Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No — this is false as a general claim, though worth separating what's true from what isn't.

**Decompose the claim.** "Pay for themselves" means the resulting revenue gain from growth effects equals or exceeds the static revenue loss from the lower rate, so total tax revenue doesn't fall. "Always" makes this a universal empirical claim, which only needs one clear counterexample to fail.

**The mechanism that's real.** Tax cuts can increase economic activity (more work, investment, reduced avoidance), which claws back some revenue — this is the basis of dynamic scoring and isn't controversial. The Laffer curve correctly shows that at a sufficiently high tax rate, a cut can increase revenue, because the rate is far enough past the revenue-maximizing point.

**Why "always" fails.** Whether a cut pays for itself depends on where the starting rate sits relative to the revenue-maximizing point, and most real-world top rates in recent US history have been estimated well below that point. The evidentiary record is fairly direct here: the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act and the 2001/2003 Bush cuts were both followed by revenue below pre-cut projections (CBO and Treasury analyses), and most empirical/dynamic-scoring estimates (including from CBO, JCT, and economists across the political spectrum, e.g., Greg Mankiw's own textbook treatment) put feedback effects at recovering some fraction of lost revenue — commonly cited ranges are roughly 10–30% — not 100%+. There are narrow, high-rate historical cases (some analyses of pre-1980 top marginal rates above ~70%) where self-financing is more plausible, but that's a different starting condition than most modern cuts.

**Verdict:** "Tax cuts always pay for themselves" is false as a universal rule. "Tax cuts partially offset their cost through growth, and in some cases involving very high starting rates could fully pay for themselves" is the supported, narrower claim.

**What would change this:** If a specific cut starts from a confiscatory rate near or above the revenue-maximizing point, self-financing becomes plausible and would need evaluating on its own facts rather than the general claim.
