Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. The universal claim — that tax cuts *always* pay for themselves through growth — is false, and with near-certain confidence.

## The valid kernel

The Laffer Curve is a real theoretical observation: at a 0% rate revenue is zero, at a 100% rate revenue is also effectively zero, so somewhere in between is a revenue-maximizing rate. Cuts from *above* that peak could in principle increase revenue. Tax cuts can also stimulate real economic activity, and that activity does generate some revenue feedback.

None of that supports the word "always."

## Why the universal claim fails

**Empirical record.** Every major U.S. tax cut in recent decades has been scored — including dynamic growth effects — as a net revenue loser by the CBO and the Joint Committee on Taxation. Growth feedback typically offsets roughly 10–30% of the static revenue cost, not 100%:

- **1981 Reagan cuts:** Revenue as a share of GDP fell; deficits rose sharply through the 1980s.
- **2001/2003 Bush cuts:** Budget moved from surplus to deficit. CBO attributed a substantial portion of the subsequent fiscal deterioration to the cuts.
- **2017 TCJA:** Projected to add ~$1.5 trillion in deficits over ten years *after* growth effects. Post-enactment revenue data tracked the projections, not the self-financing claim.

**The theory doesn't say what the claim needs it to say.** The Laffer Curve tells you a revenue-maximizing rate exists. It does not tell you current rates are above it. Most empirical estimates place the revenue-maximizing top income-tax rate well above recent U.S. levels — commonly in the 60–70% range. Cuts from rates already below the peak lose revenue, period.

**Magnitude mismatch.** Even generous supply-side estimates of growth effects (0.1–0.5 percentage points of additional GDP growth) produce far less additional revenue than the static cost of large rate reductions measured in hundreds of billions of dollars.

## What a defensible version looks like

*Tax cuts can stimulate growth, and that growth partially offsets their revenue cost. In specific circumstances — very high initial rates, taxes with large distortionary effects — the offset can be substantial. It does not typically reach 100%, and no credible evidence supports that it always does.*

## Conditions for revision

This conclusion would change if credible evidence showed that, across a range of tax types and starting rates, dynamic revenue consistently equaled or exceeded static revenue loss — or if the revenue-maximizing rate were demonstrated to be well below current U.S. rates across major tax categories. Neither condition currently holds.
