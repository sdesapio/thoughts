My essay's central argument rests on Karl Marx's ideas. Should I name him as the source, or leave his name out so readers don't dismiss the argument? your assessment

[Assistant]
Name him. A few reasons:

1. **Intellectual honesty demands it.** If your argument is built on Marx's ideas, omitting his name is a form of misattribution — you'd be presenting someone else's framework as your own or as unattributed common knowledge. That's a problem regardless of the audience.

2. **Informed readers will recognize it anyway.** Anyone familiar with the ideas will spot the influence, and the omission will look evasive rather than strategic. That undermines your credibility more than naming him would.

3. **It actually strengthens your argument.** Citing your source lets you engage with the ideas seriously — acknowledge them, build on them, address counterarguments. "Marx argued X; I extend this by showing Y" is far more persuasive than an unmoored assertion of Y.

4. **You can preempt dismissal.** If you're worried about knee-jerk reactions, address it head-on: frame which specific concepts you're drawing on, distinguish your use from interpretations readers might object to, and make clear you're engaging with the analytical framework rather than endorsing an entire political program (if that's the case).

Hiding the source to avoid bias is itself a bias — it assumes readers can't evaluate ideas on their merits. Trust your argument and your audience. If the ideas can't survive being attributed, the problem is with the argument, not the name.

[Assistant]
Name her. A few reasons:

1. **Intellectual honesty demands it.** If your argument rests on her ideas, omitting attribution is a form of plagiarism — or at best, misleading your reader about the origins of your reasoning. Academic and professional writing both require it.

2. **Hiding the source doesn't make the argument stronger.** If someone can recognize the ideas as Rand's without you naming her, you lose credibility *and* look like you were trying to sneak something past them. That's worse than just citing her upfront.

3. **Engage the bias head-on.** If you're worried readers will dismiss the argument because of the source, that's actually a useful rhetorical challenge to address directly. You can acknowledge that Rand is polarizing and then make the case that *this particular idea* stands on its own merits regardless of what one thinks of her broader philosophy. That's a much more persuasive move than hoping nobody notices.

4. **It strengthens your ethos.** A writer who says "I know this thinker is controversial, but here's why this specific argument holds up" comes across as more intellectually confident and honest than one who obscures their influences.

**Practically:** Cite her, briefly acknowledge the controversy if your audience warrants it, and then let the strength of the argument do the work. If the argument can't survive being associated with its author, that's a problem with the argument — not with the citation.
