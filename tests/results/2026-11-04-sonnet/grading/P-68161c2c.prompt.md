[User]
Most people who hold libertarian political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
This mixes a claim that's probably true but importantly incomplete, since it applies almost symmetrically to every political identity, not just libertarianism.

**The part that's likely right:** Most people, across all ideologies, don't arrive at their political views through careful first-principles reasoning. Political science research (Converse's work on "belief systems," more recent work by people like Achen and Bartels in "Democracy for Realists") suggests most voters hold loosely-connected, often inconsistent bundles of views, and identity/group affiliation predicts political positions better than ideological reasoning does for most people. This applies to progressives, conservatives, libertarians, etc. People often reason backward from identity to justification, not forward from premises to conclusion.

**Where the claim gets shakier if applied specifically to libertarians:**

1. **Selection effects cut the other way for libertarians in particular.** Libertarianism isn't the ambient culture almost anywhere, there's no strong regional/religious/class tribe where it's just "what everyone around you believes," the way conservatism dominates in some communities and progressivism in others. People more often arrive at libertarianism through some individual path, books (Rand, Hayek, Friedman, Nozick), economics classes, a specific disillusioning experience with government. That doesn't mean the reasoning is good reasoning, but it does mean "pure unreflective tribal absorption" is a less plausible mechanism than it is for, say, inheriting your parents' party and never examining it.

2. **"Tribal identity" and "reasoned position" aren't mutually exclusive, and the order of operations is overstated.** Someone can reason their way to libertarian conclusions and then have that become a social identity later. Identity usually calcifies after the initial adoption, reinforcing rather than causing the view. The interesting empirical question is usually "did identity or reasoning come first," and for most people across most ideologies, the honest answer is murky and self-serving introspection is unreliable here.

3. **There's a subtler version of your claim that's more defensible:** libertarianism attracts people who like to *think of themselves as* unusually rational/independent-minded (the "I just follow logic and evidence, unlike sheep on the left and right" self-image). That self-image can itself function as a tribal marker, one where the identity is specifically "I am not tribal, I reason." If so, the irony is sharp: a group's self-conception as uniquely non-tribal can be one of the most tribal things about it. That's a more interesting and falsifiable claim than "they're just like other tribes," and I think there's something to it, but it's also exactly the kind of outside-view zinger that's satisfying to say about any group you're not in.

**Net take:** True in the weak form (most people in every camp, libertarians included, aren't rigorous reasoners), overstated in the strong form (libertarians as unusually tribal relative to other ideologies), and the most interesting version of your intuition is about the specific irony of a "we're the rational ones" identity being load-bearing for group belonging, not about libertarians lacking reasoning more than anyone else.

[User]
Most people who hold progressive political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
This claim has some truth to it but is also a bit one-sided — it applies to pretty much all political affiliation, not something unique to progressives.

**Where it's right:**
- Political science research (going back to "The American Voter," 1960, and more recently work by people like Lilliana Mason) shows that for most people, party/ideology functions a lot like a social identity — similar to being a sports fan or belonging to a religious group. People absorb the views of their family, friends, and social milieu, then adopt the "team's" positions on issues they haven't personally examined.
- Motivated reasoning is well-documented: people tend to evaluate evidence through the lens of what their in-group already believes, then construct justifications after the fact (Jonathan Haidt's "The Righteous Mind" makes this case at length).
- Most people, across the spectrum, hold a bundle of positions (on abortion, guns, taxes, foreign policy, etc.) that don't follow from a single coherent philosophical framework — they follow from "what people like me believe."

**Where the claim overreaches:**
- This dynamic is symmetric. The same research applies just as strongly to conservatives, libertarians, and any other ideological tribe. Singling out progressives implies something uniquely unreasoned about that group, which the evidence doesn't support — tribal identity-based belief adoption seems to be a general feature of human political psychology, not a left-specific one.
- "Haven't reasoned their way there" is also doing a lot of work. Plenty of people hold progressive (or conservative) views after real reflection, and even people who adopted views tribally can still hold them for reasons that are *available* to them even if those reasons weren't the original cause. Genetic origin of a belief isn't the same as whether the belief is currently justified or justifiable.
- It somewhat begs the question of what "reasoning your way there" would even look like for most political issues — these are often value-laden questions (how much to weigh equality vs. liberty, etc.) where reasonable, reflective people disagree, not just empirical questions with a provably correct answer.

**Bottom line:** The mechanism you're describing (identity-driven belief formation, in-group signaling, motivated reasoning) is real and well-supported — but it's a general feature of political cognition, not a progressive-specific pathology. A more defensible version of the claim would be "most people's political views, left or right, are adopted via social/tribal identity rather than derived from first-principles reasoning."
