[Assistant]
Empirically, **most people who support democratic socialism—like most people who hold any political ideology—arrive there through social identity, material grievances, and elite cues rather than by reasoning their way through political philosophy or economic theory.**

Here is what the evidence from political science and public opinion research actually shows:

### 1. How people form political beliefs in general
Since Philip Converse’s foundational 1964 work (*The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*) and modern follow-ups like Achen and Bartels (*Democracy for Realists*) or Lilliana Mason (*Uncivil Agreement*), political science has consistently found that **80–85% of the public does not think ideologically.** 

Most voters do not start with abstract principles about capital ownership or market allocation and deduce policy preferences from them. Instead, they:
1. Identify with a social group, generational cohort, or cultural tribe.
2. Adopt the labels and positions championed by trusted figures within that group.
3. Use reasoning primarily *post hoc* to justify the positions their group holds.

Democratic socialists are no exception to this rule.

### 2. What "democratic socialism" actually means to most supporters
Survey data (from Pew, Gallup, and YouGov) consistently shows a massive gap between the textbook definition of democratic socialism (social or worker ownership of the means of production achieved democratically) and what self-identified supporters actually mean by it:

* **It is mostly a brand for Nordic-style social democracy:** When polled on what "socialism" means to them, the vast majority of American supporters describe a capitalist welfare state—universal healthcare, tuition-free public college, higher minimum wages, and taxing the rich—or even just "being fair and generous" or "social equality." Only a small fraction (typically under 15–20% of supporters) actually favor abolishing private property or nationalizing major industries.
* **Generational and oppositional identity:** For Millennials and Gen Z, the label largely emerged after 2008 and the 2016 Bernie Sanders campaign as a tribal marker of dissatisfaction with the status quo. Because conservative critics spent decades calling standard welfare-state programs (like the ACA or Medicare) "socialism," younger voters who wanted those programs simply adopted the label as a badge of defiance against both Republicans and the Democratic establishment.

### 3. The split within the movement
To be accurate, you have to distinguish between two very different groups under the label:

* **Casual supporters (the vast majority):** People who tell pollsters they view "democratic socialism" favorably or vote for candidates like Bernie Sanders or AOC. Their attachment is driven by concrete material frustrations (healthcare costs, student debt, housing) combined with progressive cultural identity. It is tribal and expressive, not theoretical.
* **Committed activists (a small minority, e.g., DSA cadres):** This group *does* engage heavily with theory, history, and policy reasoning—often obsessively debating Kautsky, Gramsci, or labor strategy. However, even in high-information activist spaces, once an individual is inside the movement, intense social conformity and in-group policing play a massive role in which specific positions they hold.

### Bottom Line
It isn't purely empty tribalism—there are real, material grievances (healthcare, housing, inequality) underneath the appeal. Calling it *just* a fashion statement misses why the brand caught on when it did. 

However, **almost nobody sits down, compares the incentive structures of market capitalism vs. socialized ownership, and reasons their way to democratic socialism from first principles.** For the vast majority of supporters, "democratic socialist" is an expressive identity label meaning *"I am on the left, I think the current economic system is rigged, and I want European-style social programs."*

Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Based on empirical research in political science and moral psychology, the short answer is: **for the vast majority of ordinary voters, it is primarily a tribal and identity-driven position, though a distinct, smaller subset of ideological libertarians genuinely reason their way there from abstract principles.**

Here is what the evidence shows when you break down the coalition of people who say they support "free markets" or "laissez-faire capitalism":

### 1. The Mass Public: Tribalism and Elite Cues
Decades of political science research—starting with Philip Converse’s foundational work in *The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics* and continuing through modern work by Donald Kinder, Nathan Kalmoe, and Lilliana Mason—shows that most voters do not hold coherent, deductively reasoned ideologies. 

Instead, mass political attitudes mostly work top-down:
* **Identity first, policy second:** People align with a political tribe based on social, cultural, geographic, or religious identity. Once they identify as "conservative" or "Republican," they adopt the policy positions signaled by their group's elites and media—which for decades included free-market orthodoxy.
* **The "Trump test" proved this:** If mass support for laissez-faire capitalism were deeply reasoned, voters would have rejected Donald Trump’s pivot toward tariffs, industrial policy, and economic nationalism. Instead, when party elites changed the cue, millions of voters who previously championed "free markets" effortlessly flipped to supporting protectionism and state intervention. Their commitment was to the coalition, not to Friedrich Hayek or Milton Friedman.
* **Operational vs. Symbolic ideology:** Political scientists Christopher Ellis and James Stimson showed that many Americans are "symbolically" conservative (they love the *labels* "free enterprise" and "capitalism" as cultural totems) but "operationally" liberal or moderate (they strongly support Social Security, Medicare, price caps, and specific regulations when asked about concrete policies).

### 2. The Self-Interest Component
Alongside tribalism, a substantial slice of laissez-faire support comes from straightforward material self-interest paired with motivated reasoning. Business owners, high earners, and investors face direct financial costs from taxes and regulation. Moral psychology shows that people are very good at adopting elaborate philosophical justifications *after* their wallet or status nudges them toward a conclusion—what Jonathan Haidt describes as the rationalist "rider" serving the intuitive "elephant."

### 3. The Exception: Ideological Libertarians
Does *anyone* reason their way to laissez-faire capitalism? Yes, and research shows they are psychologically distinct from standard partisan conservatives.

In a large-scale 2012 study (*Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians*), Jonathan Haidt, Ravi Iyer, and colleagues studied over 11,000 self-identified libertarians. They found:
* **High "systemizing" and need for cognition:** Unlike both mainstream liberals (who score high on empathy/care) and mainstream conservatives (who score high on group loyalty, authority, and sanctity), genuine libertarians scored unusually high on *systemizing* (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems) and cognitive reflection, while scoring lower on emotional reactivity and group-loyalty metrics.
* **Deductive reasoning from a single axiom:** This group genuinely tends to reason deductively—either from deontological premises (self-ownership, the non-aggression principle, individual liberty as the supreme moral value) or consequentialist economics (price signals, spontaneous order, public choice theory).
* **Resistance to tribal cues:** Unlike partisan conservatives, principled laissez-faire advocates consistently oppose tariffs, corporate bailouts, restrictive zoning, and immigration restrictions regardless of which party is in power.

### The Bottom Line
It depends on who you mean by "people who support laissez-faire capitalism":
* If you mean **the tens of millions of regular voters** who cheer for "free markets" at rallies or in polls, the evidence overwhelmingly shows it is **tribal identity and elite cue-taking** (mixed with material self-interest).
* If you mean **committed libertarians, classical liberals, and market-oriented economists** (who make up roughly 2% to 7% of the electorate), a large share of them actually **do** arrive there through unusually high-cognition, deductive reasoning—even if critics argue their starting axioms are too narrow.
