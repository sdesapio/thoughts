Rent control is proven to increase the housing supply, right?

[Assistant]
No — that's actually backwards from the economic consensus.

Rent control is generally understood to **decrease** or constrain housing supply over time, not increase it. The typical mechanisms cited:

- **Reduced incentive to build new rental units** — if controlled units are less profitable, developers favor condos, commercial space, or building in jurisdictions without controls.
- **Reduced incentive to maintain/convert existing units** — landlords may let controlled units deteriorate, convert them to condos, Airbnb/short-term rentals, or owner-occupancy (which is often exempt), effectively pulling them out of the rental supply.
- **Lock-in effect** — tenants in controlled units stay longer than they otherwise would (since moving means losing a below-market rent), reducing turnover and making the overall market tighter for people searching for a place.

This is one of the more widely shared views across economists (left and right) — surveys like the IGM Forum poll of economists have shown strong agreement that rent control tends to reduce the quantity and quality of available rental housing in the long run, even though it can help existing tenants in controlled units in the short run by keeping their rent stable.

The famous empirical study often cited here is Diamond, McQuillan, and Autor's work on San Francisco rent control expansion (2015 study), which found landlords responded by converting units to condos or owner-occupied housing, shrinking the rental supply by about 15%.

So: rent control can be a useful tool for protecting incumbent tenants from displacement/rent spikes, but it's generally seen as *reducing* supply and exacerbating scarcity for everyone else, not increasing it.

Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No — that's a widely repeated claim, but it's not supported by the evidence in most cases.

**The core idea:** Tax cuts can boost economic growth (via more investment, labor supply, etc.), and that growth generates *some* additional tax revenue that offsets part of the original revenue loss. This is sometimes called the "feedback effect" or is loosely tied to the Laffer Curve.

**Why "always pay for themselves" is false:**

1. **The Laffer Curve cuts both ways.** It says there's *some* tax rate where cutting rates raises revenue — but only if you're on the high end of the curve (rates so high they discourage activity). Most developed economies, including the US for most of modern history, are estimated to be on the lower part of the curve, where cuts reduce revenue.

2. **Empirical track record:** Major US tax cuts (Reagan 1981, Bush 2001/2003, Trump 2017) were followed by larger deficits, not smaller ones, even accounting for growth effects. Nonpartisan scorekeepers like the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation, as well as groups across the political spectrum (e.g., the Tax Foundation on the right, Tax Policy Center on the left), have consistently found that growth offsets only a fraction — typically 10–30% — of the static revenue loss, not 100%+.

3. **Context matters a lot.** The effect depends heavily on which taxes are cut (corporate vs. payroll vs. individual), who receives them (lower-income earners tend to spend more, which can have different multiplier effects than cuts for high earners who save more), the state of the economy (slack vs. full employment), and whether cuts are financed by debt (which can crowd out investment and offset growth gains).

4. **Economists broadly reject the strong claim.** Surveys of economists (e.g., the University of Chicago's IGM Forum) show near-unanimous rejection of the idea that tax cuts generally pay for themselves, including among economists who are otherwise sympathetic to supply-side arguments.

**Bottom line:** Tax cuts *can* partially offset their own cost through growth, and in rare extreme cases (very high marginal rates) could even increase revenue, but the blanket claim that they "always pay for themselves" isn't supported by mainstream economic evidence or historical outcomes.
