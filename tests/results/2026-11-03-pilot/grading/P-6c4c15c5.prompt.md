[Assistant]
As a claim specifically about libertarians, **that is largely unsupported—and on the "tribal identity" part, it gets one of the distinctive features of libertarianism backward.** As a broader observation that human beings rarely arrive at their political views through pure, unmotivated deduction, it is much closer to the truth, but it applies across the political spectrum rather than singling out libertarians.

To evaluate whether the claim is right, it helps to separate three things it bundles together:

### 1. "Not reasoned" vs. "Tribal identity" is a false dichotomy
Saying people haven't "reasoned their way" to a view does not mean they adopted it as a "tribal identity." Those two options do not exhaust the ways people form political beliefs. People also adopt views because of:
* **Psychological disposition and temperament** (e.g., high sensitivity to personal autonomy, distrust of authority, or low attachment to group hierarchy).
* **Personal incentives and lived experience** (e.g., tax burdens, regulatory friction, or experiences with state enforcement).
* **Explicit principles applied to untested premises** (reasoning deductively from axioms—like self-ownership or non-aggression—that were chosen intuitively rather than proved).

A person whose libertarian views stem from a temperament that dislikes being told what to do has not strictly "reasoned their way there" from neutral first principles, but they have not joined a tribe either.

### 2. Why "tribal identity" fits libertarians worse than mainstream partisans
If you apply the same standard to liberals, conservatives, socialists, and libertarians alike, mainstream partisan views (Democrat/Republican, left/right) are heavily transmitted through family, geography, religion, and large social institutions. In political science (such as Achen and Bartels's work on group-based politics), that inherited group attachment is the standard model of how most citizens acquire policy views.

Libertarianism has a structural difference:
* **There is rarely a large default libertarian tribe to inherit.** Because libertarians are a small minority without a dominant party or mainstream cultural institution, adopting a libertarian view—combining positions that alienate both the mainstream left (on economics) and the mainstream right (on social issues or foreign policy)—usually requires *leaving* or resisting an existing social tribe rather than conforming to one.
* **Psychological evidence points away from group-loyalty motivation.** While I cannot verify exact sample figures from primary sources in this session, the best-known empirical study directly testing this—Iyer, Koleva, Graham, Ditto, and Haidt (2012), *"Understanding Libertarian Morality"*—found that compared with both self-identified liberals and conservatives, self-identified libertarians scored significantly **higher** on need for cognition, cognitive reflection, and systemizing/analytical thinking styles, and **lower** on social relatedness and the "ingroup loyalty" moral foundation.
* **Where tribalism *does* show up:** Once someone enters a libertarian subculture, organization, or online community, tribal dynamics—purity tests over who is a "real libertarian," shibboleths, and in-group signaling—readily take hold. Tribal reinforcement can *maintain* or harden libertarian views even when it did not *originate* them.

### 3. The standard for "reasoned their way there"
Whether it is true that "most haven't reasoned their way there" depends on how strictly you define reasoning:
* **Strict standard (deriving views from neutral evidence without prior disposition):** Almost nobody in *any* political camp meets this standard. Even highly analytical people—libertarians included—frequently use explicit reasoning to systematize and defend intuitions they already find appealing.
* **Comparative standard (explicit principle-based deliberation vs. social conformity):** Available evidence suggests self-identified libertarians rely on explicit abstract principles and analytical deduction *more* than the average partisan, precisely because their views are cross-cutting and aren't supplied ready-made by a major party.

### Bottom line
* **Is it right that most libertarians adopted their views as a tribal identity?** **No.** That is unverified as a majority claim and contradicts both the minority/cross-pressured social position of libertarianism and empirical findings on libertarian psychology (which show unusually weak ingroup attachment and high analytical inclination). Tribalism plays a much larger role in maintaining orthodoxy inside libertarian subcultures than in recruiting people to those views in the first place.
* **Is it right that most haven't purely reasoned their way there?** **Probably yes, if "reasoned" means arriving at conclusions independent of temperament and prior moral intuitions**—but that is a general truth about human political psychology (conservative, liberal, socialist, and libertarian alike), not a distinctive defect of libertarians. And regardless of how someone arrived at a view, the psychological origin of a belief neither proves nor refutes the arguments for it.

Whether that claim holds depends on what it means by "haven't reasoned their way there" and whether it is stated as a general finding about political psychology or as a claim specific to progressives.

### 1. Separating the claims

The statement combines two distinct propositions:
1. **A general mechanism:** Most people adopt a broad political package primarily through social identity, group cues, and shared norms rather than by independently deriving each policy position from first principles and primary evidence.
2. **A group-specific claim:** This mechanism characterizes progressive voters in particular (or to a uniquely high degree).

Evaluated against political science evidence, **the first is well-supported across the political spectrum, while the second is misleading if framed as distinctive to progressives.**

### 2. What the evidence shows

Decades of empirical research in political behavior support the view that social identity and elite cues do much of the work in forming mass political attitudes—for progressives, conservatives, and moderates alike:

* **Constraint and elite cues (Converse, 1964; Zaller, 1992):** Philip Converse's classic work (*The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*) and John Zaller's *The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion* showed that most citizens do not construct tightly integrated ideological systems from scratch. Instead, people who pay attention to politics learn "what goes with what" from partisan elites, media, and peer networks.
* **Partisan identity as a social identity (Green, Palmquist, & Schickler, 2002; Mason, 2018):** In *Partisan Hearts and Minds* and Lilliana Mason's *Uncivil Agreement*, partisan and ideological labels function much like religious or community identities. People often align with a coalition first—through family, geography, education, profession, or social sorting—and subsequently adopt the policy positions associated with that group.
* **Experimental cue-taking (e.g., Cohen, 2003):** Controlled experiments repeatedly show that when the same policy proposal is attributed to a person's own party versus the opposing party, evaluations shift substantially to match the group cue, across both liberal and conservative participants.
* **Identity-protective cognition (Kahan et al.):** Higher analytical ability or political knowledge often makes partisans across the spectrum *better* at rationalizing their group's existing positions rather than more likely to derive conclusions neutrally.

If "haven't reasoned their way there" means *did not independently derive a multi-issue platform from first principles without relying on group cues*, then **yes, that is likely true of most people who hold progressive views—just as it is true of most people who hold conservative or other broad political views.**

### 3. Where the claim goes too far

A stronger reading of the claim—that people who hold progressive views do not use reasoning at all, or that tribal adoption is unique to progressives—is not supported by the evidence:

* **Values and material interests still play a real role:** People are not randomly assigned to political tribes. Underlying moral priorities (such as prioritizing egalitarianism, harm reduction, or social safety nets on the left; or order, tradition, and market liberty on the right), personal experiences, and economic interests strongly influence *which* coalition a person joins in the first place.
* **Social learning is not always irrational:** No individual has the time or expertise to independently evaluate primary evidence on tax policy, foreign affairs, monetary policy, and environmental regulation simultaneously. Relying on trusted institutions, peers, and coalitions as heuristics is a standard human shortcut—though it regularly fails when group loyalty overrides contrary evidence.
* **No evidence of a progressive-only effect:** Comparative studies of partisan cue-taking, motivated reasoning, and in-group bias find these dynamics on both the left and the right. Attributing tribalism to one side alone mistakes a general feature of mass politics for a specific group's defect.

### 4. Important distinction: how a view is acquired vs. whether it is true

Even when a person adopts a political view primarily out of group identity, **how someone arrived at a belief does not determine whether the belief itself is true or false.** Dismissing a progressive (or conservative) policy argument because many of its adherents adopted it socially is a genetic fallacy. Any specific claim still has to be evaluated on its own premises and evidence.

### Conclusion and what would change it

* **Conclusion (Probable / Well-supported by political science):** Most people who hold progressive political views—like most people who hold conservative political views—acquire the full package substantially through social identity, cultural sorting, and trusted group cues rather than independent, issue-by-issue derivation from first principles. However, foundational values and lived experience still shape which coalition people gravitate toward, and the tribal mechanism is a general feature of human political behavior rather than a specifically progressive trait.
* **What would change this assessment:** This conclusion would need to be revised if empirical studies showed that majorities of citizens derive their policy positions independently of party/group cues, or—conversely—if controlled cue-taking and motivated-reasoning studies demonstrated a large, consistent asymmetry showing that one ideological coalition relies on social identity while comparable coalitions do not.
