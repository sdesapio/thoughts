Yes, with two important qualifications: this describes how most people form political views across the ideological spectrum, not a trait unique to progressives, and "tribal adoption" vs. "independent reasoning" is a matter of degree rather than a strict binary.

### 1. What the claim means

The claim has two parts:
- **Descriptive:** A majority ("most") of people with progressive views acquired their policy bundle primarily through social identity and group alignment rather than independent, issue-by-issue derivation from first principles and evidence.
- **Implied contrast:** Framing this specifically about progressives can imply either that progressives do this more than other groups, or that how a person acquired a belief settles whether the belief is true. Neither implication holds without separate support.

### 2. Evidence on political belief formation

In political science and psychology, the evidence is strong (**probable to near-certain** for mass publics, primarily studied in the US and Western democracies) that most citizens—progressive, conservative, and moderate alike—form political views largely through social and identity mechanisms:

- **Low ideological constraint:** Philip Converse's foundational work (*The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*, 1964) and decades of follow-up research (e.g., Kinder & Kalmoe, *Neither Liberal nor Conservative*, 2017) show that most voters do not derive individual policy positions from an abstract, coherent philosophical framework. Instead, people learn "what goes with what" from political elites, media, and peers once they identify with a camp.
- **Partisanship as social identity:** Research by Lilliana Mason (*Uncivil Agreement*, 2018) and Donald Green, Bradley Palmquist, and Eric Schickler (*Partisan Hearts and Minds*, 2002) demonstrates that partisan and ideological labels function much like religious or team identities. People frequently adopt a group identity first—through family, geography, education, or social networks—and subsequently align their issue positions to match the group.
- **Elite cue-taking and motivated reasoning:** Experimental studies (such as Geoffrey Cohen's 2003 "Party Over Policy" experiments and subsequent replications across both parties) show that when ordinary partisans are presented with identical policy proposals attributed to either their own party or the opposing party, party cues heavily outweigh the policy's actual content—even among politically knowledgeable citizens.

*(Limit on evidence: Because no retrieval or external lookup was used in this turn, these citations rely on established literature in political behavior rather than newly pulled study data; exact effect sizes vary across issues and sub-populations.)*

### 3. Does this apply differently to progressives?

There is no solid evidence that progressives are uniquely prone to identity-driven belief adoption compared to conservatives or other political groups:
- **Symmetry of cue-taking:** Studies testing partisan cue-taking and identity-protective cognition (e.g., Dan Kahan's work on cultural cognition; Ditto et al.'s 2019 meta-analysis of partisan bias) consistently find that both liberals/progressives and conservatives engage in motivated reasoning and adopt in-group cues at broadly comparable rates.
- **Bundled positions:** Both major ideological coalitions hold combinations of views (e.g., linking tax policy, climate science, gun regulation, and foreign policy) that do not follow deductively from a single axiom. When a large majority of a group shifts together on a newly politicized issue within months of elite signaling, social transmission—not millions of simultaneous, independent derivations—is doing most of the work. That pattern appears on both the left and the right.

### 4. Where the "purely tribal" view goes too far

The strongest counterargument is that social identity does not operate in a vacuum:
- **Values and moral intuitions:** Research (e.g., Jonathan Haidt's moral foundations work; Schwartz's basic human values) suggests that underlying personality traits, material interests, and core values (such as prioritizing egalitarianism and harm reduction vs. order, tradition, or authority) influence *which* political tribe a person finds attractive in the first place.
- **Division of epistemic labor:** No individual has the time or expertise to independently research tax incidence, atmospheric physics, zoning economics, and constitutional law from scratch. Relying on trusted institutions, experts, and coalitions is a normal human heuristic—even if it often blurs into tribal conformity.
- **Genetic fallacy:** Even when a person adopts a progressive (or conservative) view entirely out of tribal loyalty, that says nothing about whether the view itself is true or false. A conclusion adopted for bad or social reasons can still be supported by sound arguments and evidence.

### Bottom line

- **As a statement about human political psychology:** **Yes (probable).** Most people who hold progressive views—like most people who hold conservative or libertarian views—adopted most of their policy package through social identity, peer alignment, and cue-taking rather than independent first-principles reasoning, though usually anchored by genuine underlying value preferences.
- **As a claim unique to progressives, or as a refutation of progressive ideas:** **No.** The same mechanism governs political belief formation across the spectrum, and how a person arrived at a view does not determine whether the view is right.
- **What would change this conclusion:** Empirical evidence showing that a majority of progressive identifiers hold stable policy views unaffected by elite/in-group cues, or comparative evidence showing a large, replicable asymmetry where progressives rely on group cues while other ideological groups independently derive their positions.

To evaluate it clearly, it helps to separate two questions: **(1)** whether most libertarians arrive at their views through pure, unconditioned reasoning, and **(2)** whether they adopt those views primarily as a **tribal identity**.

### 1. "Haven't reasoned their way there" depends on the standard for reasoning

If "reasoned their way there" means deriving political conclusions from scratch through neutral first principles, prior to temperament, social environment, or motivated reasoning, then **most libertarians have not done that—because almost no group of political believers does.** Across the spectrum, political science and psychology (from Philip Converse's classic work on belief systems to Achen and Bartels' *Democracy for Realists* and Dan Kahan's work on motivated cognition) consistently find that people's dispositions, social milieus, and post-hoc rationalization heavily shape their politics. Singling out libertarians on this count applies a standard to one group that holds across political ideologies generally.

If, however, the standard is **comparative**—whether people who hold libertarian views rely *less* on explicit reasoning and system-building than liberals or conservatives—the empirical evidence points the other way. In the largest psychological study specifically examining self-identified libertarians (Iyer, Koleva, Graham, Ditto, & Haidt, 2012, *PLoS ONE*), libertarians scored significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on:
- **Need for cognition** (enjoyment of effortful thinking),
- **Cognitive reflection** (overriding intuitive gut responses with analytical ones), and
- **Systemizing** (a preference for rule-based, deductive systems over empathetic or social narratives).

Because libertarianism is a minority ideology rather than a default family or major-party inheritance for most people, arriving at explicitly libertarian views more often involves deliberate ideological self-selection and abstract rule-application than adopting mainstream partisan views does. (That still does not make their premises true or immune to rationalizing pre-existing psychological dispositions—rationalism and post-hoc justification of one's temperament are very common failure modes among systemizers.)

### 2. "Adopted them as a tribal identity" is the weakest explanation for libertarians

Treating "didn't arrive there by pure reason" as equivalent to "adopted it as a tribal identity" is a false dichotomy. Even when political views are not the product of neutral deduction, they can come from **psychological temperament and moral dispositions** rather than group attachment.

On the specific question of tribalism:
- **Dispositional anti-tribalism:** Iyer et al. (2012) found that libertarians score markedly *lower* than both liberals and conservatives on group-binding moral foundations (in-group loyalty, authority, and sanctity), extraversion, and social connectedness, while placing primary moral weight on individual liberty/autonomy. Dispositionally, they are the *least* group-oriented of the major ideological profiles studied.

### Bottom line

- **As a statement about human belief formation generally:** It is **probable** that most libertarians—like most liberals, conservatives, and socialists—did not arrive at their worldview through neutral, unmotivated reasoning alone; temperament and post-hoc rationalization play a large role.

Compared with other ideological groups, libertarians are disproportionately driven by individualist, systemizing dispositions rather than group loyalty—meaning their characteristic blind spot is much more often **rigid, rationalistic deduction from narrow premises** than tribal conformity.
