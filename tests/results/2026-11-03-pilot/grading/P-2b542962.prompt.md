Political science and psychology suggest there is significant truth to that premise—not because it is uniquely true of progressives, but because it describes how **most humans across the political spectrum** form and hold their beliefs.

Here is how researchers break down the tension between reasoning and tribalism in politics:

### 1. Where the "Tribal Identity" thesis is right
Decades of research in political science and moral psychology support the idea that identity usually precedes ideology:
* **Social Sorting and Elite Cues:** Political scientists like Lilliana Mason (*Uncivil Agreement*) and Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels (*Democracy for Realists*) argue that most voters do not construct a coherent political philosophy from first principles and then pick a party. Instead, people identify with a social group (based on geography, education, religion, race, or peer group) and adopt the "bundle" of positions associated with that group. Notice how someone’s stance on tax policy can reliably predict their stance on climate change or gun control—issues that have no inherent logical connection.
* **Motivated Reasoning:** Psychologist Jonathan Haidt (*The Righteous Mind*) argues that human moral reasoning is mostly post-hoc: intuition and social belonging come first, and strategic reasoning comes second to justify what we already feel or what our tribe believes.
* **The "Education Paradox":** Studies by researchers like Dan Kahan show that higher levels of political sophistication and analytical skill often make people *better* at rationalizing their tribe’s views rather than more objective. Because progressives today are disproportionately concentrated in credentialed, university-educated, and media/academic spaces, social signaling and conformity pressures within those professional and social networks can be particularly strong.

### 2. What the "Tribal Identity" framing misses
Reducing progressive views (or conservative views) *entirely* to mindless tribalism overlooks several real drivers of political belief:
* **Psychological and Moral Dispositions:** Research shows that political leanings correlate with underlying personality traits (like high "Openness to Experience" among liberals/progressives) and moral foundations. Progressives tend to weight the moral foundations of **care/harm** and **fairness/reciprocity** much more heavily than authority, loyalty, or sanctity. Adopting progressive views is often an authentic expression of those underlying values, even if the specific policy details are absorbed from peers.
* **Material Interests and Lived Experience:** Many people arrive at progressive positions—such as support for universal healthcare, labor unions, or civil rights—through direct personal or economic experience rather than abstract philosophizing or mere trend-following. Experiencing a problem firsthand and wanting a structural solution is a form of practical reasoning.
* **Intellectual Tradition:** While the average voter on any side relies on heuristics and group cues, progressive ideology itself is rooted in a long intellectual tradition (Enlightenment egalitarianism, utilitarianism, social liberalism, critical theory, and Keynesian economics). Many people *do* engage deeply with these arguments, and even those using shortcuts are often deferring to thinkers and institutions they trust—much like people defer to doctors or scientists without reading the primary literature themselves.

### Summary
If the claim is that **most people adopt political views primarily through social belonging, underlying temperament, and group cues rather than independent, first-principles deduction**, the empirical evidence strongly agrees—for progressives, conservatives, and moderates alike. 

However, if the claim implies that progressive views are *uniquely* tribal or lack genuine moral and experiential foundations, it misses both the universality of human tribalism and the real values and experiences that draw people to the left in the first place.

Political psychology and political science suggest that your premise holds a lot of truth, with an important caveat: while tribal identity drives *most* political belief across the spectrum, libertarianism actually tends to attract a higher proportion of explicitly "reasoned" (systemizing) adherents than mainstream ideologies—though that very reasoning process often becomes its own form of tribal identity.

Here is how researchers generally break down the dynamics of libertarian belief:

### 1. The Rule: Almost All Politics is Tribal
Decades of political science research (most notably by scholars like Philip Converse, Donald Kinder, and Lilliana Mason) show that the vast majority of citizens do not form coherent ideological worldviews from first principles. Instead, people generally adopt political views through:
* **Socialization and Subculture:** Adopting the views of peers, family, or professional networks (e.g., tech, finance, or rural individualism).
* **Identity and In-Group Signaling:** Using political labels, slogans ("taxation is theft," "don't tread on me"), and aesthetics to signal membership in a group and opposition to an out-group.
* **Motivated Reasoning:** Starting with a gut preference or self-interest and using reason backward to justify it (what psychologist Jonathan Haidt calls the "rider" justifying where the emotional "elephant" already wants to go).

In this sense, many self-identified libertarians—especially those who are essentially cultural conservatives or anti-establishment populists who dislike the "Republican" label—adopt the identity tribally rather than philosophically.

### 2. The Exception: Libertarians Are Disproportionately "Systemizers"
In a major 2012 study (*Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians*), Jonathan Haidt and his colleagues surveyed over 11,000 self-identified libertarians and compared them to liberals and conservatives. Their findings directly challenge the idea that libertarians *haven't* reasoned their way to their views:
* **High Need for Cognition:** Libertarians scored significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on measures of cognitive reflection, enjoyment of effortful thinking, and systemizing (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems) over empathizing.
* **Low Group-Binding Moral Foundations:** On Moral Foundations Theory, libertarians scored very low on "Ingroup/Loyalty," "Authority," and "Sanctity" (the tribal foundations that bind conservatives) as well as lower on "Care/Harm" (which drives liberals), relying almost entirely on a single foundation: **Liberty vs. Oppression**.
* **Low Social Connectedness:** Self-identified libertarians reported weaker ties to local communities and less emotional attachment to groups, making traditional tribalism less appealing to them.

Because libertarianism is a minority position not usually taught in schools or championed by a major dominant party, many people who arrive at strict libertarianism actually *do* get there by reading texts (Ayn Rand, Milton Friedman, Murray Rothbard, Friedrich Hayek) and following deductive axioms (like the Non-Aggression Principle) to their logical extremes.

### 3. The Synthesis: When "Reason" Becomes the Tribe
Even when someone reasons their way into libertarianism, two psychological traps often turn it back into a tribal identity:
* **Axiomatic Rationalism vs. Empiricism:** Deductive reasoning from a single premise ("all coercion is wrong") is still a form of cognitive rigidity if it ignores empirical trade-offs. Choosing *which* axiom to value above all others is a pre-rational, psychological disposition—not a purely logical discovery.
* **The "Rationalist" Tribe:** For many libertarians, the identity is built around the flattering self-conception of being the only rational, independent thinkers in a world of "sheep" or "statists." Once a person joins online communities, think tanks, or subcultures centered on that identity, groupthink, purity tests ("you're not a *real* libertarian"), and dogmatic orthodoxy take over just as strongly as they do on the progressive left or nationalist right.

### Summary
If you are looking at casual, culturally adjacent libertarians (e.g., anti-government populists), yes—it is largely a tribal aesthetic. If you are looking at committed ideological libertarians, they are actually *more* likely than the average voter to have explicitly reasoned their way into the ideology—but that reliance on abstract, deductive rules often functions as a psychological defense mechanism and an intellectual tribe of its own.
