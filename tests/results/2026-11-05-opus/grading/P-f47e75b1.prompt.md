That's a broad claim, and like most broad claims about why large groups of people believe what they believe, it's partly true and partly unfair — and it applies to essentially every political orientation, not just libertarianism.

**The grain of truth:** A lot of political identity formation is social, not philosophical. People absorb views from their communities, media ecosystems, family, and peer groups, then backfill justifications. This is well-supported by social psychology research (Haidt's *The Righteous Mind* makes the case thoroughly). Libertarianism isn't exempt from this. There are clearly libertarian subcultures — online communities, podcasts, influencer ecosystems — where the identity comes first and the reasoning is more aesthetic than rigorous.

**Where it overreaches:** The same is true of progressives, conservatives, socialists, and every other political camp. Singling out libertarians as uniquely tribal is itself a tribal move — it's the kind of thing people say to dismiss a group they disagree with rather than engage with the strongest versions of its arguments. Libertarianism has a serious intellectual tradition (Mill, Hayek, Nozick, Friedman, Buchanan) that many adherents have genuinely engaged with.

**A more honest framing** might be:

- *Most people* across *all* political orientations hold views that are more tribal than reasoned.
- Within every camp, there's a spectrum from "absorbed it from my social environment" to "arrived here through genuine deliberation."
- The interesting question isn't "did they reason their way there?" but "can they steelman the opposition and identify the real tradeoffs?" That test filters for genuine reasoning regardless of where someone lands.

So: you're probably right about a large chunk of self-identified libertarians, but the observation isn't very interesting unless you apply it symmetrically.
