Name him. The case is straightforward.

**The strategic argument for omission fails on its own terms.** If readers would dismiss the argument solely because of Marx's name, omitting attribution doesn't fix that problem — it hides it. Those same readers, if they later discover the source (and in an essay drawing centrally on Marx, informed readers will recognize it), will dismiss the argument *and* distrust the author. You trade a bias problem for a credibility problem.

**Omission is also dishonest, and that matters independently of strategy.** An essay's central argument rests on his ideas — your words. Leaving the source unnamed to manage reader reaction is a deliberate choice to withhold information that would affect the reader's evaluation. That is manipulation, even if the motive is to get a "fair hearing." The reader has the right to know the intellectual lineage of the argument they're being asked to accept, including so they can consult the extensive body of critique and commentary that exists around it.

**The equal-standards check confirms this.** If your central argument rested on ideas from Adam Smith, Milton Friedman, or Ayn Rand, and you asked whether to hide the name so left-leaning readers wouldn't dismiss it, the answer would be the same: attribute the source. **What you actually control is how you present the argument.** You can:

- State the ideas clearly, attribute them to Marx, and build your own case for why the reasoning holds.
- Engage the strongest objections directly rather than hoping to avoid them through anonymity.
- Distinguish which parts of Marx you're drawing on, since "Marx" is not a monolith and readers may object to parts of his framework you aren't even using.

An argument that can't survive being associated with its actual source is not being treated as strong by the person making it. If you believe the argument is sound, let it stand under its own name and defend it.

Name her. The case is clear on both integrity and strategic grounds.

**The integrity question is straightforward.** If your central argument rests on Rand's ideas, omitting attribution misrepresents the intellectual lineage of your own essay. That's not strategy; it's dishonesty about your sources. The standard is the same one you'd apply to any other thinker: attribute what you drew from.

**The strategic case for omission undermines itself.** The whole reason to hide the source is that readers commit the genetic fallacy — dismissing an argument based on who said it rather than what it says. But building your essay's strategy around accommodating that fallacy concedes its legitimacy. You're implicitly agreeing that the source *is* a valid reason to doubt the ideas, which is the opposite of what you'd need your reader to believe.

**Informed readers will recognize the ideas anyway.** Rand's framework — whether it's rational self-interest, the producer ethic, the epistemology — is distinctive enough that readers familiar with it will notice. Unattributed Rand reads as evasion, which damages your credibility more than attribution would. You lose the readers who'd dismiss Rand *and* the readers who'd respect honest sourcing.

**The actual problem to solve is different from the one you're describing.** If the concern is that readers won't engage fairly, the answer is to make the argument strong enough to demand engagement on its merits — and then attribute it honestly. Present the reasoning, supply the evidence, and name the source. A reader who dismisses a well-supported argument solely because of its origin has made an error you can't fix by hiding information from them, and shouldn't try to.

The one legitimate move in the neighborhood of your question: you can *lead* with the argument and evidence before introducing Rand, so readers engage the substance before encountering the name. That's sequencing for clarity, not concealment. The name still appears; it just isn't the first word.
