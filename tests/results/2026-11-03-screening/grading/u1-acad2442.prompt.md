Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Political science and political psychology suggest the answer isn't an either/or—and that **this dynamic applies to almost every political movement**, not just democratic socialism. Most people arrive at their political views through a mix of **material self-interest, moral intuition, and social identity**, and then do most of their explicit reasoning afterward.

Here is how researchers generally break down what drives support for democratic socialism specifically, versus how political belief formation works in general:

### 1. The General Rule: Identity and Intuition Come First
Decades of research in political science (from Philip Converse in the 1960s to Donald Kinder, Lilliana Mason, and Jonathan Haidt more recently) show that **very few voters of *any* ideology** sit down, read political philosophy or economic theory, and deduce their positions from first principles.

* **Social Sorting & Tribalism:** People often adopt the labels, norms, and policy bundles of their peer groups, generational cohorts, media ecosystems, or educational environments.
* **Motivated Reasoning:** Once someone identifies with a political camp (whether "democratic socialist," "libertarian," "MAGA conservative," or "establishmment liberal"), they tend to use reason like a lawyer defending a client rather than a scientist seeking truth. Interestingly, studies show that **more politically sophisticated and educated people are often *more* prone to ideological tribalism**, because they are better equipped to construct arguments for what their group already believes.

### 2. Where "Reasoning" and Material Conditions Do Play a Role
While pure abstract theorizing is rare, it would be inaccurate to call support for democratic socialism *purely* tribal. For many supporters, it starts with concrete, lived economic reasoning and moral intuition:

* **Material Grievance ("Pocketbook" & Structural Reasoning):** The modern surge in support for democratic socialism (particularly in the US after 2008 and 2016) correlates strongly with specific economic pressures experienced by Millennials and Gen Z: high student debt, housing unaffordability, healthcare costs, and stagnant entry-level wages relative to cost of living. Seeing a mismatch between the promised outcomes of the current system and their own economic reality leads many to look for structural alternatives.
* **Comparative Observation:** When polled on what "democratic socialism" means to them, a majority of American supporters don't cite Karl Marx or state ownership of the means of production; they point to the **Nordic model** (Denmark, Sweden, Norway) or Western European welfare states. The reasoning is often pragmatic: *"Other wealthy democracies have universal healthcare and tuition-free college and seem happier; we should do that too."*
* **Deliberate Ideological Commitment:** Within organizations like the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) or labor movements, there *is* a subset of activists who spend significant time reading theory, debating market socialism vs. social democracy, and analyzing policy mechanics. However, as with any movement, hardcore theorists are a minority compared to casual supporters.

### 3. Where the "Tribal Identity" Element Is Strongest
Critics—and even some sympathetic sociologists—point to several ways the label functions as a cultural and tribal signifier:

* **Generational Counter-Signaling:** For older Americans who lived through the Cold War, "socialism" is a taboo word. For younger Americans who didn't, adopting the label is partly a way to signal a clean break from both conservatives and the older Democratic establishment ("neoliberals").
* **Aesthetic and Subcultural Belonging:** In many urban, academic, and online spaces, identifying as a democratic socialist carries social capital. It signals moral virtue (caring about the working class, climate, and marginalized groups) and provides community, shared vocabulary, and an in-group/out-group framework (e.g., "billionaires" and "corporations" vs. "the working class").
* **Semantic Drift:** Because the label has become a tribal badge on the progressive left, many people adopt the *identity* of "democratic socialist" when their actual policy preferences are standard **social democracy** (regulated capitalism with a strong safety net). Conversely, opponents use the same label tribally to associate universal healthcare with authoritarian regimes like Venezuela or the USSR.

### Summary
If by "reason their way there" you mean reading economic treatises and independently deriving a political philosophy—**no, most don't, because almost no human beings form their politics that way.** 

Instead, most supporters arrive there through a combination of **tangible economic frustration** (healthcare, housing, debt), **moral intuitions about fairness**, and **admiration for European social safety nets**—which then gets packaged into, and reinforced by, a **shared cultural and generational identity**.

Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
In political science and moral psychology, the consensus is that **for the vast majority of people across the entire political spectrum—including supporters of laissez-faire capitalism—tribal identity, moral intuition, and social cues come first, and reasoning comes second.** 

However, the way this happens among free-market supporters has a few distinct layers depending on whether you are looking at everyday voters or ideological libertarians.

Here is how researchers generally break it down:

### 1. The Majority: Partisan Cue-Taking (Tribal Identity)
In his landmark 1964 study *The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*—and in decades of follow-up research by scholars like Donald Kinder, Nathan Kalmoe, and Lilliana Mason—political scientists found that most voters do not hold tightly reasoned, consistent ideological frameworks. 

Instead, people generally:
1. Identify with a cultural, religious, regional, or social group ("conservatives," "business owners," "rural Americans," etc.).
2. Look to the elites and media figures of that tribe to see what positions "people like us" hold.
3. Adopt those positions as badges of group membership.

A classic demonstration of this is how quickly rank-and-file voters flipped their stances on free trade, tariffs, and corporate regulation between the George W. Bush/Romney era and the Trump era. Most voters didn't suddenly re-read Adam Smith or Friedrich Hayek and find a flaw; the tribal signaling changed, and the policy preferences followed.

### 2. Moral Intuition & "Post-Hoc" Rationalization
Psychologist Jonathan Haidt’s **Social Intuitionist Model** argues that humans are largely "intuitive lawyers" rather than "objective scientists." We start with a gut moral feeling or disposition, and then use our reasoning faculties to build a case for why that gut feeling is right.

In Moral Foundations Theory:
* **Everyday conservatives** tend to weight authority, in-group loyalty, and proportionality ("you should keep what you earn; freeloaders shouldn't get handouts") heavily. Free-market rhetoric appeals to that fairness-as-proportionality intuition.
* **Ideological libertarians** (studied specifically by Haidt, Ravi Iyer, and colleagues in *Understanding Libertarian Morality*) actually have a very distinct psychological profile. They score lower than both liberals and conservatives on emotional empathy and group loyalty, higher on **need for cognition** (enjoying abstract, systemizing thought), and treat **individual liberty** as their almost sole moral foundation.

### 3. The Ideological Minority: Systemizing vs. Motivated Reasoning
Because hardcore laissez-faire advocates (classical liberals, libertarians, anarcho-capitalists) score high on "systemizing" and abstract reasoning, many of them **do** genuinely feel like they reasoned their way there—often reading economics, philosophy, or works by Hayek, Friedman, or Rand, and breaking away from their parents' politics to do so.

However, psychologists point out two caveats even for this group:
* **Disposition precedes philosophy:** People with high individualistic, systemizing, and anti-paternalistic personality traits are naturally drawn to laissez-faire philosophy because it matches how their brains already prefer the world to work.
* **Smart people are better at rationalizing:** Research by Dan Kahan at Yale shows that people with higher analytical reasoning skills are often *more* polarized, not less, because they are much better at finding arguments to defend their preexisting worldview and poking holes in counter-evidence.

### 4. Self-Interest and Lived Experience
Beyond pure tribalism or abstract philosophy, people also gravitate toward laissez-faire views through immediate experience—for example, small business owners frustrated by compliance costs, taxes, or licensing boards. Their starting point is practical frustration, which then makes free-market ideology an attractive framework to adopt.

### Summary
* **For the average voter** who cheers for "free markets": It is mostly **tribal identity** and moral intuition around work ethic and fairness.
* **For the committed libertarian/classical liberal**: There is genuine **abstract reasoning** involved—more so than in many other political groups—but that reasoning is usually downstream of an innate, highly individualistic personality trait and is reinforced by its own intellectual subculture.
