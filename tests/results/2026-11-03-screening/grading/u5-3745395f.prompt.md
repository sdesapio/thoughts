Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No—among economists, there is a strong consensus across the ideological spectrum that broad tax cuts almost never fully pay for themselves.

Here is why, and where the idea comes from:

### 1. The Laffer Curve
The idea that tax cuts pay for themselves comes from the **Laffer Curve**, which illustrates the relationship between tax rates and tax revenue:
* At a **0% tax rate**, the government collects $0.
* At a **100% tax rate**, nobody has an incentive to work or invest legally, so the government also collects $0 (or close to it).
* Somewhere in between is a revenue-maximizing peak.

For a tax cut to completely pay for itself, existing tax rates must be **above** that revenue-maximizing peak (typically estimated by economists to be very high, often in the 60%–75%+ range for top marginal rates). In most modern economies like the U.S., overall tax rates sit on the left side of the peak—meaning cutting rates lowers total revenue.

### 2. Partial Offset ("Dynamic Scoring")
While tax cuts rarely pay for *100%* of their cost, they usually don't lose 100% of their static cost either. 
* **Static scoring** assumes people don't change their behavior when taxes change.
* **Dynamic scoring** accounts for economic growth (people working more, businesses investing more).

Nonpartisan bodies like the **Congressional Budget Office (CBO)** and the **Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT)**, as well as conservative-leaning groups like the **Tax Foundation**, generally find that broad tax cuts generate enough economic growth to offset **10% to 35%** of their initial revenue loss over a decade—leaving the remaining 65% to 90% as added deficit unless paired with spending cuts.

### 3. Historical Examples in the U.S.
Major modern U.S. tax cuts—including the **1981 Reagan tax cuts**, the **2001/2003 Bush tax cuts**, and the **2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA)**—all resulted in lower federal revenues relative to baseline projections and larger budget deficits, even during periods when the broader economy grew.

### 4. Rare Exceptions
A tax cut *can* come closer to paying for itself (or occasionally succeed) in narrow, specific cases:
* **Extreme starting rates:** Cutting a punitive marginal rate (e.g., cutting a 90% rate down to 70%, or reducing high capital gains rates that were freezing transactions).
* **Targeted incentives:** Cuts aimed purely at new business investment rather than windfalls for existing capital or consumption.
* **Tax compliance / evasion:** In countries with high tax evasion, lowering rates while broadening the base can bring shadow-economy activity into the formal tax system.
