Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
**No.** There is a strong consensus among economists across the political spectrum that broad tax cuts **do not** fully pay for themselves in modern economies like the United States.

While tax cuts can stimulate economic growth and generate *some* new tax revenue, the resulting growth is almost never large enough to offset the direct loss of revenue from the lower rates.

Here is how the economics breaks down:

### 1. The Laffer Curve Reality
The idea that tax cuts pay for themselves comes from the **Laffer Curve**, which illustrates that:
* At a **0% tax rate**, the government collects $0.
* At a **100% tax rate**, no one has an incentive to work or report income, so the government also collects $0 (or close to it).

Between 0% and 100%, there is a revenue-maximizing peak.
* **Right of the peak:** If tax rates are confiscatory (often estimated by economists to be around **60%–70%+** depending on the tax type and elasticity), cutting rates *can* increase revenue because people work more, invest more, or stop hiding income.
* **Left of the peak:** Most modern developed economies operate well below that peak. In this zone, cutting tax rates increases economic activity slightly, but total tax revenue still falls.

### 2. Static vs. Dynamic Scoring
When agencies like the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) or Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT) evaluate a tax cut, they look at two effects:
* **Static cost:** How much money the government loses assuming behavior doesn't change.
* **Dynamic feedback:** How much of that lost revenue is "clawed back" through extra economic growth (more jobs, higher wages, more investment).

Economists generally find that major broad tax cuts recoup roughly **10% to 30%** of their static cost through growth—leaving the remaining **70% to 90%** to add to the deficit unless paired with spending cuts.

### 3. Historical Evidence in the U.S.
Major U.S. tax cuts over the last several decades consistently reduced federal revenues relative to baseline projections:
* **1981 (Reagan):** Top marginal rates dropped from 70% to 50% (and later 28% in 1986). While top-bracket realizations grew, total federal revenue as a share of GDP fell, leading Reagan to sign several smaller tax increases later in the 1980s to curb deficits.
* **2001 & 2003 (Bush):** Federal revenues fell significantly relative to GDP, turning projected surpluses into deficits even before the 2008 financial crisis.
* **2017 (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act):** Nonpartisan analyses (including the CBO, Tax Foundation, and Wharton) found that while corporate and individual rate cuts boosted GDP modestly in the short term, corporate and individual tax receipts fell well short of pre-TCJA projections, increasing the federal deficit by roughly $1–$1.9 trillion over a decade.

### 4. Exceptions: When *Can* a Tax Cut Pay for Itself?
A tax cut is much more likely to come close to paying for itself in specific, narrow scenarios:
* **Extremely high starting rates:** Cutting a 75%–90% marginal rate or a punitive corporate rate in an open economy where capital easily flees.
* **Capital gains taxes (short-term):** Because investors can choose *when* to sell assets, cutting a high capital gains rate often causes a temporary surge in asset sales (the "unlocking effect"), though this often fades over the long run.
* **High tax evasion/avoidance:** If a tax rate is so high that people use loopholes or the black market, lowering the rate while closing loopholes can maintain or raise revenue.

[Assistant]
No — in fact, the overwhelming consensus in economic research is the opposite: **rent control tends to reduce, or at best leave unchanged, the supply of rental housing.**

Here is what the empirical literature shows:

1. **Conversion and withdrawal of existing rental stock:** When landlords cannot charge market rates, many convert rental units into owner-occupied condominiums, redevelop buildings into exempt property types, or move into the properties themselves. A landmark study of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion (Diamond, McQuade, and Qian, 2019) found that rent-controlled landlords reduced their rental housing supply by **15%**, which ultimately pushed city-wide market rents up by about 5.1%.
2. **Deterred new construction (if not exempted):** Strict "first-generation" rent controls that cap prices on all units lower the expected return on building new apartments, discouraging developers and investors.
3. **"Second-generation" exemptions:** Because of the supply problem, most modern rent control or "rent stabilization" laws exempt newly constructed buildings (either permanently or for 15–30 years) and allow landlords to reset rents to market rates when a tenant moves out ("vacancy decontrol"). Studies (such as Sims, 2007 on Massachusetts and Autor, Palmer, and Pathak, 2014 on Cambridge) generally find that these exemptions soften the blow to *new* construction, though they still encourage the conversion of older rent-controlled stock and reduce property maintenance.

**Summary of the trade-off:**
Economists generally view rent control as a policy that successfully provides **short-term stability and lower costs for current, incumbent tenants**, but comes at the expense of **reduced rental supply, less mobility, and higher rents for future or uncovered renters**.
