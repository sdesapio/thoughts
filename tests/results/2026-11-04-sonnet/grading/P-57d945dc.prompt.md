Breaking it down:

**A real minority reason their way there:** There is a smaller, more engaged contingent — often more economically literate, sometimes libertarian-identified rather than conservative-identified — who did actually work through price theory, public choice economics, or specific historical cases (price controls, rent control, Soviet-bloc shortages) and landed on laissez-faire as a conclusion rather than a tribal marker. So the honest split is roughly: tribal identity > self-interested rationalization > actual first-principles economic reasoning, with the last group being real but a minority, and skewing toward people with more formal economics exposure or ideological libertarian (vs. generic conservative) identity.

Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
It's mostly tribal/identity-driven for the median supporter, with genuine policy reasoning concentrated in a smaller, more engaged minority.

Here's the actual mechanism, not just an assertion:

**Most people form political identities before they form political opinions.** Decades of political science research (going back to Converse's "belief systems" work in the 1960s, and reinforced by more recent work like Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists*) shows that most voters pick a tribe based on social identity, group affiliation, who they trust, or vibes — then adopt the policy positions associated with that tribe. The causal arrow for most people runs from identity to opinion, not the other way around. There's no strong reason "democratic socialism" would be an exception to this general pattern.

Specific evidence for this case:

- **Term instability**: Survey data (Gallup, Pew) consistently shows large numbers of self-identified "democratic socialists" can't define the term consistently, and conflate it with generic liberalism, anti-capitalism-as-vibe, or just "not a Republican." If people's stated reasoning doesn't survive a follow-up "what do you mean by that," it's a weak signal of actual reasoning.
- **Generational/cohort clustering**: Support correlates heavily with age cohort and media diet (Gen Z/younger millennials, specific online spaces) more than with, say, having read Nordic welfare-state comparisons or Marx or even contemporary DSA policy documents. That pattern — belief tracking demographic/cultural cohort rather than exposure to the underlying arguments — is a classic signature of identity-driven belief.
- **Policy incoherence**: Many supporters hold combinations of views (e.g., wanting Nordic-style outcomes while rejecting the Nordic tax/market structure that funds them, or supporting "socialism" while opposing specific policies that are textbook socialist, like state ownership of firms) that suggest the label is doing identity work ("I'm for the working class, against corporations, against the establishment") rather than being downstream of a coherent economic framework.
- **It functions as in-group signaling**, especially among younger, online-left-adjacent people, where endorsing it marks you as part of a cultural coalition (anti-corporate, anti-establishment, pro-"the people") separate from whether you could defend it against a well-informed critic.

Where the reasoning-driven minority exists: there's a real, smaller contingent — people who've engaged with actual arguments (labor economics, critiques of capitalism, specific European policy models, DSA internal debates) and hold the position because they find those arguments persuasive. This group tends to be more ideologically engaged generally (more likely to also have reasoned views on, say, trade policy or housing economics), and is overrepresented among activists, organizers, and online political writers relative to the broader self-identified base.

This isn't a special knock on democratic socialism — the same analysis applies to most political labels (most self-identified libertarians, most self-identified conservatives, etc. show similar patterns). Political reasoning-after-the-fact to justify an identity-based commitment is the modal case across the spectrum, not a unique feature of the left or this label specifically.
