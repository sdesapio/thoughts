That's a provocative claim, and I think it's both partially right and unfairly narrow — because the same critique applies to virtually every political orientation, not just libertarianism.

**The grain of truth:** Most people across the political spectrum adopt their views through social environment, personality, identity, and group affiliation far more than through careful first-principles reasoning. This is well-supported by political psychology research (Jonathan Haidt's work on moral foundations, Dan Kahan's work on cultural cognition, etc.). Libertarians aren't exempt from this. Someone raised in a rural, self-reliant community or immersed in certain online subcultures may adopt libertarian language and identity the same way someone in an academic environment might adopt progressive language and identity — through osmosis and belonging, not derivation.

**Where the claim is unfair:** Singling out libertarians specifically implies other political tribes *have* reasoned their way to their positions, which is mostly false. If anything, libertarianism arguably has a stronger tradition of explicit philosophical justification (Nozick, Hayek, Friedman, Rothbard, etc.) than many mainstream political positions that people hold largely out of party loyalty or cultural habit. That doesn't mean every self-identified libertarian has read *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, but the infrastructure for reasoned adoption is there.

**A more honest framing:** For *any* political view, you'll find a spectrum — some people arrived through serious reflection, some through identity and social signaling, and most through some messy blend of both. Motivated reasoning is the human default, not a libertarian-specific failure.

So: the observation about tribal adoption isn't wrong, but presenting it as a distinctive feature of libertarianism rather than a near-universal feature of political belief is itself a kind of tribal framing.

This is a broad generalization that doesn't hold up well when applied symmetrically. A few thoughts:

**The tribalism observation applies across the spectrum.** Research in political psychology (e.g., work by Jonathan Haidt, Lilliana Mason, Dan Kahan) consistently shows that identity-driven reasoning, motivated cognition, and tribal affiliation shape political views **across all ideological groups** — conservative, progressive, libertarian, etc. Singling out progressives here isn't supported by the evidence; it's the human condition, not a left-specific phenomenon.

**"Reasoned their way there" is a high bar for anyone.** Most people's political views are shaped by a mix of personality traits, life experiences, moral intuitions, social environment, and yes, some deliberate reasoning. Very few people on *any* side arrived at their full set of positions through dispassionate first-principles analysis. That doesn't make their views invalid — it makes them human.

**The framing itself can be tribal.** Claiming "the other side" holds views as identity while implying your own side reasons more carefully is itself a common pattern of in-group bias. It feels insightful but is often just a more sophisticated form of the same tribalism it criticizes.

**That said, there's a real phenomenon worth discussing.** Social conformity pressures, particularly in certain institutional and online environments, can discourage genuine inquiry and reward signaling. This is a legitimate concern — but again, it applies to ideological monocultures of any stripe, not just progressive ones.

The more honest and productive version of the observation would be: *Most people, regardless of political orientation, hold many of their views for reasons other than pure reasoning — and all of us are susceptible to adopting positions as markers of group identity.* That framing invites self-reflection rather than just critique of an out-group.
