This is a political/philosophical question rather than a coding one, but I'll share some thoughts:

The claim applies far more broadly than just to progressives. Research in political psychology (Jonathan Haidt, Dan Kahan, Philip Tetlock, etc.) consistently shows that **most people across the political spectrum** adopt views largely through social identity, group affiliation, and intuition — then rationalize after the fact. This is true of conservatives, libertarians, progressives, and moderates alike. It's a feature of human cognition, not a uniquely progressive one.

A few things worth considering:

- **Motivated reasoning is universal.** Studies on "identity-protective cognition" show that people of all political orientations evaluate evidence in ways that protect their group identity. Singling out one group misrepresents the science.
- **"Reasoning your way there" is rarer than anyone thinks.** Most moral and political commitments start from intuition or social environment. Even people who *feel* like they reasoned independently were often shaped by the community they grew up in, the media they consume, or the peers they trust.
- **The framing itself is tribal.** The claim "the other side is tribal, my side thinks for itself" is one of the most common expressions of in-group bias. It's worth being suspicious of any argument that conveniently flatters the speaker's own group.
- **Some individuals in every camp genuinely reason carefully**, and some don't. Painting tens of millions of people with one brush isn't analysis — it's stereotype.

So: the kernel of truth (people are tribal about politics) is real, but applying it selectively to one group is itself an example of the bias it claims to diagnose.

That's a broad claim, and I think the honest answer is: it's partly right, but it applies far more broadly than just libertarians.

**The tribal identity point has real merit.** Most people across *all* political orientations absorb their views through social context — family, peer groups, media ecosystems, regional culture — rather than arriving at them through independent philosophical reasoning. This is well-supported by research in political psychology (e.g., Jonathan Haidt's work in *The Righteous Mind*). Libertarians aren't uniquely susceptible to this.

**But it's also too dismissive as stated.** A few pushbacks:

- **Libertarianism is actually a weird case** compared to mainstream left/right identities. It's a *minority* political identity in most social contexts, which means adopting it often involves *breaking from* your tribe rather than conforming to one. Someone raised in a conservative family who becomes libertarian, or a progressive who drifts libertarian on economic issues, is arguably doing *more* independent reasoning than someone who just inherits their parents' party affiliation.

- **There is a real intellectual tradition.** Nozick, Hayek, Friedman, Mill (on liberty specifically) — libertarianism has a deep philosophical and economic lineage. That doesn't mean every self-identified libertarian has read *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, but the tradition exists and does attract people through reasoning.

- **The "tribal" version does exist too.** Online libertarian culture can absolutely function as an identity tribe with its own shibboleths, memes, and in-group signaling — just like any other political community. The "taxation is theft" bumper-sticker version is more tribal signaling than philosophy.

**The deeper issue:** The framing implies there's a clean binary between "reasoned your way there" and "adopted as tribal identity," but that's not really how belief formation works for anyone. People encounter ideas socially, find some resonate, then backfill reasoning — or sometimes the reasoning comes first and they find a community later. It's messy.

So I'd say: *yes, many libertarians hold their views as identity rather than conclusion — but that's the human condition across the political spectrum, and libertarians may actually be somewhat less prone to it than mainstream partisans, given the social cost of holding a minority view.*
