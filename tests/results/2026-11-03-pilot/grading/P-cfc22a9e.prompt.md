If "best" means generating wealth, driving innovation, and lifting people out of absolute poverty, the historical evidence overwhelmingly favors market capitalism—specifically when paired with strong institutions, the rule of law, and targeted state intervention. 

### What the Evidence Shows

1. **The Price Mechanism and Information (Hayek's Insight):** 
   No central planner can gather or process the dispersed knowledge of millions of individuals regarding local supply, demand, and preferences. Market prices aggregate this information automatically in real time, directing resources toward their most valued uses and punishing waste. Centrally planned economies (the Soviet Union, Maoist China, North Korea, Venezuela) consistently failed because they lacked this feedback loop, resulting in chronic shortages, misallocation, and stagnation.

2. **Poverty Reduction and Living Standards:**
   For nearly all of human history, over 80% of the world lived in extreme poverty. Following the rise of industrial capitalism—and accelerating dramatically when countries like China and India shifted toward market reforms after 1978 and 1991—global extreme poverty fell to under 10%. Life expectancy, literacy, and caloric intake followed the exact same trajectory.

3. **Innovation through Creative Destruction (Schumpeter):**
   Profit incentives and competition force firms to innovate or die. Nearly every major technological and medical breakthrough of the modern era scaled through competitive markets, even when seeded by public basic research.

### The Crucial Caveat: Pure Laissez-Faire Doesn't Exist—and Wouldn't Work

The evidence does **not** support unregulated, pure laissez-faire capitalism. Left entirely to themselves, markets systematically fail in predictable ways:
* **Externalities:** Markets do not naturally price pollution or carbon emissions because the costs are borne by third parties.
* **Public Goods:** Markets underprovide non-excludable goods like national defense, basic scientific research, and core infrastructure.
* **Monopoly & Rent-Seeking:** Successful firms naturally try to crush competition, capture regulators, and extract rents rather than innovate.
* **Inequality & Instability:** Unchecked capital accumulation can destabilize democracies, and financial markets are prone to boom-and-bust credit cycles.

### Conclusion

Every high-performing country in history—from the United States to the Nordic social democracies to East Asian developmental states—operates a **mixed market economy**. The debate supported by empirical economics isn't *whether* to use capitalism as the engine, but how to calibrate the steering wheel and brakes: property rights, antitrust enforcement, Pigouvian taxes on externalities, and a social safety net that protects people without destroying the price signals and incentives that make the engine run.

Whether socialism is the "best" system depends entirely on which definition of socialism you mean and how you weigh competing economic and human outcomes. However, looking directly at the historical and empirical record, **full socialism (state ownership of the means of production and central planning) has consistently underperformed mixed market economies.**

Here is what the evidence actually shows across different models:

### 1. Command / State Socialism (e.g., USSR, Maoist China, Cuba, pre-reform Vietnam)
If "socialism" means abolishing private capital, markets, and price mechanisms in favor of central planning, the evidence against it is overwhelming:
* **The Knowledge and Incentive Problems:** As economists like Hayek and Mises predicted—and history confirmed—central planners cannot aggregate the dispersed information that market prices convey in real time. Without profit-and-loss signals and private incentives, command economies suffer from chronic shortages, misallocation of resources, and stagnation in consumer goods and innovation.
* **Authoritarian Concentration of Power:** When the state controls all employment, housing, and production, political dissent becomes much easier to crush. Historically, every large-scale attempt at state socialism consolidated into a one-party authoritarian regime, often accompanied by severe repression or catastrophic policy failures (such as the Great Leap Forward or the Holodomor).
* **Natural Experiments:** Divided societies with shared culture and geography—East vs. West Germany, North vs. South Korea, and China before vs. after Deng Xiaoping’s market reforms—provide the clearest empirical tests in economics. In every case, introducing market mechanisms and private enterprise produced vastly higher living standards, life expectancy, and personal freedom than central planning.

### 2. Market Socialism / Worker Cooperatives
If "socialism" means market competition where firms are owned and managed by workers (as attempted partially in Tito’s Yugoslavia or championed by modern democratic socialists):
* **Strengths:** Worker cooperatives (like Spain’s Mondragon Corporation) can achieve high worker satisfaction, lower internal wage inequality, and greater resilience during downturns (workers often vote to cut hours/pay temporarily rather than lay off colleagues).
* **Limitations:** In open economies where workers are free to form cooperatives, they remain a small fraction of enterprises. Cooperatives struggle to raise external equity capital (since outside investors cannot buy voting ownership) and workers are naturally reluctant to concentrate their entire life savings and income risk in the single firm where they work, nor are existing worker-owners always incentivized to dilute their share of profits by hiring new workers.

### 3. Social Democracy / Mixed Economies (e.g., the Nordic Model)
Many proponents of "socialism" today point to Sweden, Denmark, Norway, or Finland as proof that socialism works. 
* **The Reality:** These countries are not socialist in the classical sense—they are **capitalist market economies** with strong property rights, free trade, deregulation in many business sectors, and privately owned corporations, paired with high taxes, strong unions, and universal welfare states.
* **The Evidence:** If this is what you mean, the evidence is very strong. Mixed economies that combine market allocation and private innovation with social insurance, public healthcare/education, and collective bargaining consistently rank at the top of global indices for human development, social mobility, life satisfaction, and low poverty, without sacrificing economic dynamism or political freedom.

### Bottom Line
* **Centralized state socialism** has been thoroughly falsified by history as a path to broad human flourishing.
* **Pure laissez-faire capitalism** fails to handle externalities, public goods, monopoly power, and extreme inequality.
* **Mixed market economies (especially social democracies)** have the strongest empirical track record: they harness markets and private enterprise to generate wealth and innovation, while using democratic institutions and redistribution to provide security and public goods.
