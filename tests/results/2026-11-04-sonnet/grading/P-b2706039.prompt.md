Honestly, it's almost certainly a mix, and the balance differs by person — but a few things are probably true in general:

**The reasoning path exists and matters for some people.** Plenty of people land on democratic socialism (or any ideology) through something like: observing specific problems (healthcare costs, housing, wage stagnation), forming a causal theory about why those problems happen (concentrated capital, insufficient public provision, market failures in certain sectors), and then landing on policies/worldview that follow from that theory. For these people, the label is downstream of the reasoning, not the other way around.

**But identity and reasoning aren't cleanly separable for most people, on any side of any ideology.** A few reasons:

- **Reasoning often follows identity, not the reverse.** Social psychology research (Haidt, Kahan's "cultural cognition," motivated reasoning literature) consistently finds people adopt a group/identity first — often from family, peers, class background, or a formative experience — and then do the intellectual work of justifying it. The justification feels like reasoning from the inside, but the causal arrow frequently runs identity → belief → rationalization.
- **Most people don't have the time, information, or motivation to derive political positions from first principles.** This isn't unique to democratic socialism — it's true of basically every mass political ideology. "I thought hard about macroeconomics and concluded X" is rare for any X.
- **Democratic socialism specifically has a strong subcultural/identity component in a lot of places it's visible (certain online spaces, DSA chapters, campus politics)** — aesthetics, in-group language, shared cultural references — which makes the "tribal" dynamic especially visible for it. But the same is true of libertarianism, MAGA-aligned conservatism, effective altruism, etc. — visible subcultures tend to look more "tribal" from outside precisely because they're visible and cohesive, which isn't the same as them being more tribal than beliefs that don't have as distinct a subculture.
- **Selection matters.** People who end up in explicitly ideological movements (as opposed to just "left-leaning" or "progressive" generally) are disproportionately likely to be the more online/engaged/theory-interested subset, who are more likely to have arrived at it semi-analytically — but they're also the most visible, so outsiders may overestimate how analytically most supporters (versus the vocal core) arrived there.

So: probably a core of genuine policy/economic reasoning for a meaningful minority, surrounded by a much larger group for whom it's primarily identity/affiliation with secondary rationalization — which is roughly the pattern you'd find for most political labels, not something special about this one.

[Assistant]
Honestly, it's a mix, and the proportions vary a lot by person — but here's a reasonable breakdown:

**Genuine reasoning-based support exists, and isn't rare**
Plenty of people arrive at pro-market views through actual engagement with arguments: Hayek's knowledge problem, public choice theory, historical comparisons of planned vs. market economies, empirical work on regulation's costs/benefits, etc. Economists, policy wonks, and people who've worked through specific industries (housing, trade, licensing) often have concrete, falsifiable reasons for their views, even if you disagree with the conclusions.

**But tribal/identity dynamics are also doing a lot of work, as with most political positions**
A few mechanisms:

- **Motivated reasoning**: people often adopt the conclusion first (via upbringing, class interest, in-group signaling) and then backfill arguments. This isn't unique to laissez-faire supporters — it's true of socialists, progressives, religious conservatives, basically every identity-coded position.
- **Self-interest dressed as principle**: business owners, high earners, and people who expect to be high earners have a material incentive to favor low regulation/low taxes, independent of the empirical merits.
- **Coalition signaling**: "free markets" is bundled with other tribal markers (certain views on guns, religion, immigration, culture war stuff) in a way that has little logical connection to economics. Someone can hold the position mainly because it's part of a broader in-group package, not because they've evaluated, say, antitrust policy on its merits.
- **Asymmetric scrutiny**: people tend to interrogate arguments against their side much harder than arguments for it — a hallmark of identity-driven belief rather than pure reasoning.

**The honest general answer**
For *any* widely-held ideological position — left, right, libertarian, whatever — most adherents aren't deriving it from first principles. They're inheriting it from family/culture/peer group and then reasoning *in service of* it, with a minority of genuine first-principles thinkers at the "intellectual vanguard" who actually built the arguments. Laissez-faire capitalism isn't special in this regard; it's subject to the same sociology of belief as any other ideology.
