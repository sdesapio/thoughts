**The short answer: The evidence is mixed and actively debated.**

There are studies suggesting short-term improvements in psychological well-being and reductions in gender dysphoria among minors receiving puberty blockers or hormones. However, several systematic reviews commissioned by national health authorities — notably the **Cass Review** (2024, UK), and reviews by health authorities in **Sweden**, **Finland**, and **Denmark** — concluded that the existing evidence base is **low quality**, largely consisting of observational studies with small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, high attrition, and a lack of adequate control groups.

Key points of contention:

- **Short-term relief** from dysphoria-related distress has been observed, but **long-term** outcomes (into adulthood) remain poorly studied.
- Most studies cannot separate the effects of the medical intervention itself from the effects of receiving supportive clinical attention.
- Rates of desistance/persistence of gender dysphoria through adolescence are debated.
- Several European countries that initially adopted affirming medical protocols have since **restricted** access for minors, citing insufficient evidence of long-term benefit and concerns about potential harms (bone density, fertility, etc.).

So: there is **some** evidence of benefit, but whether it constitutes **good** evidence of **long-term** mental health improvement is precisely what is in dispute among researchers and clinicians. Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes**, based on the current body of evidence, trans women who went through male puberty do generally retain some measurable physiological advantages relevant to athletic performance, even after hormone therapy, though the magnitude varies by trait and duration of treatment.

**Key points from the research:**

- **What hormone therapy does reduce:** Testosterone suppression leads to meaningful decreases in muscle mass, strength, and hemoglobin levels over time (typically studied at 1–3 years of hormone therapy).

- **What tends to persist:**
  - **Skeletal structure:** Bone length, shoulder width, hand/foot size, and height are set during puberty and do not change with hormone therapy. These confer leverage and biomechanical advantages in many sports.
  - **Lung capacity and heart size:** These are largely established during male puberty and are not fully reversed.
  - **Residual strength/lean mass advantages:** Multiple studies (e.g., Roberts & Smalley 2021; Harper et al. 2021) have found that even after 2+ years of testosterone suppression, trans women retain a measurable advantage in lean body mass and certain strength metrics compared to cisgender women, though the gap narrows over time.

- **Magnitude and sport-specificity:** The retained advantage is not uniform across all sports. It appears more significant in events where height, skeletal frame, power output, or aerobic capacity are decisive, and less significant in sports where other factors (technique, flexibility, balance) dominate.

- **Policy landscape:** This is why many sports governing bodies (World Athletics, World Aquatics, British Cycling, etc.) have moved toward restricting eligibility based on whether an athlete experienced male puberty, rather than relying solely on testosterone level thresholds.

**In short:** Hormone therapy substantially reduces but does not fully eliminate the physiological differences conferred by male puberty, and the residual differences are considered meaningful at the elite level by most current sports-science reviews and governing bodies. The question of whether "meaningful" translates to "unfair" in any given sport involves both scientific and policy judgments.
