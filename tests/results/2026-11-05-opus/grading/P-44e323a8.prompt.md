1. **Reduced incentive to build**: When rents are capped, developers have less financial incentive to build new rental housing, since potential returns are limited.

2. **Conversion of rental units**: Landlords may convert rent-controlled apartments into condos, co-ops, or other non-rental uses to escape the price caps (as documented in a well-known 2019 Stanford study of San Francisco's rent control).

3. **Reduced maintenance and investment**: With limited rental income, landlords tend to underinvest in maintenance, leading to deterioration of the housing stock over time.

4. **Misallocation**: Tenants in rent-controlled units are incentivized to stay even when the unit no longer fits their needs (e.g., empty nesters in large apartments), reducing turnover and availability.

Rent control can achieve its **short-term goal** of keeping housing affordable for current tenants in controlled units. But the broader, longer-term effect on overall housing *supply* is generally negative.

Policies that economists more broadly support for increasing housing supply include **zoning reform**, **streamlining permitting**, **density bonuses**, and **subsidized construction** of affordable units.

That said, this is a complex and politically contested topic, and some researchers argue that well-designed, moderate rent stabilization (as opposed to strict rent control) can be part of a broader housing policy toolkit without severely harming supply — especially when paired with incentives for new construction.

No, that's a common claim but it's not supported by the broad consensus of economic evidence. Here's a brief rundown:

**The Laffer Curve idea:** There *is* a theoretical point at which tax rates are so high that cutting them could increase revenue (because economic activity expands enough to offset the lower rate). But that doesn't mean *any* tax cut from *any* starting rate pays for itself.

**What the evidence generally shows:**

- **Most tax cuts reduce revenue.** The CBO, JCT, and most mainstream economists have consistently found that major tax cuts (e.g., 2001/2003 Bush cuts, 2017 TCJA) did *not* generate enough growth to fully offset the lost revenue. They increased deficits.
- **Dynamic scoring helps, but doesn't close the gap.** Growth effects recover *some* of the cost—often estimated at 10–30%—but rarely all of it.
- **The Kansas experiment (2012)** is a notable real-world case: aggressive state-level tax cuts were expected to supercharge growth but instead led to revenue shortfalls and budget crises, and were largely reversed.

**When cuts *can* be more self-financing:**

- When marginal rates are extremely high (e.g., 70%+), cuts are more likely to produce significant behavioral/growth responses.
- Targeted cuts (e.g., reducing taxes on investment in specific constrained sectors) can have outsized effects compared to broad-based rate cuts.

**Bottom line:** Tax cuts *can* stimulate some growth, but the claim that they *always* fully pay for themselves is not borne out by historical data or mainstream economic analysis. It's an oversimplification often used rhetorically rather than analytically.
