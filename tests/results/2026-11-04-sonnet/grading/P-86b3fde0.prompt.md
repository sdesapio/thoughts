[Assistant]
No — as a universal claim, "tax cuts always pay for themselves" is false, and the evidence against it is substantial.

**The underlying claim and the theory behind it**

The idea draws on the Laffer curve: revenue = tax rate × taxable activity, and cutting rates can boost taxable activity (more work, investment, reported income) enough to offset the lower rate. That mechanism is real and uncontroversial in principle — there is some tax rate so high that cutting it would raise more revenue (the extreme case: a 100% rate produces ~$0 in activity, so cutting it from there raises revenue). The actual dispute is where current rates sit relative to that revenue-maximizing point, which is an empirical question, not a matter of definition.

**Why "always" fails**

For "always" to be true, every tax cut, at every rate, in every context, would have to sit on the "prohibitive" side of the curve. The evidence says most don't:

- **Reagan tax cuts (1981):** Revenue growth slowed and deficits rose sharply relative to pre-cut projections; even most supply-side-sympathetic retrospectives don't claim the cuts were self-financing.
- **Bush tax cuts (2001/2003):** CBO and Treasury analyses found they reduced revenue relative to baseline, contributing to deficits.
- **2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act:** The Joint Committee on Taxation's own dynamic scoring — which explicitly models growth feedback — estimated the law would recoup only a fraction (roughly a fifth to a quarter) of its static revenue loss through growth, not all of it. Realized federal revenue came in below pre-TCJA CBO projections.
- **Kansas (2012–2017):** A clean natural experiment with aggressive state income tax cuts explicitly sold as self-funding. Revenue fell far short of projections, caused a budget crisis, and the legislature repealed the cuts in 2017 over the governor's veto.

Surveys of economists (e.g., the IGM Forum/Chicago Booth panel, spanning ideological viewpoints) show near-unanimous rejection of the claim that recent major U.S. tax cuts would pay for themselves through growth.

**Where a narrower version could hold**

A much narrower, conditional claim — "a tax cut from a sufficiently confiscatory rate can increase revenue" — is defensible and has occasional historical support (e.g., some high-bracket cuts on capital gains show meaningful behavioral/timing responses, partially offsetting cost). But that's a different, bounded claim, not "tax cuts always pay for themselves."

**Verdict**

False as stated, with high confidence. It's an empirical generalization contradicted by multiple independent episodes and by the government's own dynamic-scoring estimates, which are the most sympathetic-to-growth-effects analyses available and still don't find full self-financing.

**What would change this:** a demonstration that current/proposed rates sit on the revenue-maximizing side of the Laffer curve for the specific tax and population affected — e.g., strong, well-identified evidence of behavioral responses (labor supply, investment, income-shifting) large enough to offset the static revenue loss. No such evidence exists for the major U.S. federal tax cuts cited above.

"Rent control" needs a definition: binding price ceilings on rental units (classic rent control) versus milder "rent stabilization" (caps on annual increases, often with exemptions for new construction). The economic mechanism and evidence differ somewhat by type, but neither supports "proven to increase supply."

- **Theory**: A binding price ceiling below the market-clearing rent reduces the expected return on rental housing. Standard supply-and-demand reasoning predicts landlords build less, convert units to condos/owner-occupancy, or let units deteriorate — reducing effective rental supply, not increasing it. This is about as close to textbook consensus as economics gets.
- **Empirical studies**: The most cited rigorous work here is Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (2019, *American Economic Review*) on San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion. Using exogenous assignment of rent control to small multi-family buildings, they found landlords reduced rental housing supply by 15% via conversions to condos/TICs and redevelopment, and overall city rental supply fell. Older studies on NYC and other cities similarly find reduced maintenance, reduced new construction, and conversions out of the rental stock under strict rent control regimes.
- There is debate among economists about rent control's effects on *tenant welfare* (renters who keep their units can benefit significantly) and on *affordability for incumbents* — that's a genuinely contested distributional question. But the supply effect is not contested in the same way: essentially no credible empirical study finds rent control increases housing supply, and the major field study (Diamond et al.) found the opposite.

**Where legitimate nuance exists**

- Some "rent stabilization" designs exempt new construction specifically to avoid discouraging new supply — but that's a policy carve-out working around the mechanism, not evidence the mechanism reverses.
- A few economists argue effects are smaller than textbook models predict, or that other constraints (zoning, land costs) dominate supply decisions in a given market. That's a dispute about *magnitude*, not about direction.

**Verdict**: The claim as stated is false. Rent control is not proven to increase supply — the theoretical mechanism and the best available empirical evidence (e.g., Diamond et al. 2019) indicate it reduces rental housing supply, while its effects on incumbent tenant welfare are a separate and more genuinely contested question.
