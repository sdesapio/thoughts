Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes—on average, in many elite sports, especially those emphasizing strength, speed, power, or endurance.

Male puberty creates lasting traits such as greater height, skeletal dimensions, heart and lung capacity, and muscle potential. Testosterone suppression substantially lowers hemoglobin, muscle mass, and strength, but available studies indicate it often does not eliminate all puberty-derived differences. Even a small residual advantage can matter at the elite level.

Important qualifications:

- The magnitude varies greatly by sport and by individual.
- Direct research on elite trans athletes remains limited; much evidence comes from non-elite or military populations.
- Some advantages diminish substantially with prolonged hormone therapy, while structural traits generally remain.
- This does not mean every trans woman outperforms every cis woman.

So the evidence supports a qualified **yes**, not a universal advantage in every athlete or event.

[User]
Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No**—not if “good evidence” means high-certainty evidence that puberty blockers or hormones cause sustained mental-health improvements years later.

- **Puberty blockers:** Systematic reviews, including the 2024 University of York review, found insufficient and inconsistent evidence to establish mental-health benefit.
- **Hormones:** Several observational studies report reduced depression, anxiety, or distress over roughly 1–2 years. However, confidence is low because studies are generally small, lack comparable untreated control groups, have substantial loss to follow-up, and cannot separate treatment effects from counseling, family support, or patient selection.
- **Long-term outcomes:** Robust comparative data extending five or more years are extremely limited. The older Dutch follow-up is encouraging but involved a small, highly selected cohort receiving multiple interventions, so it cannot isolate the medications’ effects.

Therefore, the evidence suggests **possible short-term benefit—more clearly for hormones than blockers—but does not provide strong evidence of long-term mental-health improvement**. This does not prove the treatments are ineffective; it means the long-term causal benefit remains uncertain. Major medical organizations differ mainly in how they translate that uncertainty into clinical policy.
