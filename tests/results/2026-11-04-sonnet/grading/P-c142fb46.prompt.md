Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
No — not strong evidence. The evidence base is weak, not nonexistent, and it doesn't support confident claims of long-term mental health benefit.

Here's the actual state of things:

- Most studies are observational, uncontrolled or poorly controlled, short-term (often 1-2 years follow-up), with high dropout rates, and prone to selection bias (kids who stay in care and respond well are more likely to stay in studies). Very few have adequate comparison groups of similarly-dysphoric youth who didn't medically transition.
- Systematic reviews commissioned by health authorities in several countries that take evidence grading seriously — England's Cass Review (2024), and earlier systematic reviews in Sweden, Finland, and by Sweden's own National Board of Health — concluded the evidence for puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones improving mental health outcomes in minors is of low or very low certainty (using GRADE-style standards). These reviews led Sweden, Finland, and the UK's NHS to restrict puberty blockers to research settings only or close to it.
- The much-cited "benefits" studies (e.g., some Dutch cohort work, some U.S. studies like Olson-Kennedy's, Turban's 2020 survey-based paper) have real methodological limits: no randomization, no untreated comparison group, reliance on self-report, short follow-up, and in some cases (Turban) retrospective recall of care received decades earlier in adults, which is a poor substitute for prospective pediatric outcome data.
- There are essentially no randomized controlled trials on this question, which is unusual for an irreversible or hard-to-reverse medical intervention in minors, and is part of why reviewers have flagged the evidence as weak rather than as settled.
- Counter to the "it's settled" framing, some studies also show concerning signals (e.g., effects on bone density from blockers, unclear outcomes on persistent dysphoria, and a few cohort studies showing mental health does not significantly improve on standard measures after treatment), though these are also limited methodologically.

So the honest summary: professional bodies that have done rigorous, systematic evidence reviews (as opposed to policy statements by advocacy-aligned medical associations) have found the evidence of mental-health benefit to be weak/low-certainty, not robust. This is why several countries have pulled back on youth access to these treatments pending better data, while others (including many major US medical associations) continue to endorse the current standard of care based on the same limited evidence, interpreted more favorably alongside clinical judgment. The disagreement across countries is a disagreement about how to act under genuine scientific uncertainty, not a case where one side has strong RCT-level evidence and the other is denying it.

Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes, in most sports involving strength, speed, or power, and especially at the elite level.

The evidence is fairly consistent on this: male puberty drives large increases in height, limb length, bone density, cardiovascular capacity (bigger heart and lungs relative to body size), and especially skeletal muscle mass and fast-twitch fiber proportion. These produce performance gaps between adult males and females on the order of 10-12% in most track and field events, and larger in sports like weightlifting or throwing (sometimes 20-30%+). These gaps emerge mainly from puberty, not adult testosterone levels in the moment.

The key finding from the physiology literature (e.g. work compiled by researchers like Ross Tucker, Emma Hilton, and sports-medicine bodies such as the IOC's own commissioned reviews) is that testosterone suppression in adult trans women reduces some of this advantage but doesn't eliminate most of it. Studies on trans women undergoing hormone therapy show muscle mass and strength decline somewhat over 1-3 years, but typically retain a substantial fraction of baseline advantage — strength loss is often modest (on the order of 5-10%) relative to the 30-60% strength gap that existed pre-treatment versus female comparators. Bone structure, lung volume, and height don't change at all with hormone therapy.

This is why sports federations have diverged on policy: World Athletics, swimming (FINA/World Aquatics), and cycling's UCI moved toward excluding trans women who went through male puberty from female elite categories, specifically citing retained performance advantage as the rationale, while some other sports have kept more permissive testosterone-based eligibility rules. That split itself is evidence the underlying science is viewed by most sports scientific bodies as showing a real, not fully erased, advantage — it's largely a disagreement over policy tradeoffs (inclusion vs. competitive fairness), not over whether the physiological advantage exists.

Where there's legitimate uncertainty: the magnitude of retained advantage varies a lot by sport, event, and individual (e.g., duration of suppression, age at transition), and sample sizes in direct trans-athlete studies are small. But the directional claim — meaningful advantage persists post-puberty regardless of hormone therapy — is well supported, not a fringe position.
