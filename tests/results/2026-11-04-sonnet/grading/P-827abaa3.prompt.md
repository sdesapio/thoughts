So: a loud, confident, identity-driven majority, and a quieter, more hedged, actually-reasoned minority — which is roughly the pattern for most strongly-held political-economic positions across the spectrum, not something specific to this one.

Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
For most people, it's tribal/identity first, reasoning second — and that's not a knock on this group specifically, it's how political belief formation works for the large majority of people across basically all ideological labels.

Here's the honest breakdown:

**The mechanism for most people:** You absorb a cluster of cultural signals — who your friends are, what media you consume, what feels morally obvious to people you respect, what identity you want to project — and you land on "democratic socialism" as the label that fits the cluster. Then you backfill reasons. The reasons aren't fake, they're just not causally prior. Ask someone to steelman the opposing view or explain the actual empirical track record of Nordic vs. US welfare-capitalism vs. historical socialist economies, and most people — again, across most political labels — struggle, because the belief was never built from that material.

**Evidence this is true here specifically:**
- Polling consistently shows most self-identified democratic socialists in the US can't define it in a way that distinguishes it from standard European social democracy, and many conflate it with "socialism" generally despite DSA literature insisting on a hard line between the two.
- A lot of the recent growth in the label (post-2015, Sanders/AOC-driven) tracks generational and media-exposure patterns far better than it tracks, say, people reading Erik Olin Wright or doing comparative economic analysis. That's an identity-diffusion pattern, not a reasoning pattern.
- The label functions socially — it signals "I'm not a neoliberal Democrat," "I care about inequality," "I'm young and online" — independent of its technical content (worker ownership of means of production, etc.), which most adherents don't actually advocate for when pressed.

**Where real reasoning does show up:** There's a smaller, genuinely ideological core — people who've read the theory, have actual views on labor economics, cite specific policy mechanisms (sectoral bargaining, co-determination, public banking) — who reason their way there or at least hold reasoned positions once there. This group is a minority of the labeled population, same as the minority of any -ism who've actually done the reading.

**The symmetry point, briefly:** This isn't special to the left. Self-identified libertarians, "classical liberals," MAGA-identified conservatives — same pattern, same ratio roughly. Identity-first belief formation with post-hoc rationalization is the norm for mass political identity, not an exception carved out for socialists. If anything, movements built heavily on youth/online growth (which democratic socialism in the US currently is) skew even further toward identity-first, because the recruitment pathway is social/cultural rather than through institutions that demand doctrinal literacy (unions, party apparatuses, study groups), which were the old recruitment path and did produce more reasoned adherents.

So: tribal/identity-driven for the majority, reasoned for a minority, and the ratio isn't unusual compared to other political identities — it's just more visible right now because the label is young and growing fast.
