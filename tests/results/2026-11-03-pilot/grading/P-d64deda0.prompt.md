Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes—on average, in many elite sports.

Male puberty produces lasting advantages in height, skeletal dimensions, leverage, heart and lung capacity, muscle mass, and strength. Testosterone suppression lowers hemoglobin and reduces some muscle mass, but it generally does not erase all puberty-derived advantages—especially skeletal size and upper-body strength.

Important limits:

- The magnitude varies greatly by sport and event.
- Evidence involving actual elite trans athletes is limited.
- This is a population-level conclusion, not a claim that every trans woman outperforms every female athlete.
- It applies to athletes who completed male puberty, not necessarily those who received puberty blockers.

Because elite competition is decided by very small margins, even partial retention can be meaningful. That supports sport-specific eligibility rules, though the evidence alone does not determine the broader ethical or policy choice.

[User]
Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
No—if “good evidence” means reliable evidence of sustained mental-health improvement into adulthood.

Some observational studies report reduced depression, distress, or suicidality after treatment, especially with hormones. But most have short follow-up, small or selected samples, substantial loss to follow-up, no adequate comparison group, and confounding from psychotherapy, family support, socioeconomic circumstances, and baseline health. These limitations make causation uncertain.

Systematic reviews—including NICE reviews and the 2024 University of York reviews—generally found:

- Puberty blockers: insufficient evidence to determine their long-term mental-health effects.
- Hormones: some evidence of short-term psychological improvement, particularly depression, but little robust evidence about long-term outcomes.

That does not establish that treatment is ineffective or harmful. It means the long-term mental-health benefit remains unresolved, and claims of either proven benefit or proven absence of benefit go beyond the evidence. Individual treatment decisions must therefore weigh uncertain psychological benefits against physical effects, alternatives, the severity and persistence of dysphoria, and the young person’s circumstances.
