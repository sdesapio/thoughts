### 1. Conversion and Withdrawal of Existing Rentals
When price caps reduce the return on rental properties, landlords often pull units out of the rental market by converting them into owner-occupied condominiums, selling them as single-family homes, or redeveloping them to qualify for exemptions. 
* **San Francisco (Diamond, McQuade, & Qian, 2019):** A landmark Stanford study of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion found that while it protected incumbent tenants from displacement, affected landlords reduced their supply of rental housing by **15%** (mostly by converting units to condos or redeveloping buildings), which ultimately pushed citywide market rents up by 5.1%.
* **Cambridge, MA (Autor, Palmer, & Pathak, 2014):** Following the repeal of rent control in 1995, previously controlled properties saw substantial increases in residential investment, maintenance, and property values, reversing years of underinvestment.

### 2. Deterrence of New Construction
Strict ("first-generation") rent control that caps rents on all units directly lowers the expected return on building new apartments, leading developers to build less or shift toward for-sale condos.
* **St. Paul, MN (2021):** After voters passed a strict 3% rent cap that initially included new construction with no exemptions, multi-family building permits dropped sharply (by roughly 40–80% depending on the baseline comparison) while neighboring Minneapolis saw a construction boom—prompting St. Paul to roll back the rules for new builds.

### 3. "Second-Generation" Rent Control (Rent Stabilization)
Recognizing the supply problem, most modern rent control policies—like those in Oregon, California, or New York—exempt new construction (often for 15–30 years) and allow landlords to reset rents to market rates when a tenant moves out ("vacancy decontrol").
* Even with these exemptions, these policies **do not increase** housing supply; at best, they are designed to *minimize the negative impact* on new construction. However, they can still discourage development if investors fear lawmakers will later shorten the exemption window (as has happened in New York and St. Paul).

### Summary
Rent control functions as an insurance policy against rent spikes and displacement for **current tenants** in covered units, and the evidence shows it succeeds at that specific goal in the short to medium term. However, it does not increase the housing supply—it reliably shrinks the available pool of rental units, reduces mobility, and tends to make non-controlled housing more expensive for new renters.

Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. Across both economic theory and decades of empirical evidence, broad tax cuts do not pay for themselves. They almost always reduce government revenue and increase the deficit.

Here is what the evidence shows:

### 1. The Laffer Curve and Current Tax Rates
The idea that tax cuts pay for themselves comes from the **Laffer Curve**, which holds that if tax rates are extremely high (approaching 100%), cutting them can stimulate enough economic activity (or reduce tax evasion/avoidance enough) to increase total revenue. 

However, economists across the political spectrum agree that U.S. and most other advanced economies operate on the **left side** of the Laffer Curve's peak. At current tax rates, the behavioral response (people working or investing more) is nowhere near large enough to offset the direct loss of revenue from charging a lower rate on existing economic activity.

### 2. What Dynamic Scoring Shows
When nonpartisan agencies and think tanks—such as the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), the Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT), the Tax Policy Center, and the Penn Wharton Budget Model—evaluate tax cuts using "dynamic scoring" (which accounts for the macroeconomic growth caused by the cuts), they typically find that:
* Growth effects offset only **5% to 30%** of the static revenue loss for broad income or corporate tax cuts.
* Furthermore, if tax cuts are financed by borrowing, the resulting increase in federal debt eventually crowds out private investment and raises interest rates, which dampens long-term growth and further shrinks that offset.

### 3. Historical Evidence in the U.S.
Major federal tax cuts consistently resulted in lower federal revenues as a share of GDP compared to pre-cut baselines:
* **1981 (Reagan):** The Economic Recovery Tax Act significantly reduced revenues; the administration and Congress subsequently passed multiple tax increases between 1982 and 1984 to claw back roughly a third to half of the original cut.
* **2001 & 2003 (Bush):** Federal revenue fell sharply, turning projected surpluses into persistent deficits even after accounting for the 2001 recession.
* **2017 (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act):** Despite strong promises that corporate rate cuts would pay for themselves, CBO data shows corporate tax receipts dropped by roughly 30%–40% below pre-TCJA projections in the years immediately following enactment, and total federal revenues fell as a percentage of GDP.

### Are there any exceptions?
Self-financing tax cuts are only plausible in narrow, specific circumstances:
* **Extreme marginal rates:** Cutting a top marginal rate from 90% to 70%, especially when accompanied by closing loopholes (which expands the tax base).
* **Highly elastic taxes:** Occasionally, reducing very high capital gains tax rates can trigger a short-term surge in realizations (people selling assets they were holding onto to avoid taxes), though this is mostly a timing shift rather than permanent new growth.

For broad-based income, payroll, or corporate tax cuts at modern rates, the consensus in empirical economics is clear: they may boost short-term demand or alter incentives, but they cost far more in lost revenue than they generate in new growth.
