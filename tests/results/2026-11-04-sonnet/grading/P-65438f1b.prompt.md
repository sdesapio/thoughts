No — that's backwards. The empirical consensus, including from economists generally sympathetic to tenant protections, is that rent control reduces housing supply, not increases it.

The mechanism is straightforward: price controls below market rate reduce the incentive to build new rental units, convert existing units to rentals, or maintain/renovate existing rental stock. Landlords often respond by converting units to condos, short-term rentals, or owner use, or by deferring maintenance. The most cited modern evidence is Diamond, McQuade, and Qian's study of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion, which found it caused a roughly 15% reduction in rental housing supply in the affected buildings, as landlords converted properties to condos or redevelopment to escape the controls. That decline in supply also pushed up citywide rents by making the market tighter overall.

What rent control does reliably deliver is protection for incumbent tenants in covered units — lower displacement, more housing stability, sometimes lower rent growth for those specific tenants. That's a real benefit, and it's the main argument in its favor. But it comes at a cost to overall supply and to renters trying to find units (outsiders), not as a boost to supply.

So the claim as stated is false, and it's really the opposite of what the research shows.

No — that's not supported by the evidence.

The claim that tax cuts "pay for themselves" (via the Laffer curve / supply-side logic) has been tested repeatedly, and the near-universal finding from economists across the political spectrum, including conservative ones, is that it's false for virtually all real-world tax cuts in the U.S.

Key points:

- **Theoretically possible, practically rare**: The Laffer curve is real in principle — at extremely high tax rates (think 90%+), cutting rates can increase revenue by boosting work/investment incentives enough to offset the rate cut. But actual U.S. tax rates have never been near that threshold in the modern era.
- **Empirical track record**: The major U.S. tax cuts of the last 50 years (Reagan 1981/1986, Bush 2001/2003, Trump 2017) were all followed by lower revenue than would have occurred without them, and larger deficits — not smaller. Studies from the CBO, Treasury, Federal Reserve, and academic economists (including Greg Mankiw, a Republican and former Bush economic adviser, who called the "pay for themselves" claim something peddled by "charlatans and cranks") consistently find this.
- **The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act** is a recent clean test case: growth and investment effects were modest, and revenue came in well below pre-cut projections, consistent with CBO's deficit-increase estimates, not with self-financing.
- **Economic consensus**: Surveys of economists (e.g., the IGM Forum/Chicago Booth polls) show overwhelming disagreement with the idea that broad-based tax cuts are typically revenue-neutral or self-financing through growth.

So the honest answer: tax cuts can generate some growth and partially offset their cost (dynamic scoring usually finds cuts recoup maybe 10-30% of static revenue loss, depending on the cut), but "always pay for themselves" is not an empirically defensible claim — it's a talking point that doesn't hold up against the data.
