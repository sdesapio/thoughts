[Assistant]
There's something true in there, but as stated it's overbroad and asymmetric in a way that doesn't hold up.

What's true: political belief formation for most people, across the spectrum, is heavily driven by identity, social belonging, upbringing, and group signaling rather than first-principles reasoning. This is well-documented — Jonathan Haidt's work on moral intuitions preceding moral reasoning, motivated reasoning research, the "politics as team sport" literature. People tend to absorb the worldview of their family, peer group, region, religion, or class, then backfill justifications. This happens on the left and right and in the center.

What's not right: singling out progressives as uniquely tribal implies conservatives, moderates, or libertarians arrived at their views through cleaner reasoning, and there's no good evidence for that asymmetry. Every political tendency has:

- A sociological profile (progressives cluster in cities, among the young, college-educated, certain professions; conservatives cluster in rural areas, among the religious, certain industries) that predicts belief at least as well as "they reasoned it out."
- Identity-signaling mechanisms (flags, slogans, in-group language, outgroup contempt) — present just as vividly in conservative, nationalist, or religious movements as in progressive ones.
- A subset of genuinely reasoned adherents who can defend their views with evidence and have changed their minds on specific issues.

A few other wrinkles worth considering:

- "Reasoned their way there" is a high bar almost nobody clears on complex, value-laden political questions — not because people are uniquely lazy, but because most political positions aren't pure logic problems; they rest on prior value commitments (how much you weight equality vs. liberty vs. tradition, etc.) that aren't themselves derived from reasoning.
- Tribal identity and reasoning aren't mutually exclusive. Someone can adopt a group identity early and then do real reasoning within that framework, sometimes updating specific positions while keeping the broader identity.
- The claim is more defensible as "political belief is substantially identity-driven for most people, on all sides" than as a claim specifically about progressives.

If you want, I can point to specific research (Haidt, Converse's "belief systems" work, or the social identity theory literature) that digs into this more.

[User]
Most people who hold libertarian political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
There's real truth in this, but it's not unique to libertarians, and stating it that way flattens something more interesting.

**The part that's right:** Political identity in general functions tribally for most people, most of the time. Decades of political psychology (Converse's work on "non-attitudes," Achen and Bartels' *Democracy for Realists*, Haidt's moral foundations research) support the idea that people usually arrive at a group identity first — through family, subculture, a charismatic book or figure, a social scene — and backfill reasons afterward. Motivated reasoning is the norm, not the exception. This applies to liberals, conservatives, socialists, and libertarians alike. There's nothing about libertarianism that makes its adherents more susceptible to this than anyone else.

**Where "most people" claims get shaky:** It's an empirical claim about a population you probably haven't surveyed. It's the kind of thing that's unfalsifiable in practice and suspiciously convenient — it lets you dismiss a view without engaging its strongest version. The same rhetorical move ("they didn't reason their way there, it's identity") gets lobbed at every ideology by its opponents. If you'd accept it as a knock against libertarianism, consider whether you'd accept the identical claim aimed at your own views.

**A few relevant wrinkles specific to libertarianism:**

- It's unusually "derivable" from a few axioms (self-ownership, non-aggression) compared to, say, modern liberalism or conservatism, which are more coalitional bundles of unrelated positions (abortion + taxes + foreign policy + gun rights have no logical connection). That axiomatic quality makes libertarianism *look* more reasoned when people perform it, even when the person didn't actually derive it themselves. You see people quote Rothbard or Nozick syllogistically — that's a style of adopted identity that mimics reasoning more closely than "I'm pro-life and pro-gun because that's my team."
- Libertarianism also has a disproportionate share of people who *did* arrive via a real intellectual path (usually economics or a specific book — *Atlas Shrugged*, *Free to Choose*, Mises) rather than family-of-origin politics, since it's rarely the default of one's upbringing or local culture in most places. That doesn't mean the reasoning was good, but it's less purely tribal-by-inheritance than, say, "I vote how my parents and church vote."
- At the same time, online libertarianism has genuine tribal markers — aesthetics, memes, in-group signaling (gadsden flags, "taxation is theft," NAP recitations) — that function exactly like any other subculture's badges, often decoupled from whether the person could defend the underlying economics or philosophy under pressure.

**Bottom line:** The mechanism you're describing (identity-first, reasoning-after) is real and near-universal across ideologies, not a special libertarian pathology. If your actual target is "a lot of libertarians recite slogans without being able to defend them," that's probably true and worth saying directly — but it's true of most partisans of most stripes, and singling out libertarians specifically suggests the frame might be doing some tribal work of its own.
