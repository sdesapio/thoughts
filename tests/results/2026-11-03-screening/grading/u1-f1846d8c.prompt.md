Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
In political science and psychology, the consensus is that **both dynamics are at play**, and this applies not just to democratic socialists, but to almost every political movement. 

How people arrive at—and hold onto—democratic socialism breaks down into three main factors:

### 1. Material Reasoning and Policy Preferences
Many supporters arrive at the position through lived economic experience or specific policy goals rather than abstract theory:
* **Pocketbook issues:** High costs of healthcare, student debt, housing unaffordability, and stagnant wages lead many (especially younger generations who entered the workforce after the 2008 financial crisis) to conclude that the current market system isn't working for them.
* **Comparative models:** Many point to Nordic countries (social democracies like Denmark, Sweden, or Norway) as practical proof that universal healthcare, tuition-free higher education, and strong labor unions can coexist with high living standards and democratic freedoms. For these voters, "reasoning their way there" simply means wanting those specific outcomes.

### 2. Tribal Identity and Social Sorting
At the same time, empirical political science (such as the work of Philip Converse, Donald Kinder, and Lilliana Mason) shows that **most voters across the entire political spectrum do not hold deeply reasoned, internally consistent ideological frameworks.** Instead:
* **Identity comes first:** People often adopt a political label because it aligns with their peer group, generational cohort, online community, or cultural values, and then adopt the policy positions associated with that group.
* **Negative partisanship:** For many on the younger left, identifying as a "democratic socialist" is a way to signal distance from both conservatives and the establishment Democratic Party, functioning as a badge of moral and cultural identity.
* **Aesthetic and community:** Organizations, podcasts, and social media spaces built around democratic socialism provide a sense of belonging, shared moral purpose, and an "in-group" vocabulary.

### 3. The "Label" Confusion
Survey data (such as polling from Pew Research and Gallup) highlights a major quirk in how Americans use the term:
* When asked what "socialism" means to them, a majority of modern American supporters do **not** define it in the classical Marxist sense (government or worker ownership of the means of production). 
* Instead, they define it as **social democracy** (a market economy with a generous welfare state and regulated capitalism) or even simply as "fairness," "equality," or "people being kind to one another."

### Summary
A smaller subset of activists and theorists arrive at democratic socialism through deliberate ideological and economic study. For the broader base of supporters, it is usually a **mix of genuine material grievance** (wanting affordable healthcare and education) **wrapped in a generational and cultural identity** that uses the label to signal dissatisfaction with the status quo.

Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
In political psychology and public opinion research, the short answer is **both, depending heavily on which segment of supporters you are looking at**—and this pattern applies across almost all political ideologies, not just laissez-faire capitalism.

Researchers generally divide the analysis into two distinct groups: **everyday partisans** and **ideological purists (libertarians/classical liberals)**.

---

### 1. For the Broad Public: Mostly Tribal Identity & Elite Cues
For the majority of voters who express support for free-market policies, political scientists (such as Philip Converse, John Zaller, and Lilliana Mason) find that **tribal identity and partisan cues** do most of the heavy lifting:

* **"Identity-Protective Cognition" (Dan Kahan):** Most people adopt the economic views of their cultural or political tribe to maintain social standing within their group. In the U.S. and similar democracies, free-market rhetoric became fused with conservative cultural identity during the Cold War and the Reagan/Thatcher eras.
* **Elite Cues:** Studies consistently show that when party leaders flip their stance on an economic issue (e.g., free trade, tariffs, deficits, or corporate subsidies), the base largely follows suit within a few years. If support were purely reasoned from first principles, support for laissez-faire wouldn't fluctuate so dramatically when a populist leader takes over a right-leaning party.
* **Symbolic vs. Operational Ideology:** Political scientists Christopher Ellis and James Stimson showed that many people are **"symbolically"** pro-free-market (they like the *idea* and label of capitalism, small government, and rugged individualism) while being **"operationally"** supportive of government intervention (favoring Social Security, Medicare, farm subsidies, tariffs, or bailouts when it benefits their in-group).

---

### 2. For Committed Libertarians: Systematizing & Moral Intuition
When psychologists study self-identified **libertarians**—people who consistently support laissez-faire capitalism even when it conflicts with conservative cultural tribes—the picture looks very different.

In a landmark 2012 study (*"Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians"*), Jonathan Haidt, Ravi Iyer, and colleagues surveyed over 11,000 libertarians and found distinct psychological traits:

* **High Need for Cognition & Systematizing:** Compared to both liberals and conservatives, committed laissez-faire advocates scored significantly higher on measures of **cognitive reflection**, **need for cognition** (enjoyment of abstract thinking), and **systematizing** (a preference for rule-based, mechanistic systems over empathy-based narratives). This group *does* spend a disproportionate amount of time explicitly reasoning through economic models, incentives, and philosophical axioms (like Hayek's knowledge problem or Nozick's self-ownership).
* **Low Group-Binding Morality:** Unlike social conservatives, who score high on in-group loyalty, respect for authority, and sanctity, libertarians scored very low on these tribal foundations—and also lower than liberals on emotional empathy. Their moral matrix rests almost entirely on **individual liberty** (reactance against coercion) and **proportional fairness** (getting what you earn).
* **Post-Hoc Rationalization Still Applies:** Even among high-reasoning libertarians, Haidt's **Social Intuitionist Model** suggests that people rarely start as a blank slate and deduce an ideology from pure logic. Instead, individuals born with a strong psychological disposition toward autonomy, high reactance to authority, and a preference for rule-based systems gravitate toward laissez-faire philosophy because it matches their temperament—and *then* use their high analytical skills to build elaborate intellectual frameworks around it.

---

### 3. Material Self-Interest & Attribution Bias
A third factor sits between pure reason and pure tribalism: **lived experience and cognitive bias**.
* People who succeed in competitive private markets (entrepreneurs, finance professionals, business owners) naturally see the market as a fair allocator of rewards and government regulation as a friction or impediment.
* Psychologically, the **fundamental attribution error** leads successful individuals to attribute their success to their own agency while viewing state intervention as an arbitrary distortion.

---

### Summary
* **Casual/Mainstream supporters:** Largely **tribal and symbolic**. They adopt free-market talking points as badges of cultural/partisan identity and readily abandon laissez-faire principles (like free trade or open labor markets) when their tribe shifts.
* **Committed/Ideological supporters:** Much more **reason-driven and anti-tribal**, characterized by a high preference for abstract, rule-based systems—though that reasoning is still anchored in an underlying temperament that prizes individual autonomy above group cohesion.
