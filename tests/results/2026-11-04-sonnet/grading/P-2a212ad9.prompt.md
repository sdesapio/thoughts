If reasoning were doing the driving, you'd expect views to shift substantially after people actually engage with the counter-evidence (e.g., empirical work on minimum wage, market failures, historical deregulation outcomes). - **Most people can't actually explain the underlying theory.** Ask a typical "the free market solves everything" person to explain, say, why markets fail to price externalities, or what the actual empirical literature says about historical child labor laws or trust-busting outcomes, and most can't engage past slogans. That's consistent with a position adopted as identity marker and then backfilled with whatever arguments are handy, not one arrived at through study.

- **The content of the belief often looks like tribal signaling.** Laissez-faire capitalism is bundled with other, logically unrelated positions (gun rights, opposition to immigration enforcement overreach, certain religious views, skepticism of climate science) in a way that a purely principled reasoning process wouldn't necessarily produce. Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
It's mostly tribal/identity-driven for the median supporter, with a reasoned core at the center.

Here's the honest breakdown:

**For most people, political affiliation precedes policy reasoning, not the other way around.** This isn't a dig specific to democratic socialism — it's just how political cognition works for the large majority of people across the spectrum. People adopt a social identity (often tied to age cohort, education, urban/rural geography, online subculture), absorb the associated vocabulary and villains, and then back-fill justifications. Survey research consistently shows most self-identified "socialists" in the US, especially younger ones, can't define the term in a way that distinguishes it from generic social democracy or even just "government helps people more." Ask people to explain what specific policy mechanism they're endorsing versus just naming vibes/values ("anti-greed," "anti-billionaire," "fairness") and most land on vibes.

**There is a real reasoned minority.** Some people — often the ones who write the theory, run the DSA chapters, or at least read Mason/Bernie-adjacent policy explainers — have an actual model: critiques of capital ownership concentration, specific views on market failures, historical arguments about New Deal/Nordic models vs. actual socialism (worker ownership of the means of production) vs. welfare-state capitalism. This group is a minority of self-identified supporters but disproportionately shapes the public-facing arguments, which creates an illusion that the reasoning is more widespread than it is.

**Why this looks worse for democratic socialism specifically than for, say, mainstream liberalism or conservatism:** it's a newer, rapidly-growing identity label (especially post-2016, post-Bernie) that's absorbed a lot of people reacting against something (inequality, 2008, student debt, housing costs) rather than people who independently built up to the label through ideological study. Fast-growing identity labels attract more "joined because of vibes/community" adoption relatively to old, stable labels where people's affiliation had decades to calcify into post-hoc rationalized "reasoning." That's a dynamic of novelty and growth rate, not something inherent to the ideology's merits or flaws.

**The caveat that keeps this honest:** the same pattern holds for most people who call themselves conservatives, libertarians, or mainstream Democrats/Republicans. Political scientists (Converse's work on "belief systems," more recent work by Achen and Bartels in *Democracy for Realists*) have shown most voters don't have stable, logically consistent ideological structures — they have group loyalties and absorb positions from the group. Democratic socialism isn't uniquely tribal; it's just a legible example of a near-universal phenomenon because it's young enough that you can still see the identity-formation happening in real time, rather than it being decades-old and already naturalized.
