[Assistant]
Partly right, but the framing singles out libertarians for something that's true of virtually every political identity.

**The general point is well-established.** Most people holding *any* political views didn't reason their way there independently. Political psychology (Haidt, Kahan, etc.) consistently shows that people adopt political positions through social identity, personality, upbringing, and group affiliation, then backfill justifications. This is the norm, not the exception, across left, right, and center.

**The specific claim about libertarians is weaker than the general one.** Libertarianism is actually a minority contrarian position in most social contexts. You usually don't absorb it passively from family, church, or neighborhood the way you might absorb mainstream left or right views. Getting there typically requires *some* active engagement with ideas — reading Hayek, encountering Rothbard, watching a debate, whatever. That's a low bar, but it's higher than the bar for adopting whatever your parents or peer group believe.

**That said, libertarianism absolutely has tribal dynamics.** There's a recognizable aesthetic, in-group vocabulary, canonical texts, community spaces, and identity signaling. And ironically, one of the strongest tribal markers in libertarianism is the self-image of being uniquely rational — "I *reasoned* my way here unlike those partisan sheep." That self-image is itself often adopted uncritically as part of the package.

**Bottom line:** The claim is true enough to be worth saying, but it's not a distinctive indictment of libertarians. It's a restatement of how political identity works for almost everyone. If someone holds it up as evidence that libertarianism specifically is less reasoned than other positions, that's where the claim outruns the evidence.

Jonathan Haidt's work in *The Righteous Mind* makes a compelling case that moral/political intuitions come first and reasoning follows as post-hoc justification. Dan Kahan's research on cultural cognition shows that people across the spectrum process factual information through the lens of their group identity. This isn't a left-wing disease; it's a human one.

Where your framing goes wrong is the implied contrast — as if conservatives, libertarians, or any other group are predominantly reasoning from first principles. They're not. People who identify as conservative are just as likely to have absorbed their views from Fox News, their church, their community, or their family as progressives are to have absorbed theirs from their college, their social circle, or their media environment. The tribal signaling, in-group loyalty, and motivated reasoning operate on all sides.

There are real phenomena worth criticizing: social desirability pressure in progressive spaces, the speed at which new orthodoxies get adopted (sometimes faster than anyone could have reasoned through them), and the social costs of dissent in certain progressive communities. These are legitimate observations. But they describe *any* group with strong social cohesion and norm enforcement — religious communities, military culture, online right-wing spaces — not something unique to progressivism.

The honest version of your claim: "Most people hold their political views largely as tribal identity rather than through independent reasoning." That's well-supported. Singling out progressives for it is itself a tribal move.
