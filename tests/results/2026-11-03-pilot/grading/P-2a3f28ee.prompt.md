Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes**, in sports where strength, power, speed, or aerobic capacity are primary determinants of performance, trans women who underwent male puberty retain a meaningful biological advantage over cisgender females on average, even after multi-year testosterone suppression. Whether that retained advantage holds in every specific elite sport, and how governing bodies should weigh it against inclusion, are separate empirical and policy questions.

### 1. Definitions and Scope
* **Population:** Trans women who completed male puberty (Tanner stages 4–5) prior to initiating gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) with testosterone suppression (typically to $<1.7\text{–}2.5\text{ nmol/L}$ for 12–36+ months).
* **Meaningful athletic advantage:** A retained sex-linked physiological difference that exceeds typical competitive margins at the elite level (where podium finishes are frequently separated by $0.5\%\text{–}2\%$), compared to the reference population of cisgender females.

### 2. Physiological Mechanism and Evidence
Male puberty produces two classes of athletic differentiation from female puberty, which normally creates a $10\%\text{–}15\%$ performance gap in running and swimming and a $20\%\text{–}50\%$ gap in strength and power events (Hilton & Lundberg, *Sports Medicine*, 2021; Harper et al., *BJSM*, 2021):

1. **Irreversible or minimally reversible structural traits:** Skeletal architecture—including greater height, longer limb levers, narrower pelvic width-to-femur ratio, larger hand/foot surface area, and larger thoracic/lung volumes—is fixed once epiphyseal plates close. Testosterone suppression in adulthood does not reduce bone length or airway/lung geometry.
2. **Partially reversible soft-tissue and hematological traits:**
   * **Hemoglobin / oxygen transport:** This is the exception that *does* normalize. Within 4–6 months of suppressing testosterone into the female reference range, hemoglobin concentration drops by roughly $10\%\text{–}14\%$ to match cisgender female levels (Harper et al., 2021; Roberts et al., *BJSM*, 2021).
   * **Lean body mass, muscle cross-sectional area, and strength:** Longitudinal studies over 1 to 3+ years of GAHT consistently show only modest reductions in muscle mass and strength—typically $4\%\text{–}10\%$—leaving a substantial portion of the pre-transition male baseline intact (Hilton & Lundberg, 2021; Wiik et al., *JCEM*, 2020; Alvares et al., *BJSM*, 2022).
   * **Athletic performance metrics:** In a longitudinal cohort of physically active US Air Force personnel (Roberts et al., 2021), after 2 years of GAHT, trans women's push-up and sit-up performance converged toward female averages, but they remained roughly $12\%$ faster in the 1.5-mile run than cisgender females. Cross-sectional studies comparing trained/recreational trans women on long-term GAHT with cisgender women (e.g., Alvares et al., 2022; Hamilton et al., *BJSM*, 2024) similarly find that absolute $\text{VO}_2\text{ max}$, grip strength, and lower-body power remain higher than in cisgender female controls.

### 3. Addressing the Strongest Counterarguments and Evidentiary Limits
* **Relative vs. absolute normalization (mass and height scaling):**
  The strongest scientific counterargument is that because trans women retain a larger skeletal frame while losing hemoglobin and some muscle power, they must move a heavier frame with a female-level oxygen delivery system ("driving a larger car with a smaller engine"). In studies like Hamilton et al. (2024) and Alvares et al. (2022), when $\text{VO}_2\text{ max}$ or strength is normalized *per kilogram of fat-free mass* or *per unit of height*, differences between trans women and cisgender women shrink substantially or disappear.
  * *Why this does not eliminate the elite advantage in most sports:* Elite sport is rarely contested per unit of lean mass or height. In absolute-output events (sprinting, swimming, rowing, throwing, jumping, cycling on flat terrain), absolute watts, absolute stride/stroke length, and absolute force determine the winner. However, in gravity-limited endurance events (marathon running, steep uphill cycling) or weight-class sports where an athlete must cut to a fixed total body mass, carrying extra skeletal mass can partially offset or complicate the structural advantage.
* **Lack of elite-specific longitudinal data:**
  Virtually all controlled physiological studies examine non-elite, recreational, or military cohorts rather than Olympic-caliber trans athletes, and they rarely control rigorously for multi-year elite training histories. Because no live external database lookup was used in this response, studies published after early 2025 are not reflected here.
  * *Why the conclusion still holds at high confidence:* The biomechanical mechanisms (bone lever length, myonuclei retention, baseline muscle cross-sectional area) are physiological properties of male puberty itself, not artifacts of being untrained. In elite sport, where margins are razor-thin, retaining even a fraction of the $10\%\text{–}50\%$ male-puberty gap is larger than the gap between first and eighth place.
* **Observational tournament outcomes:**
  Proponents of full inclusion note that trans women have not swept elite female podiums during periods when testosterone-threshold rules were in place. However, population-level podium counts are confounded by extreme sample-size differences (trans women who train for elite sport represent a tiny fraction of the population) and selection effects; absence of widespread dominance does not refute a retained physiological advantage per individual.

### 4. Calibration and Conditions for Revision
* **Near-certain:** Male puberty confers permanent skeletal and structural advantages and baseline muscular advantages that are only partially reduced after 1–3+ years of testosterone suppression.
* **Probable:** These retained physiological differences translate into a meaningful competitive advantage at the elite level in strength, power, speed, and absolute-aerobic sports.
* **Unresolved / Sport-specific:** Whether a meaningful net advantage persists in ultra-endurance, weight-restricted, or purely skill/dexterity sports (e.g., shooting, equestrian, certain gymnastics disciplines).
* **What would revise this conclusion:** Controlled longitudinal or matched-cohort data on elite-trained trans women after multi-year GAHT demonstrating that actual competition outputs (or sport-specific power/velocity metrics) converge with equally trained cisgender female elites within typical elite podium margins ($\lesssim 1\%$). Conversely, this empirical conclusion does not by itself settle the normative policy question of how sports federations should balance fairness, safety, and inclusion across different tiers of competition.

Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No**—if "good evidence" means moderate-to-high-certainty clinical evidence capable of establishing a causal, long-term improvement in mental health outcomes.

Low-certainty evidence does not prove that these interventions are ineffective or that no individuals benefit; rather, it means existing studies are methodologically unable to isolate the long-term causal effects of medical intervention from confounding factors.

### 1. What systematic reviews show
When evaluated under standard evidence-based medicine frameworks (such as GRADE), the evidence linking puberty blockers (GnRH analogues) or cross-sex hormones in minors to long-term mental health improvements is consistently rated as **low to very low certainty**. 

Independent systematic reviews commissioned by public health authorities—including the UK’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE, 2020) and the University of York reviews for the Cass Review (2024), the Swedish Agency for Health Technology Assessment (SBU, 2022), and the Finnish health authority COHERE (2020)—all reached the same core methodological conclusion: the existing literature cannot reliably establish whether puberty blockers or hormones cause lasting improvements in depression, anxiety, suicidality, or overall psychological functioning.

### 2. Why the evidence is weak
Several recurring study limitations prevent firm causal conclusions:
* **Lack of adequate control groups:** Most studies use single-arm pre–post designs or observational cohorts without well-matched comparison groups of youths with gender dysphoria who did not receive medical intervention. Without a comparator, improvements cannot be separated from natural maturation or regression to the mean.
* **Confounding by concurrent treatments:** Minors receiving puberty blockers or hormones frequently receive concurrent psychotherapy, psychiatric medications (such as antidepressants), and family or school support. Most studies do not separate the effects of endocrine treatment from these co-interventions.
* **Short follow-up and high attrition:** Most published cohorts follow patients for 6 months to 2 years rather than into mature adulthood. In longer cohorts, substantial loss to follow-up introduces attrition bias, as patients with worse outcomes or who discontinue treatment may be disproportionately missing from final analyses.
* **Mixed results between interventions:** Studies of puberty blockers alone show inconsistent psychological effects—for instance, the original Dutch cohort (de Vries et al., 2011) found improvements in some behavioral scores but no significant change in gender dysphoria or body image during the blocker phase, while the UK GIDS replication (Carmichael et al., 2021) found no statistically significant psychological benefit over time on blockers. Studies of cross-sex hormones (e.g., de Vries et al., 2014; Tordoff et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023) more frequently report improvements in depression, anxiety, or body satisfaction over 1 to 2 years, but remain limited by short follow-up, unmeasured confounding, or missing data.

### 3. The strongest case for "Yes" and its limits
Medical organizations that recommend gender-affirming care (such as the Endocrine Society, WPATH, and the American Academy of Pediatrics) argue that:
1. Observational studies and clinical registries predominantly show stable or improved mental health and body congruence over 1–2 years.
2. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are practically and ethically unfeasible because blinding is impossible once secondary sex characteristics develop, and youths assigned to a non-treatment arm would likely drop out or seek care elsewhere.

This argument explains *why* high-quality trials are absent and why clinicians must make decisions under uncertainty. However, the ethical or practical difficulty of running rigorous trials does not upgrade the methodological quality of confounded or short-term observational studies. Furthermore, even without RCTs, observational designs can be strengthened through comprehensive registries, high retention, and matched comparators—standards that most existing studies have not met.

### 4. What would change this conclusion
The assessment would shift to **Yes** if prospective longitudinal studies with multi-year follow-up into adulthood demonstrated sustained reductions in depression, anxiety, or suicidality while maintaining high retention rates, pre-registered outcomes, and rigorous controls for baseline mental health, psychotherapy, and psychiatric medication use.
