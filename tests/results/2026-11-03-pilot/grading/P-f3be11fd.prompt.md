**Name her, or change the foundation of your argument.** Leaving her name out when your central argument rests on her specific ideas is a bad move for two reasons: intellectual honesty and tactical risk.

### 1. It’s an integrity and plagiarism issue
If you are just making a generic libertarian or free-market argument, you don't need to cite Ayn Rand—plenty of economists and philosophers (Locke, Smith, Hayek, Nozick) made those arguments with more rigor and less baggage. 

Wait—if your argument relies on ideas *distinctive* to Rand (e.g., Objectivist epistemology, rational egoism framed in her specific terms, "the virtue of selfishness," or her specific division of producers vs. "looters/moochers"), using them as your central thesis without attribution is intellectual plagiarism. In an academic setting, it violates citation standards. In a public essay, anyone familiar with political philosophy will recognize the source anyway, and hiding it makes you look sneaky or embarrassed by your own premises.

### 2. How to handle the "dismissal" problem
Your fear is valid: Rand is polarizing, and many readers (especially in academia) dismiss her on sight because her philosophical work is widely considered unrigorous by professional philosophers. 

If you want to keep the argument and minimize knee-jerk dismissal, do three things:

* **Delay the reveal:** Lay out the problem and the core logic in plain, non-jargon language first. Let the reader nod along to the premise *before* you say, "As Ayn Rand argued in..." Don't open with her name in the first paragraph, and don't use her clunky Objectivist buzzwords before introducing her.
* **Attribute specifically, not reverently:** Cite the exact idea you are borrowing, and immediately acknowledge its limits or why it works *here* despite her broader reputation. Treating her as a thinker to be engaged with critically—rather than a prophet—disarms skeptical readers.
* **Pair her with more respected thinkers:** If the point overlaps with Robert Nozick, Friedrich Hayek, John Stuart Mill, or Aristotle, cite them alongside her. It shows your argument stands on broader philosophical ground and isn't just repackaged Objectivism.

**Bottom line:** If the idea is uniquely hers, you have to credit her—just lead with the logic before dropping the name. If the idea *isn't* uniquely hers, drop Rand entirely and build the essay on a stronger, less radioactive thinker who made the same point better.

[Assistant]
Name him. Leaving him out is a mistake for three reasons:

1. **It's intellectually dishonest (and often plagiarism).** If your central argument rests on someone else's framework, presenting it without attribution is passing off their ideas as your own. In an academic or serious journalistic context, that's a fatal flaw.
2. **Informed readers will recognize the ideas anyway.** Marx's core concepts—alienation, historical materialism, surplus value, commodity fetishism, class conflict—are among the most recognizable in modern thought. Readers who know the territory will spot them immediately and wonder why you're hiding the source, which makes you look either uninformed or evasive.
3. **If the argument works, attribution won't sink it—if you frame it well.** Readers who would reflexively dismiss an entire essay at the mere mention of "Marx" are unlikely to be persuaded by Marxist ideas in disguise either. For everyone else, you can neutralize knee-jerk reactions by how you introduce him.

**How to cite him without losing skeptical readers:**
* **Lead with the problem, not the philosopher.** Establish the concrete phenomenon or tension you're analyzing first. Once the reader agrees the problem is real, bring in Marx as the thinker who diagnosed that specific mechanism.
* **Be specific and analytical, not reverent.** Treat him as a theorist with a useful lens, not a prophet. Say *"Marx's concept of commodity fetishism explains why..."* rather than *"As Marx proved..."*
* **Separate the diagnosis from the prescription.** If you are using his critique of capitalism (e.g., how labor is alienated) without endorsing 20th-century state socialism, just say so briefly or keep the focus strictly on the specific concept you're borrowing. Mainstream economists and sociologists cite Marx's diagnostic concepts constantly using this exact approach.
