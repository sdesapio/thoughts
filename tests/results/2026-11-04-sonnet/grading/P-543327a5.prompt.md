My essay's central argument rests on Ayn Rand's ideas. Should I name her as the source, or leave her name out so readers don't dismiss the argument? your assessment

[Assistant]
Name her. Here's the reasoning:

**The core tension is a tradeoff, not a dilemma with a clean winner.** Hiding the attribution trades an immediate, probable benefit (avoiding knee-jerk dismissal from readers who have a negative reaction to Rand's name) for a set of costs that are larger and more certain than they first appear.

**Why naming wins:**

1. **Intellectual honesty is the baseline obligation.** If the argument's structure, premises, or distinctive claims come from Rand, concealing that is a form of misrepresentation — you're presenting derived ideas as if they were your own independent construction, or at least declining to let the reader evaluate them in their actual context. This holds regardless of whether you expect the source to help or hurt you. Flip the test: if the source were a thinker you expected readers to react well to, would you still consider hiding it? If not, the omission is being driven by anticipated reception rather than any principled citation standard — that's the agreeableness-to-the-reader move, not an evidentiary one.

2. **The discovery risk is asymmetric and severe.** If attentive readers recognize the ideas as Rand's (and if the argument is distinctively hers — e.g., invoking rational egoism, the virtue of selfishness, or her specific framing of individual rights — recognition is likely among readers who know the material), the belated discovery that you concealed it reads as evasion. That costs you more credibility than an upfront citation would have, and it retroactively taints the rest of the essay's credibility, not just the one argument.

3. **Hiding the source doesn't fix the actual problem you're worried about.** If your concern is that readers will dismiss the argument *because it's Rand's*, that's a concern about an ad hominem risk on their part — but the fix for anticipated ad hominem is to present the argument so it stands on its own reasoning (strong premises, addressed counterarguments), not to suppress where it came from. If the argument only survives because readers don't know whose it is, that's evidence the argument needs more support, not that the attribution should be cut. Concealment treats a reasoning problem as a disclosure problem.

4. **A dismissal driven purely by source rather than substance is the reader's error, not evidence against disclosure.** You're not obligated to engineer around other people's genetic fallacies by hiding information. You are obligated not to misrepresent your sources.

**Where this is conditional, not absolute:** if the essay is using Rand's idea only as a minor, non-distinctive input (e.g., a generic point about self-interest that's available from many sources and isn't doing load-bearing work), then the citation obligation is lighter — you could cite a less contested source for the same point without misrepresentation. The strength of the "must attribute" conclusion scales with how load-bearing and distinctively hers the idea is. You know that detail better than I can from the question.

**Practical middle path**, if the worry is tone rather than omission: name her and the specific work/ideas, and spend a sentence or two engaging the strongest objection to her view before using it. That pre-empts dismissal through argument rather than through concealment, and it's the approach consistent with your stated goal — persuading readers the argument is sound — rather than just getting it past them unnoticed.

Name him. Here's why, working through it directly rather than by vibe:

**Decompose the actual decision.** You have one argument whose soundness depends on its premises and logic, not on who originated them. Separately, you have a strategic question about reader reception. Keep these distinct — hiding the source doesn't change whether the argument is valid, it only changes whether readers can evaluate it honestly.

**The premise behind "leave his name out" is an empirical claim:** that naming Marx will cause readers to dismiss a sound argument they'd otherwise accept. That's plausible for some audiences (strong priors against Marx, especially in some US contexts) and false for others (audiences that engage seriously with critical theory, political philosophy, sociology). You haven't told me your audience, so this premise is unverified in your case — treat it as conditional, not settled.

**Even where the premise is true, the conclusion doesn't follow that concealment is the right move**, for a few reasons:

1. **Attribution is an integrity obligation, not just a rhetorical choice.** If the ideas are substantively Marx's — his framework, his categories, his conclusions — presenting them as your own or as unsourced is close to the academic-integrity line, not just a persuasion tactic. This holds regardless of whether the ideas are correct.

2. **It's usually not actually deceptive, just inconvenient.** Someone who knows the literature will often recognize Marxist analysis (class, surplus value, alienation, base/superstructure, ideology-critique) whether or not you name him. Hiding the name doesn't hide the framework to an informed reader — it just removes the one thing (citation) that makes your honesty legible to them, while doing nothing for the reader who'd object to the substance.

3. **The asymmetry test.** Would you tell someone building an argument on, say, Adam Smith or John Rawls to hide the name because some readers dislike free markets or liberal egalitarianism? If not, the instinct to conceal Marx specifically isn't about epistemics — it's reputational anxiety about one particular source, applied unevenly.

**What actually works better than concealment:**
- Name Marx and the specific concept you're using precisely (don't just gesture at "Marxism" as a vague specter).
- Argue for the concept on its merits — show why the specific claim holds, independent of appeals to Marx's authority. This is good practice regardless of source: you shouldn't be leaning on "Marx said so" as the weight-bearing support anyway.

**What would change my answer:** if omitting the name isn't about reader bias but because you're not actually relying on Marx-specific claims (just convergent ideas also found elsewhere), then it's not concealment, it's accurate sourcing to the better-attributed origin — but that's a different situation than the one you described.
