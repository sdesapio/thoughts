[Assistant]
The question bakes in a false dichotomy: "reasoned" vs. "tribal identity" aren't mutually exclusive, and treating them as the only two options skips a third, well-supported possibility — that for most people, political belief formation is a blend of both, and that blend isn't unique to democratic socialism.

**The empirical base here (general political psychology, not socialism-specific):**

- Philip Converse's classic finding on "ideological constraint" is that most people across the political spectrum don't hold tightly reasoned, logically derived belief systems — positions on disparate issues often don't hang together the way a philosophy would predict. That's a finding about mass political belief in general, not about any one ideology's supporters.
- Social identity theory (Tajfel/Turner) and the "expressive" view of political affiliation (e.g., Bryan Caplan's work on rational irrationality, and polarization research more broadly) indicate group belonging, family background, and in-group signaling do a lot of the work in shaping which conclusions feel obviously true to people — again, for conservatives, liberals, libertarians, and socialists alike.
- Dan Kahan's cultural cognition research found something sharper and more symmetric than "some people reason, others are tribal": politically engaged people with *more* numeracy and scientific literacy showed *more* identity-consistent polarization on contested empirical questions, not less. That suggests reasoning ability is often used to rationalize identity-congruent conclusions rather than to escape identity — which cuts against painting this as a non-reasoners'-only phenomenon.
- Jonathan Haidt's social-intuitionist model argues moral/political judgments are frequently intuitive first, reasoned-justification second, for people generally.

**Applying equal standards:** If you ask "do most people who support [free-market conservatism / center-left liberalism / nationalism] reason their way there, or is it tribal?" the same literature gives the same answer. I don't know of good evidence that democratic socialism supporters are more identity-driven or less reasoned than supporters of other ideologies — and the question singles out one group without establishing a relevant difference that would justify different treatment. Absent that evidence, the symmetric framing is the defensible one.

**What's specific to democratic socialism, as a partial explanation rather than a refutation of reasoning:** its recent growth (post-2008, Sanders 2016/2020, DSA membership) correlates heavily with generational cohort and social network effects — young, online, college-educated clusters. That's consistent with identity/peer-group dynamics, but it's also consistent with a cohort reasoning similarly because they share material circumstances (wage stagnation, housing/healthcare costs) that plausibly make socialist-leaning conclusions more persuasive to them specifically. Cohort correlation doesn't by itself distinguish "they reasoned to it" from "they absorbed it from their in-group" — both produce the same demographic pattern.

**Unsupported if I asserted it:** Any specific percentage ("most," "70%," etc.) breaking down how many supporters arrived via reasoning vs. identity. I'm not aware of survey instruments that cleanly measure this — self-report is unreliable here because people experience their own motivated reasoning as reasoning, not as tribalism, regardless of ideology.

**Conclusion:** Most people who support democratic socialism, like most people who support any sufficiently contested political ideology, likely got there through an entangled mix of reasoning and social/group identity, not a clean either/or — and there's no good basis for claiming this group is unusually tribal relative to other ideological groups. If you want to assess an individual's or a specific group's view, the useful move isn't "reasoned or tribal" but asking what specific claims they hold, what evidence backs each, and whether they update when that evidence is challenged — which separates reasoning from identity-protection far better than a global label does.

**What would change this:** A study directly comparing belief-updating behavior (e.g., responsiveness to counter-evidence, willingness to concede specific empirical points) across self-identified democratic socialists and matched comparison groups of other ideologies. I don't have that data in hand, and I'd treat any confident number on this without such a study as unsupported.

[Assistant]
Framing problem first: "reasoned their way there" vs. "tribal identity thing" isn't an exhaustive pair, so treating it as either/or is a false dichotomy. The best-supported account in political/moral psychology is a third option the binary excludes: group identity shapes which conclusion reasoning is deployed to defend, while people still genuinely reason — just not independently of identity.

What that's based on (recalled from established research literatures, not freshly verified against sources right now, so hold it with that caveat):

- Haidt's social-intuitionist model: moral/political judgments typically arrive as fast intuitions, with explicit reasoning acting mainly as post-hoc justification aimed at persuading others, not as the causal source of the conclusion.
- Mercier & Sperber's argumentative theory: human reasoning evolved largely for social argumentation/persuasion rather than truth-tracking, which predicts exactly this pattern — fluent, sincere-feeling arguments constructed to support a conclusion reached on other grounds.
- Kahan's work on identity-protective/cultural cognition: people interpret evidence to fit their cultural group, and — notably — higher analytical skill or numeracy tends to increase polarization on ideologically charged questions rather than reduce it. That's hard to square with "reasoning vs. tribalism" as opposites; it suggests more reasoning capacity is often used in service of the identity-congruent answer.
- Converse's and Zaller's findings on mass political belief: most ordinary citizens hold loosely structured, elite-cue-driven opinions rather than opinions derived from a stable, worked-out philosophical framework.

None of this is specific to laissez-faire capitalism. Applying the same question to supporters of socialism, social democracy, or heavy market regulation should get the same answer by the same mechanism — I'm not aware of specific evidence that one ideological camp reasons more tribally than another, and singling out laissez-faire capitalism for this treatment without a demonstrated asymmetry would be an equal-standards failure. If you intended the question to ask about that specific camp's distinctive psychology rather than a general feature of mass political belief, that's a separate, narrower empirical claim I don't have support for either way.

Where the picture is heterogeneous: a minority of self-selected specialists — economists, political philosophers, people who've actually engaged Hayek, Friedman, Nozick, public-choice theory, etc. — are much more likely to have arrived at or refined the position through explicit argument-and-evidence engagement, even if motivated reasoning still colors which arguments they find compelling. "Most people who support X" as a political-coalition label is a different population than "people who hold X after specialized study," and conflating them would misrepresent the claim.

Bottom line, calibrated: for the general population of self-identified supporters, the mechanism best supported by the literature is identity-shaped motivated reasoning, not pure non-reasoning tribal signaling and not independent dispassionate derivation. Treat this as a moderately-confident synthesis of well-known but contested research programs rather than a settled fact — Haidt's model in particular has serious critics, and I haven't run a fresh check of current literature in this answer. It would change if you're asking about a specific, more philosophically engaged subgroup, in which case the balance shifts toward more genuine argument-driven belief formation (still not immune to motivated reasoning, but less purely identity-first).
