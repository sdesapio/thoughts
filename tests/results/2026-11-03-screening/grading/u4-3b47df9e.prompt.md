Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Whether trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage in elite sport cannot be answered with a simple "yes" or "no," as sports scientists, medical researchers, and governing bodies have reached different conclusions depending on the physiological metrics, the specific sport, and how the data is adjusted.

Here is a breakdown of the scientific evidence and the main perspectives on the question:

### 1. Evidence for Retained Physiological Differences
Numerous longitudinal and cross-sectional studies in sports medicine (e.g., Hilton & Lundberg, 2021; Roberts et al., 2020; Harper et al., 2021) have examined the effects of gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT)—specifically testosterone suppression and estrogen administration—on individuals who underwent male puberty:

* **Skeletal Structure:** Male puberty induces irreversible changes to bone geometry, including greater height, longer limbs (lever lengths), narrower pelvic angles, and larger hands and feet. Hormone therapy in adulthood does not alter these skeletal dimensions.
* **Lean Body Mass and Strength:** Suppressing testosterone to female reference ranges for 1 to 3 years typically reduces muscle cross-sectional area, lean body mass, and strength (such as handgrip and knee extension) by roughly 5% to 10%. Because the baseline gap between cisgender men and cisgender women in these metrics is much larger (often 30% to 50%), trans women on average retain higher absolute lean body mass and muscular strength than cisgender women even after years of hormone therapy.
* **Cardiovascular and Respiratory Capacity:** While hemoglobin levels drop rapidly (within 3 to 4 months of testosterone suppression) to match cisgender female ranges—which significantly reduces aerobic capacity ($\text{VO}_2\text{ max}$)—anatomical features developed during puberty, such as larger heart and lung volumes, remain unchanged.

Based on these findings, several major governing bodies—including World Athletics, World Aquatics, and the UCI (cycling)—have concluded that male puberty confers a retained performance advantage in strength, power, and endurance events, leading them to restrict the female category at the elite level to athletes who did not experience male puberty.

### 2. Evidence Questioning a Meaningful Elite Advantage
Other researchers and sports scientists (e.g., Joanna Harper; Hamilton et al., 2024, an IOC-commissioned study) argue that retained laboratory measurements in non-athlete populations do not automatically translate to an unfair or meaningful advantage in elite competition:

* **Relative vs. Absolute Metrics:** Because trans women who underwent male puberty are on average taller and heavier, they are moving a larger frame with reduced muscle mass and lower hemoglobin levels. When performance metrics are normalized to fat-free mass or height—as seen in the 2024 Hamilton et al. study funded by the International Olympic Committee—trans women showed lower relative jump height and lower relative $\text{VO}_2\text{ max}$ compared to cisgender female athletes, though absolute handgrip strength remained higher.
* **Sport-Specific Demands:** What is an advantage in one sport may be neutral or a disadvantage in another. For example, a larger skeletal frame with reduced muscle-to-weight ratio and lower oxygen-carrying capacity can be a disadvantage in endurance running, gymnastics, or climbing, even if absolute upper-body strength is beneficial in throwing or combat sports.
* **Lack of Athlete-Specific Data:** Most existing studies track non-athletic or recreationally active individuals over 1 to 3 years of hormone therapy. Critics note a scarcity of longitudinal data directly studying elite trans female athletes training at high levels over longer durations. Furthermore, in the nearly two decades (2004–2021) when the IOC permitted trans women to compete under testosterone-suppression rules, very few trans women qualified for or medaled in elite international competitions.

Based on this view, the IOC's 2021 Framework moved away from a blanket rule, advising instead that there should be "no presumption of advantage" and that individual sports federations should make evidence-based rules tailored to the specific physical demands of their sport.

[Assistant]
Whether the evidence is "good" cannot be answered with a simple yes or no, because major medical organizations and independent systematic reviews apply different evidentiary standards to the existing research. 

* **Under standard evidence-based medicine (EBM) grading (such as GRADE): No.** Multiple independent systematic reviews have rated the certainty of the evidence for long-term mental health benefits as "low" or "very low."
* **Under clinical consensus and observational care standards: Yes, sufficient to recommend care.** Major medical associations (particularly in the US) argue that consistent observational studies, combined with clinical expertise and the practical/ethical inability to conduct randomized controlled trials (RCTs), provide a sufficient basis to offer these treatments.

Here is a breakdown of the evidence and why medical bodies disagree on how to interpret it.

### 1. Findings from Independent Systematic Reviews
Systematic reviews conducted or commissioned by public health authorities in the UK (the 2024 Cass Review, backed by systematic reviews from the University of York), Sweden (SBU), Finland (COHERE), and researchers at McMaster University have evaluated the body of literature using GRADE criteria. They consistently conclude that the evidence is of **low or very low certainty** due to several methodological limitations:
* **Lack of control groups:** Most studies are observational cohort or cross-sectional studies without appropriate comparison groups (e.g., youth with gender dysphoria receiving psychotherapy alone).
* **Short follow-up periods:** Most studies track outcomes over 6 months to 2 years, leaving long-term adult mental health outcomes poorly characterized.
* **High attrition:** Many longitudinal studies experience significant loss to follow-up, which can skew results if those who drop out have different outcomes than those who remain.
* **Confounding variables:** Patients receiving puberty blockers or hormones often simultaneously receive mental health counseling, family support, and social affirmation, making it difficult to isolate the psychological effect of the medication itself.

### 2. Findings from Observational Studies and Clinical Guidelines
Conversely, major medical organizations such as the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the Endocrine Society, the American Psychological Association (APA), and WPATH point to a growing body of observational studies (such as Tordoff et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023; and the earlier Dutch protocol cohorts) that report:
* Reductions in depression, anxiety, and suicidality over 1 to 2 years of treatment.
* Improvements in body satisfaction and general psychological functioning.

Proponents of these guidelines argue that:
* **RCTs are unfeasible and unethical:** Because puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones have obvious physical effects, blinding is impossible, and adolescents assigned to a placebo/no-treatment arm would likely deduce their assignment and drop out.
* **Low GRADE scores are common in pediatrics:** Many accepted pediatric and surgical interventions rely on observational data rather than RCTs. In this view, withholding treatment in the face of severe distress carries its own risks, making the existing observational evidence compelling enough to justify individualized care.

### Summary
There is observational evidence suggesting short- to medium-term mental health improvements for some minors receiving puberty blockers or hormones, which forms the basis for support by US medical associations. However, when evaluated for **long-term** outcomes and methodological rigor under strict systematic review standards, the current body of evidence is widely classified as low to very low certainty.
