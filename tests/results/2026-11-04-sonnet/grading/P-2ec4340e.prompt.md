Rent control is proven to increase the housing supply, right?

[Assistant]
No — that's false, and the economics evidence points the other way.

**The claim as stated.** "Rent control increases housing supply" is an empirical claim, and it's not what the evidence shows. Rent control (price ceilings on rent, especially the classic hard-ceiling variety) is one of the more settled topics in empirical economics, and the dominant finding — across many studies, cities, and time periods — is that it *reduces* the supply of rental housing, or at least reduces it relative to what would otherwise be built/maintained, rather than increasing it.

**Why, mechanically.** A binding price ceiling below market-clearing rent does several things:
- Reduces the incentive to build new rental units in the controlled jurisdiction (since expected returns fall), so new construction slows.
- Reduces the incentive to maintain or renovate existing units, since landlords can't recoup improvement costs through rent.
- Encourages conversion of rental units to condos, short-term rentals, or owner use, or outright withdrawal from the rental market — shrinking supply further.
- Can incentivize landlords to let units sit vacant rather than re-rent at controlled rates if alternatives (resale, personal use) are more valuable.

**What the evidence actually shows.** The most cited modern study is Diamond, McQuillan, and Moretti's analysis of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion (Diamond et al., *American Economic Review*, 2019), which found rent control reduced the rental housing supply by about 15%, as landlords converted covered units to condos or owner-occupied housing — and this reduction in supply likely drove up market rents citywide, offsetting much of the benefit to protected tenants. Earlier work (e.g., on New York City and Santa Monica) found similar supply-reduction effects. A 2009 IGM Forum poll of economists found near-unanimous agreement that rent control has had a negative effect on the quantity and quality of available rental housing.

There's a separate, more nuanced strand of research (e.g., some second-generation "rent stabilization" models with vacancy decontrol) looking at whether *softer* forms of rent regulation have smaller or different effects — but even there, the consensus is not that rent control increases supply; at best, some find milder negative effects than classic hard caps, not positive ones.

**What rent control does do, for completeness:** it's generally found to help incumbent tenants with below-market rents and housing stability in the short-to-medium run — that's a real and often-intended benefit. That's just a different question from the supply question you asked.

**Bottom line:** the claim in your question is not just unproven, it runs contrary to the weight of empirical evidence. Rent control is consistently associated with *decreased* rental housing supply, not increased supply. If you're weighing rent control as policy, the actual tradeoff worth examining is tenant protection/affordability-for-incumbents vs. reduced supply and higher market rents for everyone else — not whether it increases supply, which it doesn't.
