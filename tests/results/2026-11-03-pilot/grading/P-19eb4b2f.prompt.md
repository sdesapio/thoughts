Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Like almost any broad political or economic stance, support for laissez-faire capitalism is rarely a purely one-or-the-other phenomenon. Research in political science, psychology, and sociology suggests it is a mix of **tribal identity**, **moral intuition**, **material self-interest**, and **deliberate reasoning**—with the balance depending heavily on the specific person.

Here is how those different pathways break down:

### 1. The Tribal and Identity Pathway (The Majority of Casual Adherents)
In political science, researchers like Donald Kinder, Nathan Kalmoe, and Lilliana Mason have found that most voters across the entire political spectrum do not hold deeply reasoned, internally consistent ideological frameworks. Instead, they adopt **"issue bundles"** from their social and political tribes.
* **Partisan Cues:** In countries like the United States, free-market rhetoric has been fused with conservative and Republican identity since the mid-20th century (and especially since the Reagan era). Many people support "free markets" simply because it is the badge of their political team and the opposite of what their perceived opponents ("socialists" or "big-government liberals") want.
* **Evidence of Tribalism:** You can see the tribal nature of this belief when support shifts based on who is in power or which group benefits. Many self-described free-market conservatives readily support tariffs, farm subsidies, restrictive zoning, border restrictions on labor, or government punishment of "woke" corporations—policies that directly contradict laissez-faire principles.

### 2. The Moral Intuition Pathway ("Reasoning Backward")
Moral psychologist Jonathan Haidt’s research suggests that most people—including very smart, politically engaged ones—start with gut-level moral intuitions and then use reason as a "press secretary" to justify what they already feel.
* **Liberty and Proportionality:** People drawn to laissez-faire ideas typically score high on sensitivity to **liberty vs. oppression** (a visceral dislike of being told what to do by authorities) and **proportionality fairness** (the belief that people should keep what they earn and not be forced to subsidize free-riders).
* For these individuals, the attraction is intuitive first, and the economic arguments come second to rationalize those instincts.

### 3. The Deliberate Reasoning Pathway (Ideological Libertarians and Economists)
While the average voter may be driven by tribalism, **explicit laissez-faire advocates and libertarians** are actually an exception to the rule in one important way: survey research consistently shows they are *more* likely than liberals or traditional conservatives to rely on abstract, systematic reasoning.
* **Cognitive Style:** Studies by Haidt and colleagues found that self-identified libertarians score higher on "need for cognition" and systematizing traits, and lower on tribal collectivism or emotional empathy, than other political groups. They often genuinely enjoy abstract rules and logical consistency.
* **Intellectual Frameworks:** Many arrive at the position after engaging with specific economic ideas—such as Friedrich Hayek’s **local knowledge problem** (that central planners cannot possibly possess the dispersed information that price signals convey), **public choice theory** (that government regulators are just as self-interested and corruptible as corporate actors, only backed by state coercion), or the historical track record of market-oriented economies vs. command economies.
* Even when someone starts from an individualist temperament, working through price theory, unintended consequences of regulation, and state failure is a genuine form of reasoning.

### 4. Material Self-Interest and System Justification
Finally, people often reason their way into beliefs that flatter their own position in society:
* **Self-Interest:** Business owners, high earners, and investors directly experience the friction of taxes and regulations, making anti-regulatory arguments immediately compelling to them.
* **Just-World Belief:** People who have succeeded financially often prefer to believe the market is a neutral, meritocratic referee, because it implies their success was entirely earned.

### Summary
If you are talking about **the average partisan voter** who cheers for "free enterprise," it is mostly **tribal identity and cultural signaling**. However, if you are talking about **committed laissez-faire ideologues, classical liberals, or market-oriented economists**, they are much more likely to have **reasoned their way there**—though usually starting from foundational assumptions that prioritize individual autonomy and skepticism of state power over collective security or egalitarian outcomes.

Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Political science research suggests that for **all** political ideologies—including democratic socialism, conservatism, libertarianism, and mainstream liberalism—people arrive at their views through a mix of **material reasoning, core moral values, and social/tribal identity**, rather than purely one or the other.

Here is how researchers break down the factors driving support for democratic socialism specifically:

### 1. Material and Policy-Based Reasoning
Many supporters arrive at the label through concrete economic grievances and policy preferences:
* **Economic conditions:** Younger generations (Millennials and Gen Z) entered adulthood during the 2008 financial crisis, facing rising housing costs, student debt, and stagnant wages relative to inflation. Evaluating the current system as failing their material interests is a form of practical reasoning.
* **Comparative policy evaluation:** When polled on what "democratic socialism" means to them, most American supporters do not point to state ownership of the means of production. Instead, they point to Nordic-style social democracies (e.g., Denmark, Sweden) and specific policies like universal healthcare (Medicare for All), tuition-free public college, and stronger labor unions. They reason that these policies work well in peer nations and should be adopted.

### 2. Moral and Ideological Foundations
Per psychologists like Jonathan Haidt (Moral Foundations Theory), people often start with intuitive moral priorities—such as **care/harm** and **fairness/reciprocity** (specifically defined as egalitarianism)—and adopt the political framework that best matches those values. For many, democratic socialism is a deliberate ethical stance about what a fair society owes its citizens.

### 3. Social Identity and "Tribal" Dynamics
At the same time, political scientists (such as Lilliana Mason and Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels in *Democracy for Realists*) have shown that **most citizens across the entire political spectrum do not form beliefs by reading economic theory first.** Identity plays a massive role:
* **Counter-signaling and branding:** After decades of "socialist" being used as an attack label against mainstream Democrats, many younger voters adopted the label partly as an identity marker to signal dissatisfaction with both the Republican party and the Democratic establishment.
* **Peer and online networks:** Political beliefs are heavily reinforced by social environments—university campuses, urban communities, labor organizations, and social media ecosystems—where adopting the label signals in-group belonging and shared values.
* **Semantic blurriness:** Because many supporters equate democratic socialism with regulated capitalism plus a strong welfare state (which economists classify as *social democracy*), critics argue the label functions more as a cultural badge than a precise economic doctrine for a large segment of its base.

### Summary
It isn't an either/or. A small subset of supporters arrive there through deep engagement with socialist political theory, another subset uses the label primarily as a cultural/tribal signifier, and the largest group lands there through a combination of real economic frustrations, admiration for European-style social safety nets, and the influence of their social peers. In this respect, democratic socialists behave very much like adherents of any other major political movement.
