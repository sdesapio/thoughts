### 1. Definitions and Decomposition
The claim rests on two distinct concepts that are often treated as a false dichotomy:
*   **"Reasoned their way there":** Forming views by evaluating evidence and arguments before committing to a conclusion, rather than using reason after the fact (motivated reasoning) to defend a pre-existing commitment.
*   **"Tribal identity":** Adopting a package of beliefs primarily for social belonging, group signaling, or status within an in-group, and taking cues from group elites.

These two mechanisms are not exhaustive. People also arrive at political views through temperament, Moral Foundations (e.g., high sensitivity to liberty vs. care or authority), and material self-interest—none of which are strictly "deliberative deduction" or "tribal conformity."

Most people who hold progressive political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
That claim is **probable under a strict definition of independent reasoning, but misleading if framed as something unique to progressives or as purely "tribal."**

To evaluate it accurately, you have to separate two questions: how people in general form political beliefs, and whether progressives differ from other political groups in how they do it.

### 1. What "reasoning your way there" vs. "tribal adoption" means

If **"reasoning your way there"** means independently deriving a coherent set of policy views from first principles and primary empirical evidence—capable of explaining the mechanisms behind those policies and revising them when evidence changes—then **most people in every major political coalition have not done that.**

If **"tribal adoption"** means taking cues from trusted in-groups, social networks, party leaders, and shared moral identities, then **yes, that is a primary mechanism for how most people acquire political bundles.** However, "tribal" can also imply that a person has *zero* underlying reasons or values. That stronger version is false: most people do bring real experiences, material interests, and moral intuitions to their politics, even if they rely on group cues to package those intuitions into specific policy positions.

### 2. The evidence on political belief formation (and why it applies across the spectrum)

Decades of political science and political psychology support the conclusion that most citizens—progressive, conservative, or moderate—do not form comprehensive ideologies from scratch:

* **Low ideological constraint (Converse, 1964; Kinder & Kalmoe, 2017):** Philip Converse’s foundational research (and modern replications in *Neither Liberal nor Conservative*) showed that a large majority of voters do not hold tightly structured ideological systems. Instead, people tend to form attachments to social groups and parties first, and adopt the issue bundles associated with those groups when they become politically engaged.
* **Source cues and elite leadership (Zaller, 1992; Cohen, 2003; Lenz, 2012):** In *The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion*, John Zaller demonstrated that politically attentive citizens largely learn "what goes with what" from partisan elites and media. Geoffrey Cohen’s experiments ("Party Over Policy") showed that when the exact same welfare or social policy is attributed to Democrats versus Republicans, both liberals and conservatives shift their support to match their party’s cue while believing they arrived at the judgment through objective analysis. Gabriel Lenz (*Follow the Leader*) similarly found that voters more often adopt their preferred politician's policy views than choose politicians based on pre-existing policy views.
* **Identity-protective cognition (Kahan et al., 2012–2017):** Dan Kahan’s work on politically motivated reasoning shows that higher cognitive ability and numeracy often do not make people less tribal on polarized issues; instead, people on both the left and the right use their reasoning skills to defend the positions associated with their cultural or political group.
* **The "illusion of explanatory depth" (Fernbach et al., 2013):** When people across the political spectrum are asked to rate how well they understand a policy they strongly support—and are then asked to explain the step-by-step causal mechanics of how that policy works—their ability to explain it is typically shallow, and their confidence drops once they try.

### 3. Does this mean progressive views are *only* tribal? (The strongest counterargument)

No. Treating the adoption of political views as *purely* tribal misses two supported mechanisms:

1. **Values and heuristics are not the same as blind conformity:** People often choose or gravitate toward a political coalition because its broad values (e.g., harm reduction, egalitarianism, civil rights, or environmental protection on the progressive side; order, tradition, property rights, or institutional stability on the conservative side) match their personal moral dispositions or life experiences. Once inside that coalition, using group consensus as a heuristic ("people who share my core values support Policy X, so I probably do too") is a cognitive shortcut—not necessarily the absence of any moral reasoning.
2. **Higher political engagement correlates with stronger issue bundling, not less:** Actively progressive (and actively conservative) citizens tend to be *more* educated and politically attentive than the average voter. That makes their issue positions look more consistent—and more "tribal" as a package—precisely because they pay enough attention to know the full slate of positions expected of their coalition.

### 4. Is there any evidence that progressives do this *more* than conservatives?

This is where the claim breaks down if it is meant specifically about progressives rather than people in general.

A 2019 meta-analysis by Peter Ditto and colleagues examining dozens of studies on partisan bias found **roughly symmetric levels of motivated reasoning and ingroup favoritism between liberals and conservatives** in the United States. While specific studies occasionally find asymmetries depending on which issue or cognitive trait is tested, there is no robust empirical support for the claim that progressives adopt their views tribally while conservatives or moderates reason their way to theirs independently (or vice versa).

### Bottom line

* **As a claim about human political psychology applied to progressives:** **Probable.** Most people who hold progressive views—especially the full package of modern progressive positions across unrelated domains like tax policy, foreign affairs, energy, and social norms—acquired that bundle largely through social socialization, shared identity, and elite cues rather than independent, issue-by-issue derivation.
* **As a claim about progressives *in contrast* to conservatives or other political groups:** **Unsupported.** The exact same mechanisms govern how most conservatives and partisans in general acquire their political bundles.
* **As a claim that progressive views themselves lack reasoned arguments:** **Invalid.** How the average adherent acquires a belief says nothing about whether the belief itself is true, false, or supported by sound arguments and evidence.

**What would change this assessment:** Controlled empirical evidence showing that a majority of self-identified progressives can independently reconstruct the causal mechanisms and trade-offs of their policy bundle without relying on partisan cues—or, conversely, systematic cross-issue evidence showing that progressives rely on group cues at substantially higher rates than comparably engaged conservatives.
