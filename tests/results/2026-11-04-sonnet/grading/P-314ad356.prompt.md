[Assistant]
Short answer: for most people, political-economic positions generally — laissez-faire included — are driven more by group identity, inherited environment, and moral intuition than by an explicit chain of premises and evidence. But that's not a special fact about laissez-faire supporters; it's the default finding about mass political belief in general, and singling out one position as "tribal" while implying its rivals are "reasoned" isn't supported.

A few things worth separating out:

**The question as posed is a false binary.** "Reasoned" and "tribal/identity-driven" aren't mutually exclusive. A person can absorb a position from their social environment *and* later construct or encounter real arguments for it *and* hold it partly because it signals group membership. Most political beliefs are a mix, not a pure case of either.

**Who counts as "most people" matters.** There's a real difference between:
- The lay public who self-identify as pro-free-market — here the political-psychology literature (e.g., Converse's work on low ideological "constraint" in mass publics, Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists*, Haidt's work on intuition preceding reasoning, Mercier & Sperber's argumentative theory of reasoning, Kahan's cultural-cognition research) generally finds that policy positions track group identity and social belonging more than they track derivations from stated principles. People often reason *to* a conclusion they already hold for identity reasons, then reach for arguments after the fact.
- The theorists and engaged advocates (Austrian/Chicago-school economists, public-choice theorists, libertarian philosophers) — these people do have articulated, falsifiable premise-to-conclusion arguments (knowledge/information-aggregation arguments, property-rights theory, specific empirical claims about growth, poverty, or regulatory failure). Whether those arguments are *correct* is a separate question, but calling their position "tribal" rather than "reasoned" would be inaccurate.

**Equal-standards check, which is the important part here:** if you apply the same lens to supporters of the alternatives — regulated capitalism, social democracy, socialism — the same literature finds the same pattern: most lay supporters of *those* positions also arrived there mostly through identity, upbringing, and group affiliation rather than working through the relevant economic arguments and evidence. I'm not aware of good evidence that laissez-faire supporters are distinctively more identity-driven than supporters of competing systems. So a claim like "laissez-faire support is tribal" only holds up if you're willing to say the same thing about its rivals; applying it asymmetrically isn't justified by what's actually known about political cognition.

**One more distinction worth keeping straight:** even if a belief's *origin* is mostly tribal, that tells you nothing about whether the belief is *true*. Treating "this position is mostly identity-driven" as if it refutes the position is a genetic-fallacy move — it explains how people got there, not whether the conclusion is right.

**Calibration:** I don't have a rigorous quantified survey to cite that pins down a precise number for "most" specifically among laissez-faire supporters — that specific statistic would need verification I can't do right now. What's reasonably well-supported is the general pattern (identity/intuition precedes and shapes reasoning for most people on most contested political-economic questions) and the point that this pattern isn't unique to one side of this particular debate. What would change this assessment: a study specifically measuring argument-sophistication or reasoning-process among free-market supporters versus matched supporters of alternative systems, showing a real asymmetry rather than the expected mass-public baseline.
