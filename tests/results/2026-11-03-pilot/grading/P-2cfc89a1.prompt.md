[Assistant]
No. That claim is false across standard definitions of housing supply. Empirical evidence and economic theory show that rent control either reduces the controlled rental supply or leaves total housing construction roughly unchanged when new builds are exempted—it is not proven to increase the housing supply.

### 1. Definitions and Scope

To evaluate the claim accurately, "housing supply" and "rent control" must be separated into distinct categories:

* **Total housing stock / new construction:** The overall number of residential units built and maintained in a market.
* **Controlled rental supply:** The number of units available for rent under price caps.
* **First-generation rent control:** Hard price freezes or strict caps applied across rental housing.
* **Second-generation rent control (rent stabilization):** Moderate caps on annual rent increases, typically exempting newly constructed buildings (or exempting them for 15–30 years) and allowing resets when a tenant moves out (vacancy decontrol).

### 2. Evidence and Mechanisms

**Effect on existing rental supply (Near-certain decrease):**
When rent caps hold prices below market-clearing levels, property owners face reduced returns on rental operation relative to alternative uses. Empirical studies consistently find that landlords respond by converting rental units into owner-occupied condominiums, TICs (tenancies in common), or high-end redevelopments, or by moving into the units themselves.
* *San Francisco:* Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (2019, *American Economic Review*) exploited a 1994 ballot initiative that expanded rent control to small multifamily buildings built before 1980. While covered tenants benefited from lower displacement in the short run, landlords reduced the supply of rental housing in affected buildings by **15%** over the longer term by converting units to condos or redeveloping buildings so they were exempt.
* *Cambridge, Massachusetts:* Autor, Palmer, and Pathak (2014, *Journal of Political Economy*) studied the 1995 end of rent control and found that decontrol led to substantial housing investment, maintenance, and property value appreciation, reversing prior underinvestment in the controlled stock.

**Effect on new construction (Neutral to negative):**
* When rent control applies to new buildings, it lowers the expected return on development and directly reduces new housing construction.
* Most modern ("second-generation") rent stabilization laws exempt new construction specifically to avoid halting development. Studies of New Jersey cities with moderate rent stabilization and new-construction exemptions (e.g., Ambrosius et al., 2015; Gyourko & Linneman, 1989) generally find no statistically significant difference in new construction compared to neighboring cities without rent control.
* However, **not reducing** new construction (because new builds are exempt from the policy) is not the same as **increasing** housing supply. Even with exemptions, investors sometimes reduce development due to "regulatory risk"—the expectation that the exemption window will later be shortened or repealed (as occurred in jurisdictions like St. Paul, Minnesota in 2021, where a strict 3% cap without a new-construction exemption coincided with an immediate sharp drop in multifamily building permits before the law was later amended).

### 3. The Strongest Case for a Positive Supply Effect

The strongest theoretical and empirical argument that rent regulation could increase supply relies on **monopsony or oligopoly power** among landlords:
* **The mechanism:** In a perfectly competitive market, a price ceiling below equilibrium reduces quantity supplied. However, if a small number of landlords hold market power in a localized market and intentionally withhold units or restrict output to drive up rents, a carefully calibrated price ceiling (set between the competitive price and the monopoly price) could theoretically eliminate the incentive to withhold units and increase the quantity of rented units.
* **Why this does not establish the claim:**
  1. Most urban rental markets contain thousands of distinct property owners, making pure quantity-withholding monopoly behavior rare at the citywide level (though search frictions and localized concentration exist).
  2. Even in models where landlords have market power via search frictions, setting a price ceiling rarely increases physical construction; at best, it could slightly reduce strategic vacancies if the cap is set within a narrow band.
  3. No major empirical study of real-world rent control policies has demonstrated a net increase in a city's housing stock or rental supply attributable to rent control.

Sometimes proponents also conflate **tenant stability** (reduced displacement for incumbent tenants) or **stock preservation of low-rent units** (preventing a cheap unit from becoming an expensive unit) with an "increase in housing supply." Rent control does increase the probability that current tenants remain in their apartments (Diamond et al., 2019), and it preserves lower rents for covered units while they remain rentals—but that is a change in price and turnover for existing units, not an increase in the number of homes.

### 4. Conclusion and Conditions for Revision

* **Conclusion:** It is well-established that traditional and second-generation rent control reduce the supply of available rental units (primarily via conversion to owner-occupied housing and reduced mobility) and either reduce or have no positive effect on total new construction.
* **What would change this conclusion:** This assessment would change only if quasi-experimental empirical evidence (controlling for zoning changes, subsidies, and macroeconomic trends) showed a jurisdiction where binding rent caps caused a net increase in either total housing units built or total rental units made available, without relying on simultaneous upzoning or public construction subsidies as the actual cause.

Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. The claim that tax cuts *always* pay for themselves through economic growth is false, and even the narrower claim that broad tax cuts *typically* recoup their full revenue cost is contradicted by empirical evidence and standard macroeconomic analysis.

### 1. What "paying for themselves" requires

To evaluate the claim, separate two distinct effects:
1. **Economic growth effect:** Lower marginal tax rates can increase incentives to work, save, and invest (supply-side effects) and increase short-run aggregate demand.
2. **Revenue effect ("paying for itself"):** Total tax revenue equals the **tax rate $\times$ the tax base** ($R = \tau \cdot B$). For a cut in $\tau$ to leave revenue unchanged or higher, the percentage increase in the taxable base $B$ must be at least as large as the percentage reduction in the tax rate $\tau$.

For example, cutting an effective tax rate from $25\%$ to $20\%$ (a $20\%$ reduction in the rate) requires the taxable base to expand by **at least $25\%$** above its baseline trajectory just to break even on revenue. In a large economy where real GDP grows at roughly $2\%$ to $3\%$ per year, a permanent $25\%$ jump in the level of output from a single tax change is far outside observed responses.

### 2. The strongest version of the argument (The Laffer Curve)

The strongest theoretical and empirical case for self-financing tax cuts rests on the **Laffer curve**:
- At a $0\%$ tax rate, government collects zero revenue. At a $100\%$ tax rate on reported income or activity, legal taxable activity collapses toward zero. By continuity, there is some revenue-maximizing tax rate $\tau^*$.
- When an existing tax rate is **above $\tau^*$** (on the "prohibitive range" of the Laffer curve), cutting the rate expands real activity and reduces tax avoidance/evasion enough that total revenue rises.

This mechanism is real and can hold in specific, narrow cases:
- **Extreme marginal rates:** When top marginal income tax rates in the U.S. were $70\%$ to $91\%$ (pre-1981), or in Sweden in the 1980s where marginal rates exceeded $80\%$, cutting top rates reduced tax shelter usage and shifting enough that revenue losses among top earners were small or, in some narrow brackets, positive.
- **Highly elastic bases:** Taxes on bases that can easily move or be timed—such as realized capital gains, municipal tariffs, or corporate profits in small open economies (e.g., Ireland's historic shift to a $12.5\%$ corporate rate attracting multinational profit-shifting)—have much higher elasticities of taxable income ($e$) and can sometimes approach or exceed self-financing thresholds.

Specifically, a tax cut at rate $\tau$ is self-financing if and only if the elasticity of taxable income with respect to the net-of-tax rate ($1 - \tau$) satisfies:
\[
e \ge \frac{1 - \tau}{\tau}
\]
At a $\tau = 70\%$ tax rate, $e$ only needs to exceed $0.43$. At a $\tau = 30\%$ tax rate, $e$ must exceed $2.33$—far higher than typical empirical estimates for broad income taxes (which generally fall between $0.1$ and $0.5$).

### 3. Empirical evidence on broad tax cuts

Across major modern federal tax cuts—studied by the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), the Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT), the Treasury Department, and academic economists across the political spectrum (e.g., Mankiw & Weinzierl, Romer & Romer, Gale & Samwick, Auerbach)—broad tax cuts do not come close to paying for themselves:

- **Dynamic scoring feedback:** When the JCT, CBO, and academic macro models include supply-side growth effects ("dynamic scoring"), broad income and corporate tax cuts typically offset **$5\%$ to $30\%$** of their static revenue loss over a 10-year window—leaving $70\%$ to $95\%$ of the revenue loss intact (or worse once higher interest payments on the resulting debt and crowding out of private investment are included).
- **Major U.S. episodes (1981, 2001/2003, 2017):** After the 1981 Reagan tax cuts (ERTA), the 2001/2003 Bush tax cuts (EGTRRA/JGTRRA), and the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA), federal revenues as a share of GDP fell relative to baseline projections and relative to pre-cut trajectories. Note that sometimes nominal revenues rise over time after a tax cut simply because of inflation and baseline population/productivity growth; the relevant test is revenue relative to **what would have been collected without the cut**, and on that measure broad cuts consistently reduced revenue.
- **State-level experiments:** Kansas's 2012 tax cuts, explicitly predicted by proponents to pay for themselves rapidly via growth, produced immediate, persistent revenue shortfalls and underperforming growth compared to neighboring states, leading the legislature to repeal most of the cuts in 2017.

### 4. Distinguishing growth merits from revenue claims

Rejecting the claim that tax cuts pay for themselves does **not** mean tax cuts are always bad policy. Those are separate questions:
- **Revenue question (empirical):** Does the tax cut increase government revenue? Almost never for broad taxes at current rates.
- **Policy/value question:** Is a tax cut worth doing anyway to increase private disposable income, improve incentives, or shrink government spending, even though it reduces revenue and requires either spending cuts or higher deficits? That depends on fiscal priorities and economic conditions.

### Conditions that would change this conclusion

- **For a specific proposed tax cut:** If empirical evidence showed that the specific tax rate being cut was high enough and the targeted tax base elastic enough that $e \ge (1 - \tau)/\tau$ (after accounting for fiscal spillovers to other taxes and debt service), that specific cut would pay for itself.
- **For the universal claim ("always"):** Because revenue response depends on the starting tax rate $\tau$ and the elasticity $e$, the universal claim cannot hold across all rates (for instance, cutting a $5\%$ rate to $0\%$ necessarily reduces revenue to zero).
