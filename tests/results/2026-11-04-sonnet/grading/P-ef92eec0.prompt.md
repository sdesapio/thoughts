There's something to this, but it's not special to libertarians — it's true of basically every political identity, and treating it as a distinctive critique of libertarianism specifically is where the claim overreaches.

What's true: most people, across the whole spectrum, adopt political identities the way they adopt sports team loyalties — through social environment, personality fit, a handful of salient experiences, or opposition to something they dislike more. Political psychology research (Converse's classic work on "belief systems," more recent stuff from Achen & Bartels' "Democracy for Realists") backs this up pretty hard: most voters don't have coherent, reasoned-through ideological structures. They have identities first, and the policy positions get backfilled to match the identity and the in-group. This applies to progressives, conservatives, centrists, libertarians, all of it.

Where the claim might have a grain of specific truth: libertarianism has a few features that make the "tribal identity over reasoning" pattern more visible or more acute:

- It's unusually common for people to arrive at libertarianism in their late teens/early twenties via a specific gateway (Rand, Ron Paul, internet forums, a specific economics class) — a conversion-narrative pattern that looks more like identity adoption than most people's gradual political drift.
- It skews toward people who like the self-image of "the rational ones who reasoned their way here, unlike those tribal partisans" — which is itself often a tribal identity marker, just one with extra irony attached.
- It's a smaller, more online, more subculturally distinct group than "liberal" or "conservative," so its adherents are more likely to have a specific scene, specific inside references, specific aesthetics (gadsden flags, certain memes, certain authors) — more like a subculture than mainstream political identities are.

So the honest version of your claim: libertarianism isn't unusually irrational compared to other ideologies, but it may be unusually identity-flavored/subcultural in how people enter it, which can create the appearance of more "tribal adoption" even though the underlying psychology (identity first, reasoning after) is universal across ideologies.

I wouldn't accept "most people haven't reasoned their way there" as a special indictment of libertarians specifically, since that's the default condition of political belief for everyone, not a libertarian-specific failure.

[Assistant]
There's real evidence behind this, but it's not unique to progressives — it's a description of how most people acquire most political views across the spectrum.

What the evidence actually supports:

- Political identity for most people functions more like a social/tribal attachment than a conclusion derived from weighing arguments. Work in political psychology (e.g., Converse's "The Nature of Belief Systems," Achen & Bartels' *Democracy for Realists*, Lodge & Taber's motivated reasoning research) shows most people's issue positions are poorly organized, often inconsistent across related issues, and shift to match group/party cues rather than the reverse. People tend to find out what their "side" believes and then back-fill justifications.
- This is a general human cognition finding, not a partisan one. Studies on motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition (Dan Kahan's work), and partisan-motivated reasoning find the effect is roughly symmetric — conservatives and progressives both show it, and in most experiments the magnitude doesn't differ much by ideology. Claims that one side is uniquely "tribal" or uniquely "rational" generally don't hold up when tested with matched materials on both sides.

Where your claim overreaches:

- "Most people who hold progressive views haven't reasoned their way there" — true of most political views in general, but singling out progressives implies an asymmetry the data doesn't clearly show. If anything, some studies (e.g., Kahan's "motivated numeracy" experiments) find higher cognitive sophistication correlates with more motivated reasoning, not less, and this shows up on both left and right.
- "Adopted it as a tribal identity" is also true for people who hold it after reasoning. Reasoning and identity aren't mutually exclusive — someone can arrive at a position tribally and then also develop real reasons for it, or hold reasoned views that happen to align with their group. The binary framing (reasoned vs. tribal) is a bit of a false dichotomy; most people's beliefs are a mix, and the ratio varies a lot by issue and person, not just by ideology.

Bottom line: the mechanism you're describing (identity-first, reasons-later) is well-supported as a description of political cognition generally. The specific claim that this is especially or primarily a progressive phenomenon isn't supported — the honest version of your statement applies to "most people who hold strong political views," full stop, regardless of direction.
