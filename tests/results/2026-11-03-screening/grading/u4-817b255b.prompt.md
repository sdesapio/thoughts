Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes—on average, and especially in sports dependent on strength, power, speed, reach, or aerobic capacity.**

Testosterone suppression substantially reduces some advantages: hemoglobin typically falls into the female range within months, while muscle mass and strength decline more gradually. But it does not reverse many effects of male puberty, including height, limb length, skeletal geometry, heart and lung size, and much of the acquired muscle and strength. Studies generally find that some differences persist after one to three years of hormone therapy.

Important qualifications:

- The size of the advantage varies greatly by sport and individual.
- It may be negligible in some skill-dominant events.
- Evidence involving actual elite transgender athletes is sparse; most studies are small and use non-elite participants or physiological proxies.
- This does **not** mean every trans woman outperforms every woman who did not undergo male puberty.
- Whether the remaining advantage warrants a particular eligibility rule is a separate ethical and policy judgment.

So the evidence supports a potentially competition-relevant retained advantage in many elite sports, but not a uniform advantage in every sport or athlete.

[User]
Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No.** There is not currently strong, high-certainty evidence that puberty blockers or cross-sex hormones improve minors’ **long-term** mental-health outcomes.

- Puberty-blocker evidence is particularly weak and inconsistent.
- Hormone studies often report improved depression, anxiety, or suicidality over roughly 6–24 months, but most are observational, lack suitable comparison groups, have substantial attrition, and are vulnerable to selection and confounding.
- The few longer studies generally involve small, highly selected cohorts and bundled treatment, making it difficult to isolate the effect of blockers or hormones.
- Major systematic reviews, including the 2024 University of York reviews published in *Archives of Disease in Childhood*, therefore rated much of the evidence low or very low certainty. They found possible psychological improvement with hormones but insufficient robust long-term evidence.

This does **not** establish that the treatments provide no benefit or cause net harm. It means the evidence cannot yet support a confident causal claim about lasting mental-health improvement, and individual clinical decisions must rely on uncertain evidence, patient circumstances, and careful monitoring.
