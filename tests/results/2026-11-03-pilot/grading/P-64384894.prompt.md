### 1. On Politics as "Tribal Identity" vs. Ideological Reasoning
The idea that most voters adopt political labels through group identity rather than deep ideological reasoning comes from a foundational body of political science:
* **Philip Converse, *"The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics"* (1964):** The classic study showing that only a small fraction of the public holds coherent, logically consistent ideological frameworks ("ideologues"), while most vote based on group benefits or the nature of the times.
* **Donald Kinder & Nathan Kalmoe, *Neither Liberal nor Conservative: Ideological Innocence in the American Public* (2017):** Updates Converse’s work with modern data, concluding that genuine ideological thinking remains rare in the general public and that political attachments are primarily rooted in **group identity** (who "my people" are).
* **Lilliana Mason, *Uncivil Agreement: How Politics Became Our Identity* (2018):** Demonstrates through survey data how partisan and ideological labels have merged with racial, religious, and cultural identities ("social sorting"), causing people to defend their political camp for expressive, tribal reasons rather than policy disagreements.
* **Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels, *Democracy for Realists* (2016):** Argues against the "folk theory of democracy" (that voters weigh issues and pick the matching ideology/candidate) and shows that voters largely adopt the policy views of the social groups they already belong to.

### 2. On What People Think "Socialism" Means (Semantic Confusion)
The point that many supporters use the label as an identity or value statement rather than a textbook economic system is documented in public opinion polling:
* **Gallup, *"The Meaning of 'Socialism' to Americans Today"* (2018):** Gallup asked Americans an open-ended question: *What do you think of when you hear the word socialism?* 
  * In 1949, 34% defined it as government ownership of business/utilities. 
  * By 2018, only **17%** defined it as government ownership/control. Instead, the top definition (23%) was **"equality — equal standing for everybody, all equal in rights, equal in distribution,"** and 10% defined it simply as **"government benefits/services (healthcare, education)."** Another 6% defined it as "being social / talking to people / social media."
* **Pew Research Center, *"In Their Own Words: Behind Americans' Views of 'Socialism' and 'Capitalism'"* (2019):** Found that among the 42% of Americans who viewed socialism positively, the most common reason given (31%) was that it "creates a fairer, more generous society" or "builds upon and improves capitalism" (citing Nordic countries), rather than replacing capitalism with collective ownership.

### 3. On Material Grievances & Generational Shifts
The data showing that younger supporters are driven by specific economic pressures (healthcare, debt, housing) comes from demographic and economic research:

* **Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts:** Documented the massive generational wealth gap following the 2008 Great Recession (e.g., Millennials holding a tiny fraction of total U.S. wealth at age 35 compared to Baby Boomers at the same age), which political economists frequently cite as the material driver behind the resurgence of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) and the 2016/2020 Bernie Sanders campaigns.

Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Political psychology research suggests that for **most** people—across almost all political ideologies—beliefs are driven primarily by social identity, moral intuition, and group affiliation rather than first-principles reasoning. However, free-market and libertarian-leaning groups show a few distinct psychological patterns worth separating out.

### 1. The General Rule: Identity and Intuition First
Across the political spectrum, two well-documented mechanisms dominate how people form economic and political views:

* **Identity-Protective Cognition (Dan Kahan) & Partisan Identity (Lilliana Mason):** Most citizens adopt the policy positions of their social, cultural, or partisan "tribe" first. In countries like the United States, support for free markets is bundled with conservative or right-leaning partisan identity. People who identify with that group often adopt laissez-faire talking points as badges of membership, and will readily abandon free-market principles (e.g., supporting tariffs, subsidies, or corporate bailouts) when their party leader or in-group shifts position.
* **Moral Intuitionism (Jonathan Haidt):** People generally start with gut-level moral intuitions and use conscious reasoning later to justify them (acting more like a "defense lawyer" than a "scientist").

### 2. The Exception: Systemizing and Analytical Libertarians
When researchers separate out committed ideological supporters of laissez-faire capitalism (such as self-identified libertarians or classical liberals) from mainstream partisan conservatives, a different profile emerges:

* **High Need for Cognition and Systemizing:** In a major 2012 study (*Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians*), Jonathan Haidt and colleagues found that libertarians scored significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on **need for cognition**, **analytical/systemizing thinking**, and **cognitive reflection**, while scoring lower on empathy and social connectedness.
* **Rule-Based Reasoning:** Because this subgroup is less driven by warm group attachment or traditional authority, they are much more likely to have arrived at their views by consciously working through abstract systems—reading economics (Smith, Hayek, Friedman), philosophical arguments (Nozick, Rand), or applying strict deontological rules (like the non-aggression principle) to their logical conclusions.
* **Freedom as a Primary Moral Foundation:** For this group, individual liberty and autonomy function as the foundational moral axiom from which other positions are deduced.

### 3. Where Reasoning and Rationalization Blur
Even among those who explicitly "reason their way there," psychology points out a few caveats:

* **Starting Axioms Are Still Chosen:** Deductive reasoning requires starting premises. Choosing to prioritize *individual negative liberty* or *allocative efficiency* over *egalitarian outcomes* or *social cohesion* is ultimately a value judgment shaped by temperament, personality, and life experience—not pure logic alone.
* **Self-Interest and Just-World Beliefs:** People who have succeeded in competitive markets, or who work in private enterprise, are naturally more inclined to find free-market arguments persuasive (a form of motivated reasoning).
* **Intellectual Subcultures as Tribes:** Even highly analytical movements develop their own tribal dynamics—shared vocabulary, in-group heroes, Purity tests, and echo chambers—where defending the ideology becomes part of one's personal identity.

### Summary
* **Among the general public:** Support for laissez-faire capitalism is mostly **tribal and cultural**—adopted as part of a broader partisan or regional identity, and often held inconsistently.
* **Among committed ideological advocates (e.g., libertarians and classical liberals):** A unusually high proportion actually **do reason their way there** via abstract, system-level thinking—though the foundational values they build that logic upon are still rooted in personal temperament and intuition.
