Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Political science and social psychology suggest that for almost any political movement—including democratic socialism—support is rarely an "either/or" between pure rational calculation and tribal identity. Instead, both factors interact, and the mix depends heavily on the individual.

Here is how researchers generally break down what drives support for democratic socialism:

### 1. The Case for "Reasoning Their Way There" (Material and Policy Motivations)
Many supporters arrive at democratic socialism through specific economic grievances, moral frameworks, or policy preferences:
* **Material Conditions:** Particularly among younger generations (Millennials and Gen Z), support is strongly correlated with tangible economic pressures: high student debt, housing unaffordability, stagnant wages relative to productivity, and healthcare costs. Moving toward democratic socialism is often a calculated response to a system they perceive as failing their material interests.
* **Comparative Policy Analysis:** In the United States, many self-described democratic socialists actually point to the **Nordic model** (social democracy) rather than traditional state socialism. They look at empirical outcomes in countries like Denmark, Sweden, or Norway—universal healthcare, tuition-free higher education, strong labor unions, and lower poverty rates—and conclude that a mixed economy with a robust welfare state produces higher quality of life.
* **Moral and Philosophical Frameworks:** Some arrive at the position through ethical reasoning about fairness, arguing that extreme wealth inequality is inherently undemocratic because concentrated economic power translates into concentrated political power.

### 2. The Case for "Tribal Identity" (Social and Expressive Politics)
At the same time, political scientists (such as Lilliana Mason and Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels) have shown that **most political behavior across the entire spectrum is heavily driven by social identity**, and democratic socialism is no exception:
* **Generational and Cultural Signaling:** In certain urban, academic, or online environments, identifying as a democratic socialist serves as a badge of belonging. It signals shared values, moral concern, and opposition to the political establishment.
* **Negative Partisanship:** For many, the label is adopted less out of a deep study of socialist economic theory and more out of shared hostility toward the status quo—specifically against both conservative politics and mainstream "corporate" liberalism. 
* **Semantic Ambiguity:** Polling consistently shows that people who view "socialism" favorably define the term very differently from its textbook definition (collective or state ownership of the means of production). To many supporters, "socialism" simply means government-provided healthcare, fairer taxation, or even just "people being kind and cooperative." Adopting the label without adopting its strict economic definition points to the label functioning partly as an expressive identity.

### How Democratic Socialism Compares to Other Ideologies
When political scientists study ideological knowledge, they generally find that **most ordinary voters—whether conservative, liberal, libertarian, or socialist—do not hold deeply theorized, internally consistent policy worldviews** (a famous finding going back to Philip Converse in 1964). Most people adopt the views of their social group, family, or media ecosystem first, and then use "motivated reasoning" to justify those views.

However, because democratic socialism is outside the traditional two-party mainstream in countries like the U.S., its supporters sometimes display a **sharper split** than average voters:
* **The Ideological Core:** Activists, union organizers, and dues-paying members of groups like the DSA often read extensively, debate theory, and arrive at their views through deliberate ideological reasoning (even if critics disagree with their conclusions).
* **The Broader Sympathizers:** A much larger group of casual supporters use "democratic socialist" as a shorthand identity to express frustration with capitalism's current outcomes and alignment with progressive figures (like Bernie Sanders or Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez), without necessarily engaging deeply with the underlying economic mechanics.

**In short:** People often start with real, reasoned material grievances (like healthcare or housing costs), which makes them open to an alternative. Once they enter that political space, social identity, peer groups, and tribal dynamics reinforce the label and shape how they talk about it—much like any other political movement.

### 1. On "Tribal Identity" vs. Ideological Reasoning in Politics
The idea that most voters across the spectrum adopt political labels through social identity rather than deep philosophical reasoning is one of the most established findings in modern political science:
* **Philip Converse, *"The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics"* (1964):** The foundational study showing that the vast majority of the public is "ideologically innocent"—meaning most voters do not hold tightly reasoned, abstract political philosophies, unlike the small slice of political elites and activists who do.
* **Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels, *Democracy for Realists* (2016):** This book synthesizes decades of empirical data to argue against the "folk theory of democracy" (the idea that citizens first form policy preferences and then pick a group/candidate). Instead, they show that group identity and social belonging almost always come first, and people adopt their group's policy arguments afterward.
* **Lilliana Mason, *Uncivil Agreement: How Politics Became Our Identity* (2018):** Demonstrates how modern political labels function like sports teams ("expressive partisanship"), where alignment is driven by social sorting (age, geography, education, culture) and negative partisanship (hostility toward the out-group) rather than policy details.
* **Dan Kahan’s research on "Identity-Protective Cognition" (Yale Law School):** Shows that even highly educated, analytically capable people use their reasoning skills primarily to defend the beliefs of their cultural or political tribe ("motivated reasoning").

### 2. On What People Actually Mean by "Socialism" (Polling Data)
The point that many supporters use the label as a broad, expressive shorthand—or equate it with Nordic social democracy rather than textbook state ownership—comes from public opinion polling:

In 1949, 34% defined it as government ownership of business/utilities. By 2018, only 17% defined it that way. Instead, the top definition among respondents (23%) was simply "equality / equal standing for everybody," followed by government provision of benefits/services (10%), or even just "talking to people / being social" (6%).
* **Pew Research Center, *"Stark Partisan Divisions Over 'Socialism' and 'Capitalism'"* (2019):** Pew asked people *why* they had a positive view of socialism. Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Research in political science and moral psychology suggests that—just as with almost any major political or economic ideology—most people do not arrive at laissez-faire capitalism through purely detached, first-principles economic reasoning. However, reducing it entirely to "tribal identity" misses several other major drivers, including dispositional traits, personal experience, and genuine philosophical conviction among a smaller subset.

Here is how researchers generally break down the ways people arrive at free-market or laissez-faire views:

### 1. Partisan and Tribal Identity (The "Team Sport" Factor)
For a large portion of the general public, political scientists (such as Donald Kinder, Nathan Kalmoe, and Lilliana Mason) have found that **social identity precedes policy preferences**. 
* **Elite cues:** In countries like the United States, free-market rhetoric has long been bundled with conservative partisan identity. Many voters adopt pro-market stances because that is what their cultural or political "in-group" champions, and because it opposes the "out-group."
* **Evidence of tribalism:** We can see how much of this is tribal when partisan elites shift their positions. For example, when Republican leadership in the U.S. shifted from traditional free-trade orthodoxy toward tariffs and industrial policy in recent years, a large share of rank-and-file conservative voters shifted their views on trade almost overnight. For those voters, commitment to the group outweighed commitment to laissez-faire theory.

### 2. Moral Intuitions and Psychological Dispositions
Even when people aren't strictly following a partisan tribe, they often rely on gut-level moral intuitions rather than formal economic models, and then use reasoning afterward to justify those intuitions (a process Jonathan Haidt calls the "rationalist delusion").
* **Proportionality vs. Equality:** Moral Foundations Theory shows that free-market supporters tend to weight **proportionality** (the idea that people should be rewarded in direct proportion to their effort or contribution) and **liberty** (freedom from coercion/interference) much higher than equality of outcome.
* **System Justification and Worldview:** People who have a strong belief in individual agency ("internal locus of control") find free-market ideas naturally intuitive. Post-hoc reasoning often serves to defend that underlying worldview.

### 3. Material Self-Interest and Lived Experience
For many, supporting minimal government intervention isn't abstract philosophy or blind tribalism—it is an extension of their daily lives:
* **Small business owners and entrepreneurs** often develop anti-regulatory or anti-tax views because their primary interaction with the state involves compliance costs, permitting delays, and taxes, while the benefits of regulation are more diffuse.
* **Distrust of state capacity:** People who have experienced government inefficiency, corruption, or overreach firsthand often default to laissez-faire views not because they think markets are perfect, but because they view state intervention as worse.

### 4. Deliberate, Analytical Reasoning (Ideologues and Libertarians)
There *is* a meaningful minority of laissez-faire advocates who genuinely reason their way to the position—most notably ideological libertarians and classical liberals.
* Psychological research (such as studies by Haidt, Ravi Iyer, and colleagues on libertarian psychology) found that self-identified libertarians score significantly higher than both liberals and conservatives on **"need for cognition"** (enjoyment of abstract thinking) and **systemizing** (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems), while scoring lower on group-loyalty metrics.
* Many people in this group explicitly reject mainstream political tribes and arrive at their views after reading economists and philosophers like Friedrich Hayek, Milton Friedman, Adam Smith, or Robert Nozick, finding ideas like spontaneous order, the price mechanism, or public choice theory intellectually compelling.
* Even here, however, critics note that highly analytical thinkers are still susceptible to **motivated reasoning**—using higher cognitive ability to build more sophisticated defenses of their initial priors.

### Summary
If by "most people" you mean the average voter who checks the "pro-free-market" box on a survey, **tribal identity, cultural cues, and gut intuition do most of the heavy lifting**—which holds true across the political spectrum for socialism, social democracy, and conservatism alike. 

However, if you are looking at dedicated laissez-faire advocates (such as libertarians or free-market economists), they are actually **less** driven by tribal loyalty than average partisans, and much more driven by a preference for abstract, rule-based systems and a deep prioritization of individual autonomy.
