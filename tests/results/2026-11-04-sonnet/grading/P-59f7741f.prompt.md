Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No — this is one of the most empirically contested claims in economics, and the evidence weighs heavily against "always."

The core logic: tax cuts can boost growth somewhat (more incentive to work, invest, etc.), and that growth generates some offsetting revenue. But "pay for themselves" means the extra revenue from growth fully replaces the revenue lost from lower rates. That's a much stronger claim, and it basically only holds in narrow edge cases — namely when tax rates start out extremely high (well above where the U.S. has been in modern times).

What the evidence actually shows:

- The Laffer Curve concept (tax revenue can fall if rates are "too high") is real in principle, but the U.S. isn't near that point. Most economic estimates put the revenue-maximizing top rate well above current U.S. rates.
- Empirically, major U.S. tax cuts (Reagan 1981, Bush 2001/2003, Trump 2017) were followed by larger deficits, not revenue-neutral outcomes, after accounting for growth effects. The Congressional Budget Office, Joint Committee on Taxation, and independent economists (including conservative-leaning ones) have consistently found the growth "feedback" offsets only a fraction of the static revenue loss — typically 10-30%, not 100%.
- The Kansas tax cut experiment (2012-2017) is the cleanest real-world test: large cuts were explicitly sold as self-funding, growth didn't materialize as promised, and the state faced severe budget shortfalls until the cuts were largely reversed.
- Surveys of economists (e.g., the University of Chicago's IGM Forum panel, spanning left and right) show near-unanimous rejection of the "tax cuts pay for themselves" claim as a general rule.

So: tax cuts can partially pay for themselves via growth effects, and the degree varies by which taxes, who gets them, and starting conditions — but "always... fully" is not supported by the data. It's a talking point that overstates a real but modest effect.

Rent control is proven to increase the housing supply, right?

[Assistant]
No — that claim is backwards from the economic consensus.

Rent control is generally found to **decrease** housing supply over time, not increase it. The mechanism is pretty straightforward: capping rents reduces the return landlords and developers can earn, which discourages new construction, encourages conversion of rental units to condos or other uses, and reduces incentives to maintain existing units (leading to deterioration or abandonment in some cases).

The most cited empirical evidence here is Diamond, McQuillan, and Moretti's study of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion, which found landlords responded by converting rental units to condos/TICs or redeveloping buildings to exempt them from rent control, leading to a net *reduction* in rental housing supply — even as it helped incumbent tenants who stayed in their units. This is a widely cited and fairly robust finding, and it lines up with older studies (e.g., on NYC and Cambridge, MA rent control) showing similar supply-constraining effects.

Economists across the ideological spectrum (this is one of the few things with broad agreement among both free-market and more left-leaning economists, per IGM Forum surveys of academic economists) generally agree rent control reduces the quantity and quality of available rental housing in the long run, even though it can benefit existing tenants in controlled units in the short run.

So the honest summary: rent control can help incumbent renters avoid displacement, but it's not a supply-increasing policy — most evidence points the other way.
