[Assistant]
No. The claim that tax cuts **always** pay for themselves through growth is false, and this is one of the more empirically settled questions in public finance.

## Decomposing the claim

"Pay for themselves" means the revenue lost from lower rates is fully offset by new revenue from faster economic growth. "Always" is a universal quantifier — no exceptions, regardless of which tax, what rate, or what conditions. The claim is empirical and testable.

## What the evidence shows

**Official scoring bodies** — the CBO, JCT, and Treasury's Office of Tax Analysis — have consistently found that major tax cuts reduce net revenue even after dynamic scoring (i.e., accounting for growth feedback). This includes:

- **1981 Reagan cuts (ERTA):** Federal revenue as a share of GDP dropped from ~19.6% to ~17.3% over the following years. Deficits rose sharply.
- **2001/2003 Bush cuts:** Converted a surplus into deficits. CBO attributed a large share of the fiscal deterioration to the revenue reductions.
- **2017 TCJA:** CBO estimated ~$1.5 trillion in added deficits over ten years *after* dynamic effects. Revenue as a share of GDP declined in 2018–2019, before the pandemic.

**The growth feedback is real but partial.** Most empirical estimates find that income tax cuts recover roughly 10–30% of the static revenue loss through higher growth — far short of 100%. Gregory Mankiw, a Republican-appointed CEA chair, has stated plainly that broad-based income tax cuts do not pay for themselves.

## The strongest faithful version of the claim

The theoretical basis — the Laffer Curve — is valid in principle: at some rate, revenue is maximized, and cuts above that rate can increase revenue. When the top marginal rate was 91% (pre-1964) or 70% (pre-1981), cuts from those peaks had larger feedback effects. In narrow cases like capital gains rate reductions, timing and behavioral responses can produce surprising short-term revenue effects.

But this supports only: *Some tax cuts, from sufficiently high rates, under specific conditions, can recover a large share of lost revenue — and in rare cases, possibly all of it.* That is a radically different claim from "always."

## Verdict

The universal claim fails. The empirical record provides multiple clear counterexamples across decades. The partial feedback from growth is real but typically recovers a fraction of lost revenue, not all of it. The "always" qualifier makes the claim especially indefensible — even advocates of supply-side economics who are careful with evidence do not make the universal version.

**Confidence:** Near-certain. This is supported by decades of revenue data, consistent findings from nonpartisan scoring bodies, and broad agreement among economists across the political spectrum.

**Revision condition:** Evidence that broad-based tax cuts from current-level rates consistently generate revenue exceeding the static loss would require reassessment. No such evidence currently exists.

**Key evidence and mechanisms:**

- **Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (2019)** studied San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion and found that rent-controlled buildings saw roughly a 15% reduction in rental housing supply. Landlords responded by converting units to condos, redeveloping buildings into uses exempt from the ordinance, or otherwise withdrawing units from the rental market.
- **Broad survey evidence** (e.g., Jenkins 2009, reviewing over 100 studies) finds consistent results: rent control reduces both the quantity and quality of available housing.
- **IGM Forum polls** of leading economists show near-consensus that rent ceilings reduce the quantity and quality of rental housing available.

**The mechanisms are straightforward:**

1. Capped returns reduce the incentive to build new rental housing.
2. Landlords convert rental units to condos or other exempt uses.
3. Reduced revenue leads to deferred maintenance, deterioration, and eventual unit withdrawal.
4. Tenants lock into units that no longer match their needs (misallocation), reducing turnover and effective availability.

**Strongest faithful case for rent control:** It can preserve affordability and stability for *current tenants* in the short term, and careful design (exempting new construction, allowing inflation-linked annual increases) can mitigate the supply reduction. But even this version does not claim rent control increases supply — it argues the damage can be limited.

**Bottom line:** The claim reverses the established evidence. Rent control is consistently associated with reduced housing supply. Confidence in this conclusion is high, grounded in decades of empirical work across multiple cities and policy designs.

**What would change this assessment:** Large-scale empirical evidence demonstrating that a specific rent control design, under identified conditions, produced a net increase in housing units. No such evidence currently exists in the mainstream literature.
