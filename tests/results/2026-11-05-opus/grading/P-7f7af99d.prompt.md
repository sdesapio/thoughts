It's partially true but misleadingly framed, because it singles out progressives for something that applies across the entire political spectrum.

**What's true:** Most people holding *any* political views haven't rigorously reasoned their way there. Political psychology research (Haidt, Kahan, etc.) is pretty clear that humans generally form political intuitions first and rationalize them after. Social identity, upbringing, peer groups, personality traits, and media environment do most of the heavy lifting. This applies to progressives, yes.

**What's misleading:** Framing this as a progressive-specific problem. Conservatives, libertarians, populists — same thing. Rural conservative identity is every bit as "tribal" as urban progressive identity. The guy who can't articulate why he opposes estate tax reform beyond "government shouldn't take your money" is doing the same thing as the person who can't articulate why they support a specific equity policy beyond "it's the right thing to do." Motivated reasoning is a human feature, not a left-wing one.

**What's also worth noting:**

- The *content* of a belief isn't invalidated by *how* someone arrived at it. A view adopted tribally can still be correct.
- There are deeply reasoned progressives and deeply reasoned conservatives. There are also unreflective ones on both sides. The ratio is probably similar.
- The framing of your question ("Is that right?") itself has a tribal quality — it's a common conservative/contrarian talking point that flatters the asker's in-group as the more rational one. That doesn't make it wrong, but it's worth noticing.

The honest version of the claim is: "Most people across the political spectrum adopt views largely through social identity rather than independent reasoning." That's well-supported. The version that says progressives are uniquely guilty of this is not.

[Assistant]
This is true, but it's not especially true of libertarians — it's true of virtually every political identity. The research on motivated reasoning and political belief formation (Kahan, Haidt, etc.) is pretty clear: most people across the spectrum arrive at political views through social identity, temperament, and group affiliation first, then backfill reasoning. Libertarians aren't unique here.

That said, there are a few things worth noting that are somewhat specific to libertarianism:

**Arguments for your claim:**
- There's a recognizable libertarian *aesthetic* and subculture (especially online) that people clearly adopt as a package deal — the reading lists, the memes, the rhetorical style. That's identity adoption.
- Many self-identified libertarians hold views that are internally inconsistent (e.g., pro-police, pro-military, anti-immigration) in ways that suggest they adopted the label for cultural reasons rather than from working through the underlying principles.
- The pipeline from "contrarian teenager" to "libertarian" is real and is more about disposition and social positioning than careful study of political philosophy.

**Arguments against overstating it:**
- Libertarianism is actually *unusual* among political ideologies in that it has a relatively coherent intellectual tradition people can and do engage with (Hayek, Friedman, Nozick, etc.). It's more philosophically systematized than most mainstream political identities.
- Because libertarianism is a minority position, adopting it often *costs* you socially rather than earning tribal belonging. That's the opposite of the usual tribal-identity incentive structure.
- Survey data suggests libertarians score somewhat higher on "need for cognition" measures than the general population, for whatever that's worth.

**Bottom line:** You're more right than wrong about any large group of people who share a political label. But singling out libertarians for this is a bit unfair — the same critique hits mainstream Democrats, Republicans, socialists, and everyone else at least as hard, and arguably harder, since those identities are more socially rewarded and require less friction to adopt.
