There’s no solid evidence that most democratic-socialism supporters arrived there through either pure reasoning or pure tribal identity.

Like supporters of nearly every ideology, they usually combine:

- Moral intuitions—equality, fairness, solidarity
- Personal experience—healthcare, debt, wages, unions
- Social identity—friends, class, generation, political group
- Deliberation—comparisons of institutions, outcomes, and tradeoffs

Motivated reasoning and identity-based politics occur across ideological groups. A better test of genuine reasoning is whether someone can state opposing arguments accurately, acknowledge tradeoffs, and revise beliefs when evidence changes.

Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Probably neither explanation alone—and there’s no solid evidence that “most” supporters arrived by one route.

Political beliefs usually emerge from a mix of:

- social identity and trusted communities,
- temperament and moral priorities, such as autonomy or property rights,
- perceived self-interest and life experience,
- exposure to economic arguments,
- post-hoc reasoning that defends an existing intuition.

Committed laissez-faire advocates may have reasoned through coherent arguments about incentives, decentralized knowledge, and government failure. But reasoning is rarely identity-free; people selectively accept arguments that fit their group and values. The same is true of socialists, centrists, and nearly every political camp.

So the best-supported generalization is: people often reason *within* an identity-influenced framework, rather than either deriving their politics from first principles or merely copying their tribe. Also, strict laissez-faire capitalism is less popular than labels such as “free market,” so polling based on self-identification can exaggerate agreement on the actual policies.
