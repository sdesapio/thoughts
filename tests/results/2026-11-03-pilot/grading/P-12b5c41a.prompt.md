Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes—on average, and especially in sports dependent on strength, power, speed, endurance, reach, or body size.

Male puberty produces lasting skeletal dimensions, leverage, heart and lung capacity, and substantial muscle adaptations. Testosterone suppression reduces hemoglobin, muscle mass, and strength, but it does not fully reverse many puberty-driven traits. Direct studies of elite transgender athletes remain small and limited, so the size of the advantage varies and cannot be precisely quantified for every sport.

This does not mean every trans woman beats every female athlete, or that the advantage matters equally in all events. It means that, compared with similarly talented and trained female athletes, prior male puberty generally leaves performance-relevant advantages in many elite sports. Whether that warrants a particular eligibility rule is a separate ethical and policy question.

[User]
Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
No—not if “good evidence” means moderate- or high-certainty evidence of lasting causal improvement over many years.

Some observational studies report improved depression, suicidality, or general well-being after puberty blockers or hormones. However, most have short follow-up, small or selected samples, substantial attrition, no adequate comparison group, and vulnerability to confounding—such as differences in family support and baseline mental health.

Systematic reviews, including NICE’s reviews and the 2024 University of York reviews, have consequently rated the evidence as low or very low certainty. Evidence is especially limited for puberty blockers considered separately; hormone studies offer somewhat more positive findings, but generally over only one or two years.

This does not establish that treatment provides no mental-health benefit. It means the size, durability, and causal basis of any benefit remain uncertain. Clinical recommendations may still incorporate patient circumstances, dysphoria relief, physical effects, risks, and values, but they should not be represented as resting on strong evidence of improved long-term mental health.
