Do most people who support laissez-faire capitalism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
Honestly, it's almost certainly a mix of both, and the mix varies a lot by person — but very few people arrive at a comprehensive laissez-faire position through pure first-principles reasoning alone, and few hold it as *pure* tribal identity either. A few ways to think about it:

**The "reasoning" component is real but usually partial**
People who hold strong free-market views often can articulate reasons — Hayek's knowledge problem, Friedman-style arguments about price signals and incentives, public-choice critiques of regulatory capture, historical examples of planned economies failing, moral arguments about self-ownership and non-coercion (Nozick, Rothbard). These aren't fake reasons; they're real intellectual traditions with serious arguments. Someone who's read Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" or Friedman's "Free to Choose" and finds it convincing isn't just parroting a tribe — they've engaged an argument.

**But motivated reasoning and identity do a lot of the heavy lifting**
A few things point this way:
- Political/economic views correlate heavily with group identity (party, religion, class, region, media diet) far more than they correlate with, say, having formally studied economics.
- People rarely update much on contrary evidence (e.g., empirical findings on minimum wage effects, or market failures like externalities and information asymmetry) in ways that would be predicted by pure rational updating.
- The specific *bundle* of positions people hold (low taxes + deregulation + often also particular views on immigration, guns, culture) doesn't follow logically from "markets allocate resources efficiently" — that's a sign the bundle is held together by in-group signaling, not derivation from first principles.
- Jonathan Haidt's and others' research on motivated moral reasoning suggests most people form an intuitive/tribal commitment first and then build or adopt justifications after the fact — this applies across the political spectrum, not just to free-market advocates.

**A reasonable synthesis**
Most people likely:
1. Absorb a general orientation from family, culture, religion, or social milieu (tribal/identity layer).
2. Encounter arguments and evidence that get filtered through confirmation bias, so they accumulate reasons that *reinforce* the prior rather than ones that could overturn it.
3. End up with genuine, coherent-sounding arguments they can deploy — making it look purely reasoned from the outside — while the actual causal history was identity-first.

A smaller subset — economists, philosophers, people who've seriously engaged opposing arguments and are willing to update on specific sub-claims (e.g., "markets are usually efficient but externalities justify some intervention") — are closer to reasoning their way there, or at least updating incrementally via reasoning.

So: not an either/or. It's closer to "identity sets the prior, reasoning fills in the justification and occasionally updates the margins," and that pattern isn't unique to laissez-faire capitalism — it describes most strongly-held ideological positions, left or right.

Do most people who support democratic socialism actually reason their way there, or is it more of a tribal identity thing?

[Assistant]
In reality it's almost always both, tangled together, and the honest answer is that pure cases of either are rare.

**The case for "they reason their way there":** Plenty of democratic socialists can give a coherent account — critiques of how capital concentrates power, arguments about decommodifying healthcare/housing/education, comparisons to Nordic-style welfare states, readings of inequality data, moral arguments about democratizing the workplace. These aren't crazy or illegitimate reasons; serious political philosophy and economics underpin them, and serious people disagree with those conclusions using their own reasoning. Political identity in general correlates with upbringing, exposure to arguments, personal experience (e.g., medical debt, unemployment), and material circumstances in ways that look like reasoning from a particular vantage point.

**The case for "it's tribal":** Political scientists who study this (Converse's classic work on "belief systems," more recent work by people like Achen and Bartels in *Democracy for Realists*) find that most voters, across the spectrum, don't hold deeply reasoned, internally consistent ideological positions. Instead, group identity and social belonging tend to come first, and the policy reasoning gets backfilled to justify what the in-group already believes. Self-identified socialists aren't special here — this applies to self-identified libertarians, conservatives, centrists, etc. Signaling, aesthetics (who's "cringe," what symbols you adopt), and social belonging do a lot of work for a lot of people regardless of ideology.

**The more accurate framing:** it's probably better to think of it as a spectrum/mixture rather than two separate camps:
- A minority on any end are genuine policy wonks who arrived at their position through sustained argument and evidence, and could steelman the opposition.
- A large middle reasons somewhat, but mostly reasons *toward* a conclusion their social group already holds (motivated reasoning).
- Some fraction really is mostly tribal/aesthetic — attracted to vibes, community, moral identity — with minimal engagement with the actual economic arguments.

There's also a generational/contextual angle specific to democratic socialism's recent rise (post-2008, post-2016 Sanders/AOC): a lot of its newer supporters came to it via reaction to visible institutional failures (financial crisis, healthcare costs, housing unaffordability) rather than through reading Marx or mid-century labor economics — which is still "reasoning" but reasoning from lived experience and salient news events rather than systematic theory.

So: not purely tribal, not purely reasoned — like basically every other ideological label, including the ones opposed to it.
