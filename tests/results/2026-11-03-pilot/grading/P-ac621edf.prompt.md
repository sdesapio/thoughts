In standard supply-and-demand analysis, a binding price ceiling reduces the expected return on building, maintaining, or leasing rental housing. No. The universal claim that tax cuts *always* pay for themselves is false, and even for typical broad-based tax cuts in modern advanced economies, the empirical and theoretical consensus is that they only partially offset their static revenue loss.

### 1. Defining "Pay for Themselves"

In public finance, a tax cut "pays for itself" if the **dynamic revenue effect**—the additional tax revenue generated by induced economic growth, increased labor supply, higher investment, or reduced tax avoidance—equals or exceeds the **static revenue loss** (the mechanical drop in revenue assuming behavior does not change) over a defined budget window (typically 10 years, or in present-value terms over the long run) without requiring spending cuts or other tax increases.

*(Note: Because no external retrieval was used in this response, specific historical estimates below reflect established public-finance literature rather than live-retrieved primary tables.)*

### 2. The Accounting and Behavioral Threshold

Total tax revenue \(R\) can be simplified as the tax rate \(t\) multiplied by the taxable base \(B(t)\), where the base is a function of the rate:
\[
R(t) = t \cdot B(t)
\]
When the tax rate is cut, you collect a smaller share (\(t\) falls) of every existing dollar in the tax base. For total revenue \(R\) to rise or stay flat, the percent increase in the tax base must be at least as large as the percent decrease in the tax rate. 

Expressed in terms of the **elasticity of taxable income (ETI)** with respect to the net-of-tax rate \((1 - t)\), a tax cut is self-financing only if:
\[
\text{ETI} \ge \frac{1 - t}{t}
\]
This creates a strict mathematical hurdle:
* **At a 90% tax rate:** \(\frac{1 - 0.90}{0.90} \approx 0.11\). Only a tiny behavioral response (an elasticity of 0.11) is needed for a rate cut to increase revenue.
* **At a 50% tax rate:** \(\frac{1 - 0.50}{0.50} = 1.0\). An elasticity of 1.0 is required.
* **At a 35% tax rate:** \(\frac{1 - 0.35}{0.35} \approx 1.86\). The taxable base must respond nearly twice as strongly in percentage terms as the change in the after-tax share.

In empirical public finance (e.g., reviews by Saez, Slemrod, and Giertz), estimated long-run elasticities of broad taxable income for U.S. earners generally fall well below 1.0 (often in the 0.1 to 0.4 range). At existing statutory and effective tax rates in most developed economies, the behavioral and growth response is nowhere near large enough to clear the self-financing threshold for broad tax cuts.

### 3. The Strongest Case for Self-Financing Cuts (and Its Limits)

The valid core of the argument—associated with the Laffer curve—is that **marginal incentives and tax avoidance matter**, and under specific conditions, cutting a tax rate can raise revenue:

1. **Punitively high marginal rates:** When top statutory rates are extreme (such as the 70%–91% top U.S. individual rates in the mid-20th century, or Sweden's >80% marginal effective rates in the 1970s–1980s), cutting top rates can substantially reduce tax sheltering, shifting of income, and avoidance. Even then, empirical studies of the 1981 and 1986 U.S. tax reforms found that while top-bracket revenue shares responded noticeably, broad across-the-board income tax cuts still reduced overall federal revenues.
2. **Highly elastic, narrow, or mobile tax bases:** Taxes on bases with easy substitutes or high timing flexibility—such as extreme local excise taxes (where buyers cross borders), capital gains realization taxes (where owners can delay selling indefinitely), or small open economies competing for mobile multinational paper profits—sometimes sit above the revenue-maximizing rate.
3. **Long-run capital accumulation:** Corporate and capital tax cuts have a stronger theoretical channel for growth because lowering the user cost of capital increases the steady-state capital stock and labor productivity over decades. However, dynamic scoring models (such as those used by the Congressional Budget Office, the Joint Committee on Taxation, and the Penn Wharton Budget Model) consistently find that for broad U.S. corporate and individual tax cuts (including the 2001, 2003, and 2017 cuts), growth offsets only a fraction—typically estimated between **10% and 35%**—of the static revenue loss over a 10-year window.

Furthermore, when a tax cut is **deficit-financed** rather than paired with spending reductions, increased government borrowing can partially crowd out private investment and raise interest costs, shrinking the net growth feedback.

### 4. Conclusion and Conditions for Revision

* **On the universal claim ("always"):** **False (near-certain).** Both basic arithmetic (as \(t \to 0\), revenue approaches 0) and historical budget outcomes refute the claim that tax cuts always pay for themselves.
* **On broad federal income or corporate tax cuts from current U.S./OECD baselines:** **Highly probable that they lose net revenue.** They typically generate positive supply-side or demand-side growth effects that recover a partial fraction of the static cost, while increasing the fiscal deficit unless paired with spending cuts.
* **What would change the assessment for a specific proposal:** A specific tax cut would be expected to pay for itself if independent empirical estimates showed the existing tax rate on that specific base exceeded the revenue-maximizing threshold \(t^* = \frac{1}{1 + \text{ETI}}\)—for instance, if the cut targeted a narrow, highly elastic, or heavily avoided tax at an already high rate. Conversely, if your goal is maximizing private after-tax income or GDP growth rather than maintaining government revenue neutrality, a tax cut can still be desirable on those criteria even when it reduces net tax revenue.
