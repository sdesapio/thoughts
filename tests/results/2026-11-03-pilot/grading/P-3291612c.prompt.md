### 1. Definitions and Scope
Evaluating the claim requires separating two distinct metrics often conflated under "housing supply":
- **Rental housing stock:** The number of units available to rent in a regulated market.
- **Total housing construction:** The total number of residential units (rental plus owner-occupied) built over time.

It also requires distinguishing between **first-generation rent control** (hard price freezes across the board) and **second-generation rent stabilization** (caps on annual rent growth, typically exempting newly constructed buildings and sometimes resetting upon vacancy).

### 2. Why the Claim Fails: Mechanism and Empirical Evidence
For rent control to increase the housing supply, capping the price or return on rental units would have to induce developers to build more units or induce owners to keep/convert more units into rentals. Standard price theory predicts the opposite: lowering the expected return on rental property relative to alternative uses (such as condominiums, owner-occupied housing, or non-real-estate investments) reduces the incentive to supply rental units.

Empirical natural experiments consistently support this mechanism:
- **San Francisco (1994 expansion):** Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (*American Economic Review*, 2019) examined the 1994 ballot initiative that extended rent control to small multifamily buildings built before 1980. While incumbent tenants benefited from lower rents and stayed longer, landlords of regulated buildings reduced the supply of available rental housing by roughly 15%—primarily by converting units to condos or TICs (tenancies in common), redeveloping buildings into exempt structures, or moving in themselves.
- **Cambridge, Massachusetts (1995 repeal):** Sims (*Journal of Urban Economics*, 2007) and Autor, Palmer, and Pathak (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2014) studied the sudden statewide abolition of rent control in 1995. Under rent control, units had been shifted out of the rental stock and suffered lower maintenance investment; following repeal, residential property values, maintenance investment, and the return of units to the rental market increased.
- **New Jersey and Washington, D.C.:** Studies of moderate second-generation stabilization laws that exempt new construction (e.g., Gilderbloom and Ye, 2007; Turner, 1990) generally find little to no statistically significant effect on *new* construction rates—because new builds are exempt—or find modest declines if developers anticipate that the exemption window will eventually be rolled back. Even in these cases, the effect on new supply is at best neutral, not positive.

### 3. The Strongest Faithful Case for a Non-Negative Supply Effect
The strongest theoretical argument that rent regulation could avoid shrinking (or theoretically increase) supply is a **monopsony/market-power model**: if a single landlord or cartel dominates a local housing market and artificially restricts quantity to drive up rents, a price ceiling set between the competitive price and the monopoly price could theoretically induce the monopolist to fill vacant units.

However, this does not establish the user's premise in practice:
1. Urban housing markets typically have thousands of distinct property owners; empirical concentration studies do not find the market structure required for textbook monopoly quantity restriction to be the primary driver of housing shortages (which are instead heavily driven by zoning, land-use restrictions, and construction costs).
2. Modern second-generation laws are designed specifically to *mitigate* supply losses by exempting new construction for 15–30 years or permanently. An exemption is a policy concession that rent control otherwise depresses construction, not a mechanism by which rent control increases it.

### 4. What Rent Control Actually Does (Distinct from Supply)
Proponents of rent stabilization generally defend it on **tenant stability and anti-displacement** grounds—functions where empirical evidence (including Diamond et al., 2019) shows it does succeed for incumbent tenants in the short to medium run—rather than as a tool to expand the housing stock. Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No. As a general rule, broad tax cuts do not pay for themselves through economic growth. They can generate some additional economic activity that offsets a fraction of the lost revenue, and cuts to specific, exceptionally high or narrowly targeted rates can occasionally be self-financing, but "always" is false both in theory and in historical experience.

### What "paying for itself" requires

To keep the meaning consistent, a tax cut "pays for itself" if total government revenue over a defined budget window (typically 10 years, or in present-value terms) is at least as high after the cut as it would have been under the baseline law, holding spending policy fixed.

Total tax revenue (\(R\)) is roughly the effective tax rate (\(t\)) multiplied by the tax base (\(B\)): \(R = t \times B\).

When you cut the tax rate by \(\Delta t\), you lose mechanical revenue on the existing base (\(\Delta t \times B\)) and gain revenue on whatever new economic growth the cut induces (\(t_{\text{new}} \times \Delta B\)). For the cut to be 100% self-financing, the percentage increase in the tax base must be at least as large as the percentage reduction in the tax rate:

\[\frac{\Delta B}{B} \ge \frac{|\Delta t|}{t_{\text{new}}}\]

At normal tax rates in modern advanced economies, that arithmetic bar is very high. For example, if an effective tax rate of 25% is cut to 20% (a 20% reduction in the rate), the tax base must grow by **25% above its baseline path**—permanently and solely because of the tax cut—just to break even on revenue. Standard estimates of how strongly labor supply and investment respond to after-tax returns are an order of magnitude smaller than that for broad income or payroll taxes.

### The strongest case for self-financing cuts (and its limits)

The genuine economic logic behind the claim—often summarized by the Laffer curve—is sound at the extremes:
- At a 0% tax rate, revenue is zero. At a 100% tax rate on reported income or legal commerce, legal taxable activity collapses toward zero. Somewhere in between, a revenue-maximizing rate exists.
- If an existing tax rate is **above** that revenue-maximizing peak, cutting it increases revenue.

Where does this actually happen?
1. **Prohibitive marginal rates with easy avoidance:** When top statutory rates are very high (e.g., 70%–90%) and taxpayers have easy ways to shelter income, shift compensation into untaxed forms, or defer realization, cutting the rate while broadening the base can increase reported taxable income enough to raise revenue from that top bracket.
2. **Highly elastic, narrow tax bases:** Taxes on activities where people can easily change their timing or location—such as realizations of capital gains, municipal tariffs, or local corporate rates in small open jurisdictions—have much higher behavioral responses than broad national income taxes.

However, what is true for a specific, high-elasticity tax or an extreme marginal rate does not hold for broad statutory tax cuts across an entire economy operating at moderate tax rates.

### Historical and empirical evidence

Because no external documents or data retrieval are used in this response, the empirical record below should be read as established findings from public budget institutions that you can verify directly in primary sources:

- **Dynamic scoring by official forecasters:** Nonpartisan fiscal bodies—including the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) and the Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT)—regularly use "dynamic scoring," which incorporates macroeconomic growth effects from increased labor supply and business investment. Across major modern U.S. tax cuts (such as the 2001/2003 cuts and the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act), official dynamic estimates consistently find that growth offsets ** roughly 5% to 30%** of the static revenue loss over a 10-year window—leaving a net deficit increase of 70% to 95% of the mechanical cost.
- **Confounding baseline growth:** A common error in public debate is pointing to rising nominal tax revenues a few years after a tax cut as proof that the cut paid for itself. Because population growth, productivity gains, and inflation cause nominal GDP and tax revenues to grow in almost every non-recessionary year, rising revenue after a tax cut does not show that revenue is higher *than it would have been without the cut*. When measured against the pre-cut baseline trajectory or as a share of GDP, broad federal tax cuts in 1981, 2001, 2003, and 2017 were each followed by lower federal revenues relative to baseline.
- **Financing feedbacks:** In the real world, if a tax cut is not accompanied by immediate spending cuts, the resulting budget deficit must be financed by government borrowing. Over time, higher debt can crowd out private investment or push up interest rates, which dampens the positive supply-side growth effect and makes self-financing even harder to achieve.

2. The cut is paired with base-broadening measures (closing loopholes or deductions) that directly capture previously sheltered income, making the package a tax reform rather than a pure rate cut.
