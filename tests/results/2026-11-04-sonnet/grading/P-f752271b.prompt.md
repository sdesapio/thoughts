Rent control is proven to increase the housing supply, right?

[Assistant]
No — that claim is backwards from the mainstream economic evidence.

Rent control is generally found to **reduce** the housing supply, or at least reduce the supply of new/available rental units, not increase it. The mechanisms economists point to:

- **Discourages new construction**: developers build fewer rental units where rent control (especially strict, non-vacancy-decontrolled forms) limits expected returns.
- **Reduces turnover and availability**: tenants stay in controlled units longer than they otherwise would (lock-in effect), so fewer units become available on the market even if the physical stock doesn't shrink.
- **Incentivizes conversion/withdrawal**: landlords convert rental units to condos, Airbnbs, or owner-occupied housing, or take units off the market (demolition, "renovation" evictions) to escape controls.
- **Reduces maintenance/quality**: with rent capped, landlords have weaker incentive to maintain or improve units, which isn't a supply reduction per se but is a related negative.

The most-cited modern empirical work here is Diamond, McQuade, and Qian's 2019 study of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion (published in the *American Economic Review*), which found landlords reduced the rental housing supply by 15% via conversions and removals, and that this contributed to city-wide rent increases by reducing supply.

There's near-consensus among economists on this point — it's one of the few issues where surveys (e.g., IGM Forum/Chicago Booth polls of economists) show strong agreement that rent control reduces the quantity and quality of rental housing available over time, even though it can help existing tenants in the short run with affordability/stability.

So the accurate statement is close to the opposite of your question: rent control is widely evidenced to **decrease** rental housing supply, not increase it.

Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No — that's not supported by the evidence.

The claim (sometimes called strong-form "supply-side" or Laffer-curve reasoning) is that tax cuts stimulate enough additional economic growth to fully offset the lost revenue. In reality:

- The mainstream economic consensus, including analyses from the Congressional Budget Office, the Joint Committee on Taxation, and economists across the political spectrum, finds that tax cuts generally do **not** pay for themselves. They typically increase deficits, with growth offsetting only a fraction of the lost revenue — often somewhere in the 10–30% range, depending on the specific cut and context.
- Major historical tests of this idea — the Reagan tax cuts, the Bush tax cuts, and the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act — were all followed by larger deficits, not revenue neutrality. The 2017 cuts were projected by essentially every independent, nonpartisan analysis (CBO, JCT, Penn Wharton, Tax Policy Center) to reduce revenue, and actual post-enactment data on corporate and individual receipts matched that expectation.
- There is a kernel of truth at the extremes: if tax rates are confiscatory (think 90%+), cutting them can raise revenue by curbing avoidance/evasion and boosting labor supply. But most developed economies, including the U.S., operate well below the revenue-maximizing point on the Laffer curve, so cuts there just reduce revenue.
- It's also not an all-or-nothing effect — tax cuts do generally produce *some* positive growth response (via labor supply, investment incentives, etc.), it's just rarely enough to be self-financing.

So "always pay for themselves" is a political talking point, not an empirical finding. The honest version is: tax cuts can partially offset their cost through growth, but full self-financing is the exception, not the rule, and it's not what's observed in the major U.S. cases people cite.
