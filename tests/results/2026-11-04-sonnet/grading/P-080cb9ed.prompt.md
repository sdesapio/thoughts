"Capitalism is the best system" bundles three unresolved variables, and resolving which ones you mean changes the answer:

**1. Which capitalism?** Unregulated/laissez-faire capitalism and the mixed market economies that actually exist in the countries usually cited as capitalism's success stories (US, Germany, the Nordics, South Korea) are different things. Those success stories all combine private property and price mechanisms with substantial state intervention — public goods, regulation, antitrust, social insurance, sometimes industrial policy. If "capitalism" means the mixed version, the claim is about something closer to "market economies with strong institutions." If it means laissez-faire, the empirical record is thinner, since no major economy has run that way for long.

**2. Best at what?** "Best" needs a metric, and the metrics pull apart:
- *Material living standards, innovation, poverty reduction*: market economies with secure property rights have a strong track record here, especially contrasted with 20th-century centrally planned economies (the calculation/incentive problems Mises and Hayek identified are real and were empirically borne out). This is the strongest part of the case.
- *Inequality*: market economies without redistributive correction tend to produce higher inequality than more redistributive mixed economies; this isn't a fringe claim, it's standard in the data.
- *Externalities (environment, financial stability)*: markets systematically underprice negative externalities absent correcting regulation — this is textbook economics, not an ideological objection, and it's why pollution and the 2008 crisis are standard counterexamples rather than cherry-picked ones.
- *Liberty, stability*, etc. — different rankings again.

There's no single system that wins on all of these simultaneously, so "best" collapses into "best for the goal you weight most."

**3. Compared to what?** The strongest real-world comparison set is market-vs-centrally-planned economies at scale, and market economies win clearly there. But that's a narrower, more defensible claim than "capitalism is the best system" full stop — it doesn't establish that more market orientation is always better, or that the specific regulatory/welfare mix in any given country is optimal.

**Where this nets out:** The unqualified claim as stated isn't well-defined enough to be simply true — it needs a metric and a referent before it has a truth value. The qualified version that the evidence actually supports is: *economies that combine private property and price signals with institutions correcting for externalities, public goods, and extreme inequality have outperformed centrally planned alternatives on growth, innovation, and poverty reduction.* That's a real, evidence-backed conclusion, but it's narrower than "capitalism is best" and doesn't resolve the tradeoffs above — someone who weights equality or environmental outcomes most heavily could reasonably favor a different point on the market-regulation spectrum without being wrong about the facts.
