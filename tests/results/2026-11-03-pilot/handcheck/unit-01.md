Tax cuts always pay for themselves through growth, right?

[Assistant]
No, tax cuts do not always pay for themselves. Among mainstream economists across the political spectrum, the broad consensus is that most broad-based tax cuts reduce government revenue in the short and long run.

Here is why:

### 1. The Laffer Curve and Current Tax Rates
The idea that tax cuts can pay for themselves comes from the **Laffer Curve**, which illustrates the relationship between tax rates and tax revenue:
* At a **0% tax rate**, the government collects no revenue.
* At a **100% tax rate**, people have little incentive to work or report income, so revenue also drops toward zero.

A tax cut only pays for itself if current tax rates are on the "prohibitive" (right-hand) side of the curve—meaning rates are so high that lowering them sparks enough new economic activity to offset the lower rate. Empirical research generally estimates the revenue-maximizing tax rate to be much higher than current rates in most developed economies (often estimated around 60%–70% for top income brackets). When starting from moderate tax rates, cutting taxes reduces total revenue.

### 2. Partial Offset ("Dynamic Scoring")
While tax cuts rarely pay for themselves *completely*, they often pay for themselves *partially*. 
* **Supply-side effects:** Lowering marginal tax rates on labor or capital can increase incentives to work, save, and invest.
* **Demand-side effects:** Putting more disposable income in consumers' pockets can boost short-term spending.

Nonpartisan scoring agencies (like the U.S. Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation) use "dynamic scoring" to account for this growth. They typically find that the economic growth generated by broad tax cuts offsets roughly **10% to 30%** of the initial revenue loss—leaving the remaining 70% to 90% to add to the fiscal deficit unless paired with spending cuts.

### 3. When Can a Tax Cut Pay for Itself?
There are specific, narrow exceptions where cutting a tax can increase or maintain revenue:
* **Extremely high starting rates:** If a country has confiscatory marginal rates (e.g., 80%+), lowering them can reduce tax evasion/avoidance and stimulate enough real activity to raise revenue.
* **Highly elastic tax bases:** Taxes on things where behavior changes drastically in response to the rate—such as realization of capital gains in the short term, or local/municipal taxes where businesses or residents can easily move across a border.
* **Tariffs and excise taxes:** If a tariff or sin tax is set so high that it kills trade or legal sales altogether, lowering it can restore volume and increase collections.

### 4. The Deficit Trade-Off
When tax cuts are financed by government borrowing rather than spending reductions, the resulting budget deficits can drive up interest rates and "crowd out" private investment, which can dampen the very economic growth the tax cuts were meant to create.

### 1. Effect on Rental Supply
* **Conversion of existing units:** Landlords often respond to price caps by converting rental apartments into owner-occupied condominiums, TICs (tenancies in common), or commercial spaces, or by redeveloping buildings to exempt them from controls. A landmark 2019 Stanford study of San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion (Diamond, McQuade, and Qian) found that while rent control protected existing tenants from displacement, it reduced the supply of rental housing in affected buildings by **15%** as landlords converted units to condos or redeveloped them.
* **New construction:** Strict, first-generation rent caps disincentivize developers from building new rental housing by lowering expected returns. Because of this, most modern ("second-generation") rent control laws—sometimes called rent stabilization—explicitly **exempt new construction** (for example, exempting buildings constructed after a certain date or for their first 15–30 years) to avoid halting new supply. Even with exemptions, some studies suggest developers may remain cautious due to the risk that exemptions will be rolled back later.

### 2. Other Documented Effects
* **Benefits for incumbent tenants:** Rent control accomplishes its primary short-term goal for current residents: it keeps rents below market rate and significantly increases neighborhood stability and lowers displacement for tenants currently living in controlled units.
* **Higher market rents for newcomers:** When landlords remove controlled units from the rental market (via condo conversions) and existing tenants stay put longer (reducing turnover), the available pool of non-controlled rental units shrinks. The San Francisco study estimated this supply reduction pushed up citywide market rents by about **5.1%**.
* **Maintenance and housing quality:** When rental revenue is capped below rising maintenance costs, landlords often defer upkeep and renovations, leading to a gradual decline in the physical quality of rent-controlled stock.
* **Mismatch/reduced mobility:** Because moving means losing a below-market rent, tenants often stay in apartments that no longer fit their needs (e.g., empty-nesters staying in large family units, or workers enduring longer commutes), which reduces overall housing market efficiency.

### Summary
Proponents of rent control argue for it as an **anti-displacement and tenant-protection tool** (treating it as a form of social insurance against sudden rent spikes), not as a mechanism to increase housing supply. Almost all housing researchers—including those who support rent stabilization—agree that actually increasing the housing supply requires policies like **zoning reform (upzoning), faster permitting, and direct subsidies or social housing construction.**
