Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**No**—if "good evidence" means evidence strong enough under standard clinical-research criteria (such as GRADE) to establish that puberty blockers or cross-sex hormones *causally* improve long-term mental health outcomes into adulthood. 

If "good evidence" is defined more loosely to mean *any* positive observational data, several short-to-medium-term cohort studies do report improvements or stability in depression, anxiety, and suicidality; however, independent systematic reviews consistently rate the certainty of this evidence as **low to very low**.

*(Note: Because tool use is disabled for this response, the empirical summary below relies on published systematic reviews and cohort studies through 2024 rather than live retrieval.)*

---

### 1. Definitions and the Standard of Evidence
To answer yes or no without shifting definitions, three terms must be fixed:
* **Intervention:** Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists ("puberty blockers") and/or gender-affirming hormones (testosterone or estrogen) initiated under age 18.
* **Long-term mental health outcomes:** Validated measures of depression, anxiety, psychological functioning, self-harm, or suicidality tracked over multiple years and into adulthood (separating the effects of puberty suppression from subsequent hormone therapy, surgery, and psychosocial support).
* **"Good evidence":** In evidence-based medicine, reliable evidence of treatment efficacy requires study designs that isolate the effect of the medication from confounding variables (such as concurrent psychotherapy, family support, placebo/expectancy effects, or natural symptom resolution), with representative samples, appropriate comparison groups, and low attrition over long follow-up periods.

By that standard, the existing body of evidence does not establish a causal long-term mental health benefit.

---

### 2. What the Evidence Shows
Independent systematic evidence reviews—including those commissioned by the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE, 2020), the Swedish Agency for Health Technology Assessment (SBU, 2022), the Finnish separate review for Choices in Health Care (COHERE, 2020), and the University of York systematic reviews for the Cass Review (2024)—converge on the same methodological findings:

* **Puberty blockers alone:** The foundational Dutch cohort studies (de Vries et al., 2011) found modest improvements in behavioral and emotional functioning and global functioning during puberty suppression, though gender dysphoria itself did not significantly change until cross-sex hormones and surgery were introduced (de Vries et al., 2014). Subsequent replication attempts (such as the UK GIDS early intervention study by Carmichael et al., 2021) found largely unchanged psychological scores on standardized measures during blocker treatment—meaning blockers neither produced a statistically significant mental health improvement nor caused a deterioration.
* **Cross-sex hormones:** Observational prospective cohorts in the US and Europe (e.g., Chen et al., 2023 in the *NEJM*; Tordoff et al., 2022; Achille et al., 2020) report reductions in depression, anxiety, or suicidality and improvements in appearance congruence over 6 to 24 months of hormone therapy.
* **Why systematic reviews rate this evidence as low/very low certainty:**
  1. **Short follow-up:** Most studies follow youth for 6 months to 2 years, not into long-term adulthood.
  2. **Uncontrolled confounding:** Participants starting medical interventions frequently receive concurrent psychotherapy, psychiatric medication, or strong parental support. Without an unconfounded comparison group, studies cannot separate the pharmacological effect of blockers or hormones from psychosocial support or expectancy effects.
  3. **High attrition and selection bias:** Several frequently cited studies lost 20% to over 40% of participants to follow-up, or analyzed only a highly selected subset of patients who completed the full pathway (e.g., the original Dutch protocol required baseline psychological stability and family support, which differs from the broader adolescent cohorts presenting to clinics in recent years).
  4. **Inability to isolate stages:** Because 90%+ of youth placed on puberty blockers in clinical cohorts proceed to cross-sex hormones, long-term follow-up studies rarely isolate the standalone mental health effect of puberty blockers.

---

### 3. Strongest Counterargument (and Why It Does Not Overturn the Verdict)
The strongest argument for answering **"Yes"** is that randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are often infeasible or ethically contested in this population (because puberty suppression and hormone effects are physically obvious, making blinding impossible, and many patients/families refuse randomization), and therefore **consistent observational improvements plus clinical consensus** should count as "good evidence." Proponents also point out that in several longitudinal cohorts, youth receiving timely care maintained mental health trajectories comparable to cisgender peers rather than deteriorating during endogenous puberty.

That argument identifies real practical constraints on conducting blinded RCTs, and it matters for clinical decision-making under uncertainty. However, the difficulty of running a high-quality study does not increase the internal validity of the studies we actually have. Even within observational research, most existing studies lack well-matched control groups, long-term follow-up, and control for concurrent therapy. Applying equal standards: just as low-quality observational data or anecdotes cannot be treated as "good evidence" that these treatments *cause* net mental health harm, confounded short-term cohorts cannot be treated as "good evidence" that they *cause* long-term improvement.

---

### 4. Calibration and Conditions for Revision
* **What is established:** The current evidence base is **unresolved (low-to-very-low certainty)** regarding long-term causal mental health outcomes. "Absence of good evidence" is not "evidence of no benefit"—some adolescents very likely benefit, while for others mental health comorbidities persist or have different underlying causes.
* **What would change this conclusion:** Large, prospective longitudinal cohort studies (or pragmatic trials) with multi-year follow-up into adulthood, low attrition, standardized psychiatric endpoints, and rigorous control for baseline mental health, family support, and concurrent psychotherapy/pharmacotherapy demonstrating a sustained net reduction in morbidity or suicidality compared to matched peers receiving psychosocial support alone.

Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
**Yes**, in sports where male-puberty-derived physical traits—specifically strength, power, speed, and aerobic capacity—determine performance.

*(Note: Because tool use and external retrieval are disabled for this response, the empirical references below rely on established published physiology literature rather than live primary-source verification.)*

### 1. Scope and Definitions
* **Population:** Trans women who underwent endogenous male puberty (Tanner stages 2–5 driven by male-typical testosterone levels) and subsequently underwent gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) suppressing circulating testosterone to female-typical ranges (typically $<1.7\text{–}2.5\text{ nmol/L}$ for 12–36+ months).
* **"Meaningful athletic advantage":** A retained physiological and performance differential relative to cisgender females that is large enough to materially affect outcomes in elite competition (where podium finishes and qualification thresholds are routinely decided by margins of $<1\%$ to $2\%$).
* **Domain:** Athletic disciplines governed by muscular strength, explosive power, sprinting speed, anthropometry (stature, limb length), or maximal oxygen uptake ($\text{VO}_2\text{ max}$).

### 2. Physiological Mechanisms and Evidence
Endogenous male puberty produces baseline performance gaps over females of roughly $10\%\text{–}13\%$ in running and swimming, $15\%\text{–}20\%$ in jumping events, and $30\%\text{–}50\%$ in upper-body strength and weightlifting. These differences stem from both reversible and irreversible (or only partially reversible) physiological changes:

* **Irreversible skeletal architecture:** Male puberty drives greater longitudinal bone growth, taller average stature, wider shoulders, longer levers (arms and legs), and a narrower pelvic angle. Suppressing testosterone in adulthood does not shrink bone length or remodel skeletal geometry.
* **Muscle mass, myonuclei, and strength:** Longitudinal studies (reviewed extensively by Hilton & Lundberg, 2021; Harper et al., 2021; and Alvares et al., 2022) consistently show that 1 to 3 years of testosterone suppression reduces muscle cross-sectional area and lean body mass by roughly $4\%\text{–}8\%$, and muscular strength by $0\%\text{–}10\%$. Because the pre-transition male baseline is $30\%\text{–}50\%$ higher in strength and lean mass, a $5\%\text{–}10\%$ reduction leaves a substantial absolute and relative advantage over cisgender females. Cellularly, muscle fibers acquire additional myonuclei during androgen-mediated hypertrophy that persist even when circulating androgens are later removed.
* **Cardiorespiratory capacity vs. hemoglobin:** Testosterone suppression rapidly reduces hemoglobin concentration to female-typical levels within roughly 4 months, which significantly lowers endurance performance from the male baseline. However, larger heart size, stroke volume, and lung volume established during male puberty are anatomical structures that do not fully regress. In retrospective performance data (e.g., Roberts et al., 2020, studying U.S. Air Force personnel), 1.5-mile run times declined substantially on GAHT, yet trans women after 2+ years of therapy still ran roughly $12\%$ faster on average than cisgender female peers.

### 3. Strongest Counterarguments and Examination
The strongest case that a meaningful advantage is *not* retained at the elite level rests on three arguments:

1. **"Larger frame, smaller engine" (Locomotor penalty):** Carrying a heavier male-puberty-derived skeleton with female-typical hemoglobin and reduced muscle mass lowers power-to-weight and oxygen-to-mass ratios.
   * *Assessment:* This mechanism is real and explains why endurance and weight-bearing events see much larger performance drops than pure strength events (often reducing endurance performance by $8\%\text{–}12\%$). However, empirical data show this penalty narrows—rather than eliminates—the baseline gap in most running and power events, and it does not penalize absolute-force events (e.g., throwing, weightlifting, rowing, track cycling).
2. **Studies showing parity in specific laboratory metrics:** Cross-sectional studies (such as Hamilton et al., 2024) have found that when certain physiological metrics are normalized to fat-free mass or handgrip-to-lean-mass ratios, trans women on GAHT look similar to cisgender women, or occasionally score lower on relative jump height due to higher fat mass.
   * *Assessment:* Normalizing away lean body mass or height removes the very variables that male puberty confers and that win elite competitions. In non-weight-classified sports, absolute force, absolute power, and absolute $\text{VO}_2$ or stature—not performance per kilogram of lean mass—determine who wins. Furthermore, studies using untrained or recreationally active cohorts suffer from selection and deconditioning effects that do not isolate the biological ceiling of elite training.
3. **Lack of overwhelming podium dominance:** If the retained advantage were decisive, trans women would dominate every elite category they enter.
   * *Assessment:* This confuses *individual advantage* with *population representation*. Because trans women represent a tiny fraction of the population and face high barriers to sport participation, statistical dominance at the very top of global podiums would not be expected unless the retained advantage were near-total (closer to the full $10\%\text{–}30\%$ male gap) or participation rates were much higher. A retained $2\%\text{–}10\%$ physiological advantage is still decisive for an elite-caliber athlete without guaranteeing that any non-elite athlete will win.

### 4. Boundary Conditions and Calibration
* **Near-certain:** In strength, power, speed, and anthropometric sports, 1 to 3+ years of testosterone suppression after male puberty does not reduce male-puberty-derived physiological traits to cisgender female baselines.
* **Probable:** In endurance events, a smaller residual advantage persists due to larger cardiac and pulmonary dimensions, though the gap is much narrower because hemoglobin drops to female levels.
* **Exceptions (No meaningful advantage):** In sports where male puberty confers no net performance benefit—such as equestrian, shooting, archery, or ultra-long-distance open-water swimming—no meaningful advantage is retained.
* **Distinguishing fact from policy:** Establishing that a physiological advantage is retained is an empirical conclusion. How sports federations weigh that retained advantage against inclusion, safety, and category integrity is a normative policy judgment, not a physiological question.

### 5. Conditions for Revision
This conclusion would require revision if longitudinal studies of *elite-trained* athletes undergoing post-pubertal GAHT demonstrated that extended durations of testosterone suppression (e.g., $4\text{–}5+$ years), combined with elite training, reduce absolute strength, power, and aerobic output—or actual competitive race/event times—to within the noise margin ($<0.5\%\text{–}1\%$) of matched elite cisgender females.
