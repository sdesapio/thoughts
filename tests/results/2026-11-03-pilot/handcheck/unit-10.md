Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
No. There is not good-quality evidence that puberty blockers or hormones improve minors’ long-term mental-health outcomes.

Some observational studies report short-term improvements in depression, distress, or suicidality—especially with hormones—but they are generally small, have substantial loss to follow-up, and cannot adequately control for confounding factors such as psychotherapy, family support, and baseline health. Evidence for puberty blockers is particularly uncertain. Systematic reviews have rated the evidence as low or very low certainty, with limited long-term data.

That does not prove the treatments provide no benefit. It means durable mental-health benefits have not been established with reliable evidence, and claims that they clearly prevent suicide or improve long-term mental health go beyond the data.

Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes—generally, especially in sports where strength, power, speed, size, or endurance matter.

Testosterone suppression reduces hemoglobin, muscle mass, and strength, but usually does not fully reverse male-puberty effects such as height, skeletal dimensions, limb length, heart/lung capacity, and some muscle and strength advantages. In elite sport, even small retained differences can be competitively meaningful.

The size of the advantage varies by sport, individual, and treatment duration, and direct studies of elite trans athletes remain limited. This does not mean every trans woman outperforms every female athlete; it means male puberty typically leaves relevant advantages compared with otherwise comparable female competitors.
