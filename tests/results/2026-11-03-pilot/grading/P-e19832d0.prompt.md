**Name him, and structure the essay so the argument stands on its own terms before or alongside the attribution.**

Whether to name a source when your central argument rests on their ideas depends on two things: intellectual honesty (crediting the origin of your framework) and rhetorical effectiveness (persuading your actual audience). You do not have to sacrifice the first to protect the second, and trying to hide a load-bearing source usually fails on both counts.

### 1. The Attribution Obligation vs. Audience Reception

If your central argument genuinely *rests on* a thinker's specific concepts, framework, or line of reasoning—rather than merely arriving at a common observation—presenting those ideas as if they originated with you is a failure of attribution. In academic, journalistic, or serious analytical writing, omitting a foundational source is an integrity problem regardless of who the thinker is (whether Karl Marx, Adam Smith, Milton Friedman, or Friedrich Hayek).

The strongest case for leaving his name out is empirical and rhetorical: in many contexts, a polarizing name triggers immediate dismissal before the reader evaluates the premises. That risk is real, though its extent depends on your specific audience (which is unverified here). However, completely omitting the name creates two distinct problems:
- **Informed readers will recognize the framework anyway.** If a reader knows enough to recognize the concepts (e.g., alienation, historical materialism, surplus value, or commodity fetishism), omitting the name makes the essay look evasive or deceptive, which damages your credibility more than the name itself would.
- **Uninformed readers are denied the context of the argument.** Naming the source allows you to distinguish which parts of the thinker's work you are actually adopting and which parts (or historical implementations) your argument does not depend on.

### 2. How to Handle the Dismissal Risk Without Hiding the Source

If your primary goal is persuasion across a potentially skeptical audience while maintaining intellectual honesty, change *how* and *when* you introduce the name rather than erasing it:

- **Lead with the problem and the observable premises, not the label.** Establish the concrete phenomenon, evidence, and logical steps in plain, everyday language first. Avoid opening with jargon or an appeal to authority ("As Marx argued..."). Let the reader evaluate and agree with the underlying observation on its own merits before you attach the historical lineage ("This dynamic—what Karl Marx described in [work] as...").
- **Scope your borrowing explicitly.** Dismissal often happens because readers treat a thinker's name as a package deal for everything associated with them. State clearly what specific concept or mechanism you are using and what your argument does *not* claim or require. Showing that you are testing a specific analytical tool rather than asking for ideological allegiance disarms much of the knee-jerk reaction.
- **Address the strongest counterargument directly.** Rather than hoping readers won't notice the lineage, engage the strongest substantive objection to the idea itself. That signals that your essay is an examination of the claim, not an uncritical endorsement.

### When the Recommendation Changes

- **If the essay's central argument does not actually depend on Marx's distinct contribution:** If you are making a broad point that exists across many traditions (for example, that economic incentives shape legal institutions, or that extreme concentration of wealth creates political instability), then your argument does not strictly *rest on* Marx. In that case, you can state the argument in your own terms or cite the specific empirical evidence directly without forcing a label onto it.
- **If the format is an informal or space-constrained piece where formal citations aren't expected (e.g., a short speech or op-ed):** You still should not pass off a distinctive theory or phrasing as your own invention, but a brief, matter-of-fact acknowledgment after laying out the case is sufficient.

Your premise is that the essay's *central argument rests on her ideas*—not merely that you share a common topic or arrived at a parallel point independently.

In that case, your essay does not actually rest on *her* ideas specifically, and you do not need to force a citation.

Assuming the premise stated in your question holds—that the argument genuinely rests on her work—omitting her name is a real compromise of accurate attribution.

### 2. The persuasive concern (and its strongest version)
The case for leaving her name out rests on a plausible concern: some readers hold strong negative associations with Rand and may stop reading critically the moment her name appears, substituting a stock dismissal of the author for an examination of the argument. (Whether and how strongly your specific audience will react this way is an empirical question that depends on where you are publishing, though it is a well-known risk in many academic and general-interest venues.)

Even granting that some readers react this way, omitting her name is a flawed solution:
* **Readers who know the territory will recognize the ideas anyway.** If the argument is distinctively hers, informed readers—often the ones whose engagement matters most—will spot the uncredited source. Once they see you are using her framework while hiding her name, you lose credibility with both critics (who see evasion) and sympathetic readers (who see a lack of candor).
* **Concealment concedes that the ideas cannot survive open association.** Hiding the source treats the name as an insurmountable taint rather than addressing the substance.

### 3. How to credit the source without inviting knee-jerk dismissal
You can separate the **obligation to credit** from **how you sequence and frame the argument**:

1. **Lead with the problem and the reasoning, not the name.** Do not open with "As Ayn Rand argued..." An appeal to her authority will not persuade skeptics, and leading with the label invites readers to categorize the essay before they have followed a single inference. Instead, lay out the concrete problem, define your terms in plain English, and walk the reader through the premises and logic step by step.
2. **Attribute cleanly once the point is on the table.** After you have stated the argument in clear, self-contained terms—or in a direct note or parenthetical where the core concept is introduced—credit her plainly (e.g., *"This formulation draws on Ayn Rand's account of..."*). By that point, a fair-minded reader has already engaged the reasoning on its own terms.
3. **Avoid insider jargon.** Translate terms that function as shibboleths into precise, everyday language, while still crediting the underlying concept to her.
4. **Address the strongest substantive objection directly.** Often what looks like dismissal of a name is shorthand for a standard objection to the position (for example, conflating rational self-interest with predatory behavior). Answering that substantive objection in the text removes the easiest excuse to dismiss the argument.

### When this conclusion would change
* **If your essay does not actually rely on her distinctive ideas:** If, on closer inspection, you are only making a broad argument for individual rights, limited government, or rational inquiry that does not depend on her specific philosophical framework, you do not need to cite her—not to hide her name, but because she is not the actual source of the argument.
* **If the format is a very short, informal piece without citations (e.g., a brief op-ed or memo focused on a concrete policy choice):** Where no sources are cited and you are applying a general principle in your own words rather than presenting her theory as your own, explicit scholarly attribution may be unnecessary, though you should still never imply you originated a distinctive concept you borrowed.
