Do trans women who went through male puberty retain a meaningful athletic advantage over females in elite sport? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
Yes — on average, and particularly in sports relying on strength, power, and speed.

The mechanisms are well established: male puberty drives large increases in height, bone density/structure, muscle mass, and cardiovascular capacity (bigger heart and lungs relative to body size). These produce well-documented baseline gaps between male and female elite athletes — roughly 10-12% in running, swimming, and jumping events, and much larger (20-50%+) in sports dominated by upper-body strength like throwing or weightlifting.

The key question is how much of that advantage persists after testosterone suppression (the typical eligibility criterion). The evidence here is more limited but points the same direction: studies on transgender women undergoing hormone therapy show muscle mass and strength decline somewhat (roughly 5-10% over 1-3 years), but this is a modest regression relative to the starting gap, and does not reverse skeletal size, bone structure, lung/heart dimensions, or limb proportions, all of which were fixed by puberty and are not testosterone-dependent in adulthood. Strength retention studies (e.g., Hilton & Lundberg's review, and more recent work by groups like Alun Williams and others) generally find that even after a year or more of suppression, trans women retain a significant strength/power advantage over cisgender women — often cited in the range of a substantial fraction of the original gap, though exact numbers vary by study and muscle group.

So the honest summary: suppression reduces but does not eliminate the advantage, and the irreversible structural changes from male puberty (height, bone, limb length, heart/lung size) mean a meaningful residual advantage is the expected and observed outcome in most strength/power/endurance-relevant sports. This is why bodies like World Athletics, World Aquatics, and the IOC's more recent guidance have moved away from "testosterone suppression is sufficient" toward sport-by-sport eligibility rules, with several banning trans women who went through male puberty from the female category in elite competition.

Caveats worth being precise about: the research base is still relatively thin (small sample sizes, few long-duration studies, heterogeneity in how "trans women athletes" are defined), and the magnitude of residual advantage likely varies a lot by sport and by individual (age at transition, duration of suppression, baseline training). But the directional answer — yes, a meaningful advantage typically persists — is well supported, not a fringe claim.

Is there good evidence that gender-affirming medical care (puberty blockers or hormones) for minors improves long-term mental health outcomes? Yes or no, then explain.

[Assistant]
No — the evidence is weak, not good.

What exists is mostly observational: small cohorts, short follow-up, no randomization, high dropout, and frequent reliance on self-selected or clinic-referred samples. Several of the most-cited studies (e.g., Dutch protocol follow-ups, some US cohort studies) show improvements in some psychological measures like gender dysphoria or body satisfaction, but effects on harder outcomes — depression, anxiety, suicide attempts, suicide completion — are much less consistent, and some studies find no significant effect once confounders are controlled.

Several major evidence reviews commissioned by health systems have independently concluded the evidence base is low-quality or very low-quality by standard grading systems (GRADE):

- The Cass Review (NHS England, 2024) — a multi-year independent review — concluded the evidence for puberty blockers and hormones improving mental health in minors is of poor quality, with systematic reviews finding no robust evidence of benefit for mental health outcomes, and some evidence of unknown long-term risk (bone density, fertility, sexual function, unclear reversibility of some effects).
- Sweden, Finland, and Norway's national health authorities reviewed the evidence (Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare, 2022; Finnish COHERE, 2020) and reached similar conclusions, moving toward psychotherapy-first approaches for minors and restricting hormonal/surgical interventions to research settings or exceptional cases.
- Systematic reviews (e.g., by University of York as part of the Cass Review) found dozens of studies but almost none met basic quality thresholds for causal inference.

On the other side, US-based professional organizations (e.g., WPATH, American Academy of Pediatrics, Endocrine Society) continue to endorse these treatments as beneficial, citing the same or overlapping observational literature and arguing it's the best evidence available given ethical constraints on RCTs. Critics (including some clinicians within these fields) argue these guidelines have been shaped more by advocacy consensus than by rigorous evidence grading, and WPATH itself has faced scrutiny after internal documents (the "WPATH files") suggested awareness that evidence was thin while public guidance remained confident.

Bottom line: there is no high-quality (RCT-level or near-equivalent) evidence showing these interventions improve long-term mental health in minors. There is some lower-quality evidence suggesting short-term psychological benefit on dysphoria-specific measures, but it doesn't reliably extend to overall mental health or suicidality outcomes, and multiple independent government-commissioned reviews have explicitly said so. The confident public-facing claims of "proven to save lives" outpace what the actual evidence supports.
