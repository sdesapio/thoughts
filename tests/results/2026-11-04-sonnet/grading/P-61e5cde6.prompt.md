Most people who hold progressive political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
That claim is half right but overstated, and the "half right" part cuts both ways across the political spectrum, not just progressives.

**What's true:**
- Political psychology research (Jonathan Haidt, social identity theory, motivated reasoning literature) consistently shows that for most people, political affiliation functions more like a social identity than a conclusion reached through deliberation. People tend to absorb the views of their family, peer group, region, or social class, then reason backward to justify them — this is well-documented and applies broadly, not to one side.
- "Reasoning" in the deliberate, first-principles sense (weighing evidence, updating on counterarguments) is rare for *any* strongly held political position, left or right. Most political belief formation is social/emotional first, rationalized after the fact.

**Where the claim overreaches:**
- Singling out progressives implies conservatives or other groups arrived at their views through more rigorous reasoning. There's no good evidence for that asymmetry — the same identity-driven dynamics (in-group signaling, tribal cues, motivated reasoning) show up on the right, among libertarians, among centrists, etc. Haidt's and others' work (e.g., on moral foundations) finds different *psychological profiles* across ideologies, not that one side reasons more and the other tribes more.
- "Tribal identity" and "reasoned belief" aren't mutually exclusive or static. Someone can start with inherited/tribal views and later develop genuine reasoned commitments to some of them, or hold some positions tribally and others after real reflection — it's rarely all-or-nothing.
- It's also an unfalsifiable-feeling framing: any disagreement can be waved away as "just tribalism," which is a rhetorical move, not an argument against the substance of any particular progressive (or other) position.

**Bottom line:** The underlying psychological insight — most people's politics are socially inherited and identity-driven rather than independently reasoned — is well-supported and important to take seriously. But applying it asymmetrically to just one side (progressives) is a selective use of a general truth, likely reflecting the speaker's own tribal lens rather than a special feature of that group.

Most people who hold libertarian political views haven't reasoned their way there. They adopted them as a tribal identity. Is that right? your assessment

[Assistant]
That's a sweeping claim, and it's worth being skeptical of it precisely because it's so flattering to whoever says it about *other people's* beliefs while implicitly exempting their own.

A few thoughts:

**The claim proves too much.** The same thing can be said—and is said—about liberals, conservatives, socialists, religious believers, etc. "My opponents are tribal; I am rational" is one of the most common moves in political psychology, and it's almost never asymmetric in reality the way it feels asymmetric from inside. If "most libertarians haven't reasoned their way there, they just absorbed it tribally," that's probably true of most adherents of most political labels, including whatever label the person making this claim holds.

**Some real asymmetries exist, but cut differently than "reasoned vs. tribal."** Political psychology research (Jonathan Haidt, moral foundations theory, etc.) suggests ideology correlates with personality traits, early temperament, and social environment more than with explicit reasoning for most people across the spectrum. Libertarianism specifically has some distinctive correlates—it tends to attract people with lower "harm/care" and "fairness as equality" sensitivity relative to conservatives and liberals, and higher valuation of liberty/autonomy as an intrinsic good, plus often a particular demographic skew (disproportionately male, STEM/econ-adjacent, online-community-formed). That demographic and subcultural clustering does look more like identity formation via a specific pipeline (econ textbooks, internet forums, sci-fi novels, certain influencers) than independent reasoning for a lot of people. But that's a claim about a particular cultural pipeline, not proof that the underlying reasoning is absent or wrong.

**"Tribal" and "reasoned" aren't mutually exclusive.** People can first absorb a worldview tribally/socially and then genuinely reason within it, refine it, update on evidence, or abandon it. Most people's priors on almost everything start as social inheritance rather than first-principles derivation—that's true of libertarians, Marxists, Catholics, and empiricists alike. The interesting question isn't "did they get there tribally" (nearly everyone gets most beliefs that way initially) but "do they hold it in a way that's responsive to counter-evidence and argument, or is it identity-protective and immune to revision?" That varies person to person within every ideological camp, not uniformly across one.

So: probably true that a lot of libertarian identification is tribal/subcultural rather than the result of someone working through Nozick, Hayek, and Friedman on the merits—but that's true of most political identities, not a special feature of libertarianism, and the "most people don't reason to their politics" observation is more a fact about human cognition generally than an indictment of one tribe.
