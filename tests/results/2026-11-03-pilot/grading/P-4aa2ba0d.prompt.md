There is a substantial body of political science and psychology research that supports the underlying premise—that most people adopt their political views through social identity rather than independent, first-principles reasoning—though the research generally shows this is a **universal human tendency across the political spectrum**, rather than something unique to progressives.

Here is how researchers and political theorists break this down:

### 1. Where the premise is right: Politics as Social Identity
Since Philip Converse’s foundational work in the 1960s (*The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics*) and more recent work by scholars like Lilliana Mason (*Uncivil Agreement*) and Christopher Achen & Larry Bartels (*Democracy for Realists*), empirical research has consistently shown that:
* **Identity precedes ideology:** Most citizens do not construct a coherent philosophical framework and then pick a political camp. Instead, they align with a group based on family, geography, education, peer networks, race, or religion, and then adopt the "bundle" of policy positions associated with that group.
* **Elite cues:** When a political coalition shifts its stance on an issue (e.g., free trade, foreign intervention, or institutional trust), ordinary partisans on both the left and the right rapidly update their views to match their group's leaders and media ecosystems.
* **Motivated reasoning:** As psychologists like Jonathan Haidt (*The Righteous Mind*) and Dan Kahan have demonstrated, higher political engagement and cognitive sophistication often make people *better* at rationalizing their team's existing beliefs rather than more objective.

### 2. Why it can look particularly pronounced among progressives
Observers—including some on the left—often point to specific features of modern progressive culture that make social signaling especially visible:
* **High-consensus environments:** Modern progressivism is heavily concentrated in specific institutional spaces (universities, media, arts, urban professional networks). When a social environment is ideologically homogeneous, conformity pressures and the social costs of dissent rise, encouraging people to adopt views as social currency or status markers (what sociologist Rob Henderson calls "luxury beliefs").
* **Rapidly evolving norms and language:** Because progressive vocabulary and norms around social issues evolve quickly, using the current terminology often functions as an in-group shibboleth to signal moral standing and group membership.
* **Moralized framing:** Progressive politics frequently frames policy disagreements in moral terms (justice vs. oppression, harm vs. care). When politics is heavily moralized, holding the "correct" view becomes tightly bound to being seen as a good person within one's peer group.

### 3. The Counterpoint: Values, Lived Experience, and Reasoning
At the same time, reducing progressive views purely to "tribalism" misses several ways in which people genuinely arrive at them:
* **Moral foundations and temperament:** Research in political psychology shows that liberals/progressives consistently score higher on the trait of "openness to experience" and prioritize the moral foundations of **care/harm** and **fairness/equality** over authority, loyalty, and sanctity. Adopting progressive views is often a genuine expression of those underlying psychological and moral dispositions.
* **Material and lived experience:** Many people arrive at progressive positions on healthcare, labor rights, environmental regulation, or civil rights through direct personal experience with economic precarity, discrimination, or institutional failure—not merely to fit in with a peer group.
* **Internal fractiousness:** The left is famously prone to intense internal debate and factionalism (e.g., democratic socialists vs. neoliberal technocrats, labor-focused populists vs. socially focused progressives). While factions can have their own tribal dynamics, the sheer volume of ideological argument on the left suggests many participants are deeply invested in the substance of the ideas.

### Summary
If the claim is that *most* progressives didn't sit down and derive their views on tax policy, zoning, climate change, and social norms from scratch—**that is almost certainly true**, just as it is true of most conservatives, libertarians, and moderates. Humans are social, coalition-building creatures who rely heavily on cultural heuristics and group identity. 

However, those group identities usually cluster around real differences in temperament, moral priorities, and material interests—meaning people's views are rarely *just* tribalism, nor are they purely the product of detached reason.

### 1. The General Rule: Identity Mostly Precedes Ideology
Across the board, scholars like Donald Kinder, Nathan Kalmoe (*Neither Liberal nor Conservative*), and Lilliana Mason (*Uncivil Agreement*) have shown that the vast majority of voters do not hold tightly reasoned, philosophically consistent worldviews. Instead, people tend to:
* Adopt the political identities of their family, peer groups, or cultural milieu.
* Take cues from trusted elites or media figures on what to believe about specific policies.
* Use **motivated reasoning**—employing their intellect to defend the conclusions their group already holds, rather than to arrive at those conclusions neutrally.

In that sense, many self-identified libertarians—particularly those who are culturally conservative or anti-establishment—adopt the label as a tribal badge (e.g., "Don't Tread on Me" aesthetics, distrust of mainstream institutions, or a way to distance themselves from the Republican brand while remaining on the right) without necessarily grappling with the philosophy of John Locke, Robert Nozick, or Friedrich Hayek.

### 2. Where Libertarians Are Actually Different
Moral psychologists—most notably Jonathan Haidt and his colleagues—have studied self-identified libertarians specifically (e.g., the 2012 paper *"Understanding Libertarian Morality: The Psychological Dispositions of Self-Identified Libertarians"* by Iyer, Koleva, Graham, Ditto, and Haidt). Their findings challenge the idea that libertarians are *uniquely* or *purely* tribal:

* **Higher cognitive reflection and "systemizing":** On average, self-identified libertarians score higher than both liberals and conservatives on tests of **Need for Cognition** (enjoying abstract, effortful thinking), **Cognitive Reflection** (overriding gut intuition with analytical thought), and **systemizing** (the drive to analyze and construct rule-based systems, as opposed to empathizing).
* **Explicit reasoning from axioms:** Because libertarianism is not one of the two dominant cultural tribes in countries like the US, people rarely inherit it passively from mainstream culture. Many libertarians genuinely do "reason their way" into the ideology—often starting from a single axiom (like the Non-Aggression Principle or maximizing individual liberty) and following it deductively to conclusions, even when those conclusions are socially unpopular.
* **Lower group loyalty:** On Moral Foundations questionnaires, libertarians score unusually low on the "loyalty/ingroup" and "authority" foundations compared to conservatives, and lower on "care/harm" than liberals. Psychologically, they report weaker attachment to group identities and lower social connectedness.

### 3. The Catch: Deductive Reasoning vs. Psychological Disposition
Even when libertarians *do* reason their way to their views, psychologists point out two caveats:
1. **Intuition still chooses the starting premise:** A person might use rigorous logic to deduce policy positions from the premise that *individual liberty is the sole supreme moral value*. However, choosing that starting premise over others (such as minimizing suffering or preserving social order) is usually driven by underlying personality traits—such as high psychological **reactance** (an instinctive aversion to being told what to do) and lower emotional empathy—rather than pure logic.
2. **Tribalism still emerges in practice:** Once people adopt libertarianism, they often form tight-knit communities (online forums, think tanks, factions within parties) that develop their own purity tests, shibboleths, in-group/out-group dynamics, and blind spots—behaving much like any other political tribe.

If the claim is that **libertarians, like everyone else, are heavily influenced by personality traits, social environments, and tribal signaling**, that is well-supported—especially among casual or cultural libertarians. 

If the claim is that **libertarians are *less* likely than liberals or conservatives to have reasoned their way to their views**, the empirical evidence points the other way: libertarianism disproportionately attracts analytical, rule-oriented thinkers who explicitly construct their politics from abstract principles, even if their attraction to those principles is rooted in their temperament.
