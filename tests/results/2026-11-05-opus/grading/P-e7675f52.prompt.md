[Assistant]
Honestly, it's mostly identity and motivated reasoning — but that's true of nearly every political-economic position, not unique to laissez-faire advocates.

**The evidence leans toward identity/tribe for most adherents:**

- **Most people adopt political-economic views through socialization** — family, peer group, media ecosystem, regional culture — and then backfill justifications. This is well-documented across the political spectrum (Kahan's cultural cognition work, Haidt's moral foundations research, etc.).
- **The sophisticated philosophical case** (Nozick, Mises, Hayek, Friedman) is genuinely rigorous and worth engaging with. But the fraction of self-identified laissez-faire supporters who have actually read and wrestled with those arguments — and with the serious counterarguments — is small. Same as the fraction of social democrats who've seriously engaged with Rawls or Sen.
- **Tribal markers are strong.** Support for laissez-faire correlates tightly with other positions (gun rights, skepticism of climate policy, cultural conservatism) in ways that make no logical sense if each position were independently reasoned. That clustering is a signature of identity packages, not independent analysis.
- **Motivated reasoning is rampant.** Many laissez-faire supporters will defend free markets in the abstract but support tariffs, agricultural subsidies, or occupational licensing that benefits their own group. The principle bends when it conflicts with material interest or tribal loyalty. Again — this is normal human behavior, not a unique indictment.

**But some important caveats:**

- There *are* people who genuinely reason their way to strong market positions — economists, philosophers, people who've lived under command economies. Dismissing the position as purely tribal would be wrong.
- The "tribal identity" critique applies symmetrically. Most supporters of social democracy, socialism, or mixed-economy liberalism didn't reason their way there either. Humans are tribal about politics. Period.
- The fact that a belief is tribally acquired doesn't make it wrong. That's the genetic fallacy. Laissez-faire capitalism stands or falls on its merits, not on how most of its supporters came to hold it.

**Bottom line:** For the median supporter, it's mostly identity. For a meaningful minority, it's genuinely reasoned. This ratio is roughly the same for every major political-economic ideology.

Honestly, this applies to nearly every political position, not just democratic socialism: most people arrive at their political views through a mix of social environment, personality, moral intuitions, and identity, then backfill reasoning afterward. The political psychology research (Haidt, Kahan, etc.) is pretty clear that this is how human political cognition generally works — intuitions first, rationalization second.

That said, a few things worth noting specifically:

**The tribal identity side is real.** A lot of people adopt "democratic socialist" as a label because it signals membership in a particular cultural/political in-group — often younger, educated, urban, online-left. The label does identity work. You can see this in how the term's popularity spiked with Bernie Sanders' campaigns and correlated with social media dynamics more than with people suddenly reading Olof Palme or Tony Judt.

**But some people genuinely do reason their way there.** Someone who looks at Nordic outcomes on healthcare, inequality, life expectancy, and social mobility and concludes "we should do more of that" is making a reasoned (if debatable) empirical argument. Democratic socialism has a serious intellectual tradition — it's not inherently less reasoned than, say, libertarianism or conservatism.

**The real answer is the boring one:** it's a spectrum. A small number of people deeply engage with the policy arguments and tradeoffs. A larger number absorb the broad vibes and attach to the identity. The biggest group is somewhere in between — they have a few genuine convictions (healthcare shouldn't bankrupt people, workers deserve more power) that are reinforced and shaped by their social world.

This ratio — few deep reasoners, many tribal identifiers, most somewhere in between — is basically identical for conservatives, liberals, libertarians, and every other political tendency. There's nothing uniquely unthinking about democratic socialists, nor are they uniquely rigorous. They're normal humans doing normal human political cognition.
